Christian apologetics · Miracles and divine action

Can Miracles Really Happen?

A biblical and historical examination of whether God can act in the world, what miracles reveal, and why the resurrection of Jesus stands at the centre of the Christian faith.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

Most people have heard a story about a miracle. Someone says that a relative was healed after prayer. Another person describes an unexpected escape from danger. A video circulates online claiming to show something that cannot be explained. Sometimes these stories are told with complete sincerity; sometimes they are exaggerated, misunderstood, or deliberately fabricated. It is reasonable to ask what we should make of them.

But there is a more fundamental question beneath all these reports: can miracles happen at all? For some people, the answer is already settled. Nature operates according to regular laws, and therefore anything described as a miracle must be mistaken. Others believe that God can intervene but think that miracles belong only to the distant past. Still others accept almost every extraordinary testimony without asking whether it is true.

Christians should avoid both easy scepticism and careless credulity. The Bible does not teach that every unusual event is a miracle, nor does it ask us to believe every person who claims to have witnessed one. It does, however, present the living God as Creator and Lord over all things, and it records particular acts by which he made his power and purposes known.

The question deserves more than a quick answer. We need to understand what a miracle is, whether it conflicts with the regular order of creation, what Scripture says about God's works, and how we should evaluate claims made in our own time. Above all, we need to see why the resurrection of Jesus is not a minor detail in this discussion but the event on which the Christian message stands.

A distinction worth keeping: It is one thing to ask whether God can perform a miracle, another to ask whether a particular miracle report is credible, and still another to ask what that event would prove. These questions are related, but they are not interchangeable.

1. What do we mean by a miracle?

In ordinary conversation, people use the word miracle in several different ways. Someone may call a fortunate coincidence a miracle, or describe a difficult recovery as miraculous because it seemed unlikely. Such language can express genuine gratitude, but it is not always using the word in its more precise theological sense.

A miracle, in the biblical sense relevant to this discussion, is a special act of God in which he brings about an event beyond the ordinary course or powers of creation, making his presence, authority, or purpose known. It is not merely something unusual. It is an act of divine power.

The Bible uses different words and descriptions for God's mighty works. They may be called signs, wonders, powers, or mighty works. Each term draws attention to something important. A wonder produces astonishment; a work of power displays divine ability; a sign points beyond itself to the identity or purpose of the one who performs it.

This last point is especially important. Biblical miracles are not spectacles performed simply to entertain an audience. They belong to God's unfolding revelation. The parting of the Red Sea was not an impressive display detached from the story of redemption. It was God's deliverance of his people from slavery. The healings of Jesus were not party tricks. They testified to the arrival of God's kingdom and to the identity of the Messiah.

Not every providential kindness needs to be classified as a miracle in this narrower sense. God may answer prayer through ordinary means, guide events through circumstances, or provide help through the actions of other people. Those things are no less gifts from him. The distinction is not between events in which God is present and events in which he is absent; all creation depends on his sustaining care. The distinction is between his ordinary providence and his extraordinary works.

2. Are miracles even possible?

The claim that miracles are impossible often rests on an assumption about the world: nature is a closed system, and nothing beyond nature can act within it. If that assumption is true, miracles are excluded before any historical evidence is considered. But the assumption itself is a philosophical claim, not a result established simply by observing that nature behaves regularly.

We rightly expect fire to burn, water to flow downhill, and living bodies to require oxygen. These expectations are not foolish; they are based on repeated observation. Yet the regularity of nature does not prove that nature is all that exists. It tells us how created things ordinarily behave. It does not establish that the Creator is unable to act within his creation.

Consider the difference between a law and the thing whose behaviour it describes. A law of nature is not a physical barrier standing over God, preventing him from acting. It is a description of the regular patterns we observe in the created order. If God is the source and sustainer of that order, it would be strange to treat its regularities as though they were a power greater than he is.

This does not mean that we should call every unexplained event supernatural. Lack of an explanation is not proof of a miracle. It means only that the possibility of divine action cannot be dismissed by appealing to the regularity of nature. The question of whether a particular miracle happened must be examined separately.

The Bible begins with a claim that makes the possibility of miracles entirely coherent: “In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth” (Genesis 1:1). The God who brought the world into existence is not dependent on the processes of that world. If he created all things, he is not powerless to act within them.

3. Do miracles contradict the laws of nature?

People sometimes speak as though a miracle would mean that the universe suddenly became irrational or that the laws of nature stopped making sense. But this misunderstands what Christians are claiming. A miracle is not an assertion that the world has no order. It is a claim that the God who ordinarily governs the world has acted in a particular way for a particular purpose.

We should be careful with the language of “breaking the laws of nature.” It can be useful as a shorthand, but it may suggest that natural laws are independent authorities that God must obey. A better way to put the matter is that a miracle is an extraordinary divine act that is not produced by the ordinary powers and processes of nature.

Imagine a person who knows how a machine normally operates. The machine's regular behaviour can be described accurately, but that description does not prove that its maker cannot interact with it. The analogy is limited, because God is not a human engineer and creation is not a device outside his control. Still, it illustrates why knowing the ordinary operation of a system does not establish that its maker cannot act within it.

Scientific investigation depends on the expectation that nature is orderly. Christians have every reason to take that order seriously. If an event is reported, we should investigate possible natural explanations, check the evidence, and avoid rushing to supernatural conclusions. But science studies the created order through observation and experiment. It cannot establish, merely by describing ordinary patterns, that the Creator does not exist or cannot act.

A miracle, if it occurs, does not make scientific inquiry pointless. It is precisely because miracles are extraordinary that they should not be confused with ordinary events or invoked to fill every gap in our knowledge.

4. The Creator is not trapped inside creation

The biblical doctrine of creation gives the Christian answer its foundation. God did not discover a universe that already existed, nor did he shape matter that was independent of him. He created all things by his word, and everything continues to depend on him for its existence (Genesis 1:1; John 1:1–3; Colossians 1:16–17; Hebrews 1:3).

This means that God's relationship to the world is not like the relationship between two objects within the same universe. He is not one force competing with gravity, electricity, or other physical forces. He is the sovereign Creator who gives created things their existence and sustains them. His ordinary providence is already at work whenever the world continues in its ordered course.

Psalm 104 celebrates God's continuing care for creation. The psalmist describes the creatures receiving food, the earth being watered, and life depending on God's provision. Acts 17:24–28 likewise teaches that God gives life and breath to all people and that in him we live and move and have our being. Creation is not independent and then occasionally visited by God. It is always dependent upon him.

Miracles are therefore not the only evidence of God's activity. The regularity of the world is itself an expression of his sustaining providence. A sunrise is not a lesser testimony to God's power because it occurs every day. What makes a miracle distinctive is not that God is suddenly present, but that he acts in an extraordinary way that serves a particular purpose in his redemptive work.

5. Miracles in the Old Testament

The Old Testament contains accounts of extraordinary acts of God, but they are not scattered randomly through Israel's history. Many are connected to major moments in which God reveals himself, judges evil, delivers his people, or confirms his covenant purposes.

The exodus from Egypt

The plagues against Egypt and the crossing of the Red Sea stand among the most significant displays of divine power in Scripture (Exodus 7–14). God was not merely showing that he could do extraordinary things. He was judging the oppression of his people, exposing the impotence of Egypt's gods, and bringing Israel out of slavery in fulfilment of his promise to Abraham.

The crossing of the sea became a defining act in Israel's memory. Again and again, the prophets and psalmists looked back to it as evidence of God's faithfulness and saving power. The miracle served the larger purpose of redemption.

Elijah and Elisha

In the ministries of Elijah and Elisha, miracles appear in a period of deep spiritual conflict. Elijah's confrontation with the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel demonstrated that the Lord, not Baal, is God (1 Kings 18). Elisha's ministry included healings, provision, and acts of deliverance (2 Kings 4–6). These signs were connected to the prophetic word and the Lord's authority over life, death, and the needs of his people.

Miracles were never a substitute for faithfulness

The Old Testament also warns us against assuming that witnessing a miracle automatically produces lasting faith. Israel saw the mighty works of God and yet repeatedly rebelled against him. The generation that came out of Egypt experienced extraordinary deliverance but still hardened its heart in the wilderness (Psalm 95:7–11; Hebrews 3:7–19).

Miracles can confirm God's word and expose unbelief, but they do not turn a rebellious heart into a faithful one by sheer spectacle. Human beings need more than impressive experiences. We need the mercy of God and the renewing work of his Spirit.

6. The miracles of Jesus

The miracles of Jesus occupy a central place in the Gospel accounts. He healed the sick, gave sight to the blind, cleansed lepers, cast out demons, calmed storms, multiplied food, and raised the dead. These events were not presented as isolated wonders. They revealed who Jesus is and what his coming means.

When John the Baptist sent messengers to ask whether Jesus was the Coming One, Jesus pointed to the works taking place: the blind received sight, the lame walked, lepers were cleansed, the deaf heard, the dead were raised, and the poor had good news preached to them (Matthew 11:2–6; Luke 7:18–23). His answer drew on the promises of the prophets, especially Isaiah's descriptions of the salvation God would bring.

Jesus' miracles showed his authority over the effects of the fall. Disease, demonic oppression, hunger, storms, and death are not greater than he is. His works offered signs of the kingdom of God breaking into history. They were foretastes of the restoration that will be complete when he returns.

They also revealed his divine authority. When Jesus forgave the sins of the paralysed man before healing him, the scribes understood the implication: only God can forgive sins (Mark 2:1–12). Jesus then healed the man so that they would know the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins. The miracle was connected to a claim about his identity.

Yet Jesus did not perform signs on demand to satisfy curiosity or submit to the tests of his opponents (Matthew 12:38–40; 16:1–4). He refused to turn his mission into a spectacle. The miracles served his Father's purpose and testified to the truth of his person and work.

It is also worth noticing that the Gospels do not portray every person in the region as healed during Jesus' earthly ministry. His compassion was real, but his miracles were not a promise that every believer would experience immediate physical healing in this present age. The final defeat of sickness and death awaits the resurrection and the renewal of all things.

7. The signs performed through the apostles

After Jesus' resurrection and ascension, the apostles were witnesses appointed to proclaim him. The book of Acts records signs and wonders performed through them, including healings and other extraordinary works (Acts 3:1–10; 5:12–16; 9:32–42; 14:8–10; 19:11–12).

These signs did not draw attention to the apostles as independent wonder-workers. Peter made this clear when a man lame from birth was healed at the temple. He asked why the crowd stared at him and John as though their own power or godliness had made the man walk. He directed the attention to Jesus Christ, whom God had glorified (Acts 3:12–16).

The New Testament connects signs and wonders with the confirmation of the apostolic witness. Hebrews 2:3–4 speaks of the great salvation first announced by the Lord, attested by those who heard him, and confirmed by God through signs, wonders, various miracles, and gifts of the Holy Spirit. Second Corinthians 12:12 refers to the signs of a true apostle.

This matters when considering the place of miraculous gifts in the church today. The apostles occupied a foundational role in the history of redemption (Ephesians 2:19–20). Their eyewitness testimony to the risen Christ and their authoritative teaching are not a role that can simply be repeated by later Christian leaders. The church remains built on the foundation laid through the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone.

Christians differ over how particular miraculous gifts relate to the church's life after the apostolic age. That debate requires careful attention to the relevant passages, and it should not be settled by assuming that every miracle in Acts must be repeated in precisely the same way in every generation. Whatever position one takes on the continuation of particular gifts, the authority of the apostolic gospel remains foundational and unrepeatable.

8. What were miracles meant to accomplish?

It is easy to become so interested in the extraordinary character of miracles that we overlook their purpose. In Scripture, miracles are never simply demonstrations of raw power. They have meaning within God's revelation and redemptive plan.

They reveal God's identity and power

The plagues in Egypt revealed that the Lord is God and that the gods of Egypt were powerless before him (Exodus 7:5; 12:12). Elijah's confrontation on Carmel had the same central concern: the people needed to know that the Lord, not Baal, is God (1 Kings 18:36–39).

They authenticate God's messengers and word

Signs could confirm that a messenger had been sent by God, although Scripture never treats signs as an excuse to accept teaching that contradicts God's revealed truth. Deuteronomy 13:1–5 warns Israel that even a sign that comes to pass must not lead them after other gods. The truth of God's word remains the standard by which claims are judged.

They display the compassion of God

Jesus' healings were acts of compassion as well as signs of authority. He was moved by the suffering of people who came to him (Matthew 14:14; 20:29–34). The miracles show that God's kingdom is not indifferent to human misery. They point toward the day when suffering and death will be removed.

They point beyond themselves to Christ and his kingdom

A sign is meant to direct attention somewhere. The miracles of Jesus call us to consider his identity, his authority, and the salvation he brings. They do not invite us to become preoccupied with the pursuit of extraordinary experiences while neglecting the Saviour to whom those signs point.

9. David Hume and the argument against miracles

One of the best-known philosophical objections to miracles comes from the Scottish philosopher David Hume (1711–1776). In his discussion “Of Miracles,” Hume argued that a wise person should proportion belief to the evidence. Since our experience consistently shows that the laws of nature operate in regular ways, he reasoned, testimony for a miracle must be weighed against the strong experience that appears to count against it.

Hume famously defined a miracle as a violation of a law of nature and argued that no testimony could establish one unless the falsehood of that testimony would be even more extraordinary than the event being reported. He also questioned the reliability of miracle witnesses, pointing to human credulity, religious enthusiasm, and competing miracle claims.

There is something valuable in the demand that extraordinary claims be examined carefully. Christians should agree that testimony must be assessed, that people can be mistaken or dishonest, and that religious enthusiasm can lead to exaggeration. A claim does not become true simply because someone tells it with conviction.

But Hume's argument has been challenged at a deeper level. If the probability of a miracle is treated as effectively zero before the evidence is considered, then no amount of testimony could ever change the conclusion. That would not be an open investigation of a particular claim; it would be a rule that excludes the possibility in advance. The central question is whether our experience of the ordinary course of nature gives us grounds to rule out the possibility of divine action altogether.

There is also a difference between saying that nature ordinarily behaves in a regular way and saying that no event beyond its ordinary powers can occur. The first statement is supported by observation. The second requires an additional philosophical assumption about the nature of reality and whether God exists. If there is a Creator who can act, the regularity of nature alone cannot establish that he never does so.

Hume's challenge should not be dismissed with a slogan. It reminds us to think carefully about evidence and testimony. But it does not settle the matter simply by defining the world as a closed system. The possibility of miracles must be considered alongside the larger question of whether the biblical God exists and whether the historical evidence for particular events is credible.

10. Can miracle claims be investigated?

Yes, although the kind of evidence available will depend on the claim. A report of a recent healing may involve medical records, diagnoses, independent examination, witnesses, and the possibility of natural recovery or misdiagnosis. An event in the ancient past must be investigated through historical sources, the context in which they were written, the relationship between witnesses, and the explanations that best account for the evidence.

Historical investigation cannot repeat the past in a laboratory. That is true of all historical events, not only miracles. Historians examine documents, testimony, material evidence, chronology, and the circumstances in which reports arose. They ask whether sources are early, whether they are independent, whether the witnesses had access to the events, and whether there are good reasons to suspect invention or distortion.

It is important to distinguish between establishing that people reported an event and establishing that the event occurred. It is also important to distinguish between establishing that an extraordinary event occurred and identifying its cause. A medical case may be genuinely unusual without proving that a particular religious explanation is correct. A report of a remarkable experience may be sincere without being accurate.

Christians should therefore avoid claiming that every unexplained medical recovery proves a miracle, or that a dramatic testimony settles a theological dispute. We should welcome careful examination. Truth does not need exaggeration to defend it.

At the same time, historical reasoning should not exclude a supernatural explanation simply because it is supernatural. That would make a prior philosophical commitment do the work that historical investigation is supposed to do. The evidence should be examined honestly, while the broader question of God's existence and action is considered openly.

11. The resurrection: Christianity's central miracle

All the discussion of miracles eventually brings us to the resurrection of Jesus Christ. Christianity does not stand or fall on whether every modern report of a healing is genuine. It does stand or fall on whether Jesus truly rose from the dead.

“These are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name.”John 20:31

The apostles did not present the resurrection as a symbol of hope or as a way of saying that Jesus' influence continued after his death. They proclaimed that the man who had been crucified and buried was bodily raised by God. The tomb was empty, and the risen Christ appeared to his followers. The resurrection was an act of God in history.

Paul makes the importance of this claim unmistakable in 1 Corinthians 15: if Christ has not been raised, Christian preaching is empty, faith is futile, and believers remain in their sins. The apostles were not offering a spiritual message that could survive regardless of what happened to Jesus' body. They were announcing something they said God had actually done.

Why the resurrection is not an ordinary recovery

Jesus' resurrection was not a return to ordinary mortal life like the restorations to life recorded during his earthly ministry. Those people eventually died again. Christ was raised in glory, never to die again (Romans 6:9). His resurrection is the beginning and guarantee of the final resurrection of his people.

The testimony of the earliest Christians

In 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, Paul passes on a summary of the gospel he had received: Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to witnesses. He names Peter, the Twelve, more than five hundred people, James, and finally himself. This is an early Christian testimony rooted in the proclamation of the first generation of believers.

The Gospels also present accounts of the empty tomb and appearances of the risen Jesus (Matthew 28; Mark 16; Luke 24; John 20–21). The accounts differ in details and emphasis, as we would expect from distinct testimonies, but they agree on the central claim that Jesus truly rose. Differences should be examined carefully rather than ignored or exaggerated into contradictions without investigation.

What best explains the evidence?

Historical arguments for the resurrection commonly consider Jesus' crucifixion, the early proclamation that he had risen, the testimony concerning appearances, the transformation of his disciples, and the conversion of Paul. Each point must be assessed with care. None should be inflated beyond what the sources establish. Together, however, they form a serious historical case that deserves more than the assumption that resurrection is impossible.

The Christian explanation is not that dead people ordinarily return to life. They do not. It is that God raised Jesus. The extraordinary nature of the event is precisely why it matters. If God exists and has acted in history, the fact that resurrection is not part of ordinary human experience does not make it impossible.

The resurrection also has a meaning that goes beyond proving that miracles can happen. It vindicates Jesus, confirms the truth of his teaching, demonstrates his victory over sin and death, and assures believers that he will return to judge the world and raise his people. The Christian hope rests not on a general belief that wonderful things sometimes happen, but on the risen Lord Jesus Christ.

12. What about miracles today?

Christians do not all answer this question in the same way. Some believe that the miraculous gifts described in the New Testament continue in the church today. Others believe that certain sign gifts were particularly connected to the foundational apostolic period and ceased with that period. This is a theological question that should be addressed from Scripture, not settled by personal preference or by the intensity of someone's experience.

It is helpful to separate several questions that are often mixed together. Does God still heal? Certainly he can, and Christians should pray for the sick (James 5:13–18). Does God still answer prayer in remarkable ways? Yes. Is God able to perform an extraordinary act whenever he pleases? His power has not diminished. Are the sign gifts given in the apostolic era continuing in the same form and with the same function today? That requires a more specific examination of passages such as Acts 2, 1 Corinthians 12–14, Ephesians 2:19–20, and Hebrews 2:3–4.

A person who holds that certain gifts have ceased need not believe that God has become inactive or that prayer is useless. Cessationism, properly understood, is not a denial of God's power. It is a conclusion about the purpose and duration of particular gifts in the history of redemption. A person who holds a continuationist position should likewise be willing to test claims carefully by Scripture and not assume that every reported experience is genuine.

We should also resist using the word miracle as a way to avoid careful thought. A remarkable answer to prayer may be a wonderful expression of providence without being the same kind of public sign described in the apostolic accounts. God deserves thanks for both his ordinary and extraordinary works.

Whatever position Christians take on the continuation of particular gifts, we must not make contemporary experiences the standard by which Scripture is judged. The apostolic witness is the foundation; the church is called to remain faithful to the teaching once delivered.

13. How should Christians evaluate miracle claims?

We should neither believe everything nor dismiss everything. Scripture itself calls God's people to discernment. First John 4:1 tells believers not to believe every spirit but to test the spirits. Deuteronomy 13 warns that even a sign or wonder must not lead God's people away from the Lord. The truth of a message matters more than the excitement surrounding a claim.

Ask what is actually being claimed

Is the person describing an unusual coincidence, an unexpected recovery, a vision, a healing, or a claim that someone was raised from the dead? Vague language can make an ordinary event sound more extraordinary than it is. Ask for a clear description before deciding what the claim means.

Examine the evidence

Where possible, look for independent witnesses, reliable records, and details that can be checked. In medical cases, a previous diagnosis and subsequent medical assessment matter. Be cautious when the only evidence is a carefully edited video, an anonymous testimony, or a report repeated by people who have not examined it themselves.

Consider the teaching associated with the claim

A supposed miracle does not authenticate a false gospel. Scripture warns that signs can be associated with deception (Matthew 24:24; 2 Thessalonians 2:9–12). A person who uses a claimed miracle to demand unquestioning loyalty, enrich himself, or promote teaching contrary to Scripture should not be trusted merely because a crowd is impressed.

Look for honesty and humility

Faithful Christian ministry does not need manipulation, staged testimonies, pressure to give money, or promises that every sick person will be healed if only they have enough faith. Those who speak about God's works should be truthful about what they know and what they do not know. When a claim cannot be verified, it is better to say so than to present it as certain.

Do not confuse discernment with cynicism

Some people reject every testimony before hearing it. That is not careful discernment either. We should listen, investigate, and weigh evidence. But our confidence must rest in God and his revealed word, not in the most dramatic story we have heard.

14. Miracles, faith, and the authority of Scripture

There is a temptation to think that if we could only see a miracle, all doubt would disappear. Yet Scripture gives us reasons to question that assumption. Israel witnessed the plagues and the crossing of the sea, but many still rebelled. People saw Jesus' signs and nevertheless rejected him. In the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, Jesus teaches that those who refuse to hear Moses and the Prophets will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead (Luke 16:19–31).

This does not mean that miracles are unimportant or that evidence has no place in Christian faith. John tells us that Jesus did many signs which were not recorded in his Gospel, but that the signs he did record were written so that readers may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life in his name (John 20:30–31). The signs serve faith by directing us to Christ.

But saving faith is not produced by spectacle alone. The Holy Spirit works through the Word of God to bring sinners to Christ (Romans 10:14–17; 1 Peter 1:23–25). Faith receives the testimony God has given concerning his Son. It is not a demand that God continually provide new signs to satisfy our conditions.

The authority of Scripture is therefore essential. We do not place a modern testimony alongside the Bible as though both have equal authority. We test every teaching and experience by the Word God has given. Scripture equips the church for faithful life and ministry, and it must not be displaced by claims of new revelation or extraordinary spiritual authority (2 Timothy 3:16–17).

Miracles should lead us to worship the God who acts, to trust the Christ to whom the signs point, and to receive the apostolic gospel. If an alleged miracle draws people away from the truth of Scripture, it has not served the purpose of biblical revelation.

15. What difference should this make?

Can miracles really happen? If the God revealed in Scripture exists, then yes. The Creator is not limited by the ordinary processes of the creation he made. The regularity of nature is real and should be respected, but it does not prove that God cannot act in extraordinary ways. The Bible presents miracles as particular works of divine power woven into the history of redemption.

That answer does not give us permission to believe every extraordinary story. We should be careful with claims, honest about uncertainty, and unwilling to confuse enthusiasm with evidence. Scripture itself teaches discernment. It also teaches us that a sign must never become more important than the truth of the God whose glory it is meant to display.

And when we ask which miracle matters most, the answer is clear. The resurrection of Jesus Christ stands at the centre of the Christian faith. If he has not been raised, our preaching is empty and our faith is futile. But Christ has been raised, Paul declares, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep (1 Corinthians 15:17–20).

The resurrection tells us that sin and death will not have the final word. The risen Christ is Lord. His resurrection confirms the truth of the gospel, assures his people of their justification, and guarantees the coming renewal of all things. Christianity is not built on the pursuit of extraordinary experiences. It is built on the finished work and living reign of Jesus Christ.

So we should not be embarrassed by the biblical claim that God performs miracles. Nor should we become obsessed with chasing them. We should listen to the Scriptures, examine claims honestly, pray with confidence in God's wisdom, and place our hope in the risen Saviour. The greatest question is not whether we have witnessed something extraordinary. It is whether we have come to know and trust the Lord to whom the signs point.

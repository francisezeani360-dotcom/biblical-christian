Christian apologetics · The existence of God

Does God Exist?

A careful look at why Christians believe the universe points beyond itself, how we should weigh the arguments, and what to do with the questions that remain.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

There are questions a person can put off for a while, but this is not an easy one to leave alone. If God exists, then reality is not ultimately impersonal. We are not here by accident in the deepest sense, human life has a purpose beyond what we give it, and our choices matter before the One who made us. If God does not exist, then we need to face that conclusion honestly too.

People arrive at this question from very different places. Some have grown up in church and are beginning to wonder whether what they were taught is actually true. Others have never believed. Some are not opposed to God but cannot see why they should accept a claim that seems to go beyond what science can establish. And some have been pushed away from Christianity by suffering, hypocrisy, or unanswered prayer. Those are not all the same concern, and they should not be answered as though they were.

This article is not going to pretend that one clever argument settles every question. It will consider several lines of reasoning that, taken together, make belief in God intellectually serious. It will also take objections seriously, including the problem of evil and the apparent hiddenness of God. The aim is not to force a conclusion by exaggerating the evidence. It is to ask what best explains the world we actually inhabit.

A word about the arguments: These are not all mathematical proofs, and they do not all work in exactly the same way. Some ask what explains the existence of the universe; others point to features of the world that fit more naturally within a theistic view. Their strength is cumulative. Each should be examined on its own terms, and none should be made to say more than it can.

1. What are we asking when we ask whether God exists?

Before considering arguments, it helps to be clear about the claim. The Christian is not simply saying that there is a powerful being somewhere in the universe, perhaps living in a place we have not yet explored. God is not one more object among other objects, competing with stars, particles, or forces for a place in the same physical system.

The God revealed in Scripture is the eternal Creator who depends on nothing, gives existence to everything else, and is not contained by the world he made. He is personal, intelligent, holy, just, and good. He knows and governs his creation without being part of it. If this God exists, his existence is not one more scientific discovery waiting to be made by a telescope or microscope.

That distinction matters. Asking whether God exists is not quite like asking whether a particular planet exists. We are asking why there is a reality at all, why it has the character it does, and whether the deepest explanation is personal or impersonal. A good argument for God's existence should address that question rather than rely on a gap in current scientific knowledge.

2. Why is there a universe at all?

Look around. The things we encounter exist, but they do not have to exist. A tree depends on soil, water, sunlight, and a long chain of causes. A person depends on parents, food, oxygen, and countless conditions beyond their control. Planets, stars, and galaxies are not self-explanatory either. They have particular properties and histories; they could have been different, and they might never have existed.

Philosophers call things of this kind contingent: they exist, but their existence is not necessary. The question is whether the whole collection of contingent things can ultimately explain itself. If every part of a chain depends on something else, has the chain as a whole explained why there is anything there in the first place?

Imagine finding a long line of lamps, each receiving its electricity from the next. Knowing how one lamp receives power from another does not explain where the power comes from. Extending the line does not answer the question. The point is not that the universe is literally a row of lamps, but that an explanation made entirely of dependent things may still leave the existence of the whole unexplained.

The argument from contingency says that dependent reality calls for an explanation beyond itself. If everything that exists were contingent, there would be no ultimate ground for why anything exists. The explanation must finally rest in something that does not receive existence from another—something necessary rather than dependent. Christians identify this necessary, self-existent source as God.

This argument is philosophical, not a claim that scientists have failed to find one more physical cause. It also requires careful thought: some philosophers dispute whether the universe needs an explanation of this kind, or whether reality may simply be a brute fact. But saying “the universe just exists” is not a discovery that answers the question. It is a decision to stop asking it. The Christian view offers a deeper account: the world exists because the eternal God freely created it.

3. Does the universe have a beginning?

Another line of reasoning begins with the history of the universe. Modern cosmology describes an expanding universe whose observable history can be traced back to an extremely hot, dense early state. The standard Big Bang model has become central to contemporary cosmology, though questions remain about the earliest moments and about what, if anything, can meaningfully be said of a “before” that state.

Some philosophers and theologians use this evidence as part of a kalam cosmological argument. In its familiar form, the argument is simple:

  1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
  2. The universe began to exist.
  3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

The argument then asks what a cause of the universe would have to be like. If space, time, matter, and energy belong to the universe, its ultimate cause cannot simply be another physical object located somewhere inside space and time. The cause would have to be beyond the physical universe and powerful enough to bring it into existence. The personal nature of that cause is a further philosophical question, not something established by the syllogism alone.

It is important not to misuse the science here. The Big Bang theory does not, by itself, prove creation from nothing, nor do cosmologists all agree about what the ultimate origin of the universe involves. Some proposals explore quantum cosmology, bouncing models, or other possibilities. These are serious topics of investigation, not reasons to dismiss cosmology as though scientists were hiding an answer.

Even if a model described an earlier physical state, the deeper question would remain: why does that entire physical reality exist? The argument from a beginning is one part of the discussion, not the whole case. The Christian doctrine of creation does not depend on pretending that every detail of contemporary cosmology has already been settled.

4. Why is the universe so finely ordered?

The universe is not just here; it has a structure that allows stars, chemistry, and life. Many features of physics appear to fall within ranges that permit complex structures to form. If certain fundamental constants or initial conditions were significantly different, familiar forms of matter, long-lived stars, or the chemical complexity on which life depends might not be possible.

This is often called the fine-tuning of the universe. The observation is not that scientists have discovered a dial that someone visibly turned. It is that the life-permitting character of the physical universe raises a question about why its basic features are as they are.

Three broad responses are commonly discussed. Some argue that the apparent fine-tuning is best explained by design: an intelligent cause intended a universe capable of supporting life. Others suggest that there may be many universes with different conditions, and that it is unsurprising for observers to find themselves in one compatible with observers. Still others argue that we do not yet understand the deeper physical laws or constraints that might make the constants less arbitrary than they now appear.

These alternatives should not be caricatured. A multiverse proposal is not automatically foolish, and design should not be treated as a scientific shortcut that ends investigation. At the same time, a multiverse does not remove every question: we can still ask why there is a universe-generating system, why it has the laws it does, and whether the possibility of life-permitting worlds is ultimately explained.

Fine-tuning is therefore suggestive rather than a stand-alone demonstration. It gives a thoughtful person reason to consider whether the order and life-permitting structure of the universe are better understood as the product of intelligence than as an unexplained coincidence. It also fits naturally with the biblical picture of a wise Creator whose works display order and purpose (Psalm 19:1; Psalm 104:24).

5. The reality of life, reason, and consciousness

There is something remarkable about the fact that the universe contains living beings who can think about it. Human beings do not merely react to their surroundings. We ask whether our beliefs are true, form arguments, recognise beauty, make promises, feel guilt, and consider what we ought to do. We are conscious of ourselves and of a world that is not simply our own experience.

Science has made extraordinary progress in explaining the biological processes that sustain life and the brain activity associated with thought. Christians have no reason to fear those discoveries. Understanding how memory works, how the brain processes language, or how organisms develop does not automatically answer why there is conscious experience at all, or why our reasoning should be trusted as a guide to truth.

Some philosophers argue that a purely physical account of human beings struggles to explain consciousness, intentional thought, or the normativity of reason. Others believe that these features can ultimately be explained through physical processes. The debate is not settled by saying that science has no answers; nor is it settled by assuming that a future scientific account must explain every feature of the mind.

The Christian view sees human beings as creatures made in the image of God (Genesis 1:26–27). Our ability to reason is not an accident that somehow appeared in a universe indifferent to truth. It is part of what it means to be made by a rational Creator. This does not solve every question in philosophy of mind, but it gives a coherent place to the human capacities we all use when we discuss whether God exists.

6. Is there a foundation for moral truth?

Most people recognise a difference between disliking something and believing it is actually wrong. We may prefer one kind of food to another, but cruelty, exploitation, and the deliberate abuse of the vulnerable seem to be more than matters of taste. We often speak as though some actions are wrong even when a society approves of them and even when the person committing them benefits.

The moral argument asks what makes that kind of moral obligation real. If moral truths are objective—true regardless of what individuals or societies happen to think—what is their ultimate foundation? An atheist can certainly be morally serious, compassionate, courageous, and committed to justice. The question is not whether nonbelievers can behave well. They plainly can. The question is what, on a worldview without God, makes moral duties objectively binding rather than the product of preference, social agreement, or evolutionary history.

Some non-theistic philosophers defend objective moral values as basic truths that do not require God. Others explain morality through human flourishing, rational consistency, or social life. These are serious positions and should be engaged on their own terms. The Christian argument is that moral goodness is not an impersonal rule floating above the universe. Goodness is grounded in God's own holy and faithful character, and our duties arise from the fact that he made us and has authority over us.

This also means that morality is not arbitrary. God does not call cruelty good on a whim. His commands express who he is. We can recognise real moral truths because we are made in his image, even though sin distorts our judgement and our behaviour (Romans 2:14–16; James 1:17). The moral argument does not mean that every moral question is easy, but it helps explain why justice and goodness seem to matter beyond personal preference.

7. The objections deserve an answer

At this point, someone may reasonably say that the arguments have not proved enough. Perhaps the universe has no further explanation. Perhaps the apparent fine-tuning will receive a natural explanation. Perhaps consciousness and morality can be understood without God. And even if some kind of Creator exists, why think that this Creator is good, personal, or concerned with human beings?

Those are fair questions. It would be a mistake to present the arguments as though every philosopher agrees with them or as though no intelligent objection has ever been raised. The arguments are debated, and their force depends on wider questions about explanation, causation, probability, mind, and morality.

But the existence of disagreement does not make the question meaningless. Almost every major worldview has difficult questions it must answer. Atheism must account for why there is a universe, why it has its particular order, and how reason, consciousness, and moral obligation fit into an ultimately impersonal reality. Theism must explain why a good and powerful God created a world in which suffering and evil are so real. Neither side gets to avoid the hard questions.

The responsible approach is to compare whole explanations, not to demand that one side answer every mystery while allowing the other to leave its own starting assumptions unexplained. And there is one objection that deserves more than a passing paragraph.

8. If God is good, why is there so much evil?

For many people, this is not an abstract puzzle. It is personal. They have watched someone suffer, experienced violence, lost a loved one, or prayed for help that did not come in the way they hoped. To answer such pain with a neat philosophical formula can be cruel. The suffering of real people should never be treated as a convenient debating point.

The problem of evil is often put this way: if God is all-powerful, he could prevent evil; if he is perfectly good, he would want to prevent evil; yet evil exists. How can all three claims be true?

One distinction is between the logical problem of evil and the evidential problem. The logical version claims that God's existence is incompatible with any evil at all. But it is possible to see that God might have morally sufficient reasons for permitting some evil, even when those reasons are not fully known to us. For example, a world containing meaningful human choices may also contain the possibility of people choosing wickedly. This does not explain every instance of suffering, but it shows that the bare existence of evil does not create a formal contradiction.

The evidential problem is harder. It asks whether the amount and apparent pointlessness of suffering make God's existence unlikely. The Christian should not wave this away. Scripture itself gives voice to grief, bewilderment, and protest. Job does not receive a tidy explanation for every loss. The Psalms repeatedly ask how long the Lord will allow the wicked to prosper. Jesus weeps at the tomb of Lazarus (John 11:35). The Bible does not teach us to pretend that suffering is less terrible than it is.

Christianity also makes claims about evil that go beyond a general theism. God created the world good, human rebellion brought sin and death into human experience, and evil is not part of God's character. God is not tempted by evil and does not tempt anyone to sin (James 1:13–17). At the same time, Scripture teaches that God is able to govern even the wicked acts of people without becoming morally guilty of those acts. The clearest example is the crucifixion: human beings acted wickedly, yet the death of Christ took place according to God's definite purpose (Acts 2:23; 4:27–28).

That answer must be handled carefully. It does not make evil good, excuse perpetrators, or give us permission to tell a grieving person that we know exactly why their suffering happened. The Bible calls us to mourn with those who mourn, oppose injustice, care for the afflicted, and trust God's final judgement. The Christian hope is not that suffering is secretly harmless. It is that God has entered human suffering in Christ, will judge evil, and will finally make all things new (Romans 8:18–25; Revelation 21:1–5).

9. Why does God seem hidden?

Another serious question is why God does not make his existence unmistakable to everyone. If he wants people to know him, why is belief not as obvious as the presence of the sun? Why do sincere people search and still feel that heaven is silent?

It is not enough to answer that such people simply do not want God. Some do resist the moral claims of their Creator, as Scripture says (Romans 1:18–23). But we should not assume that every doubter is dishonest or that every question comes from rebellion. People can be confused, wounded, poorly taught, or genuinely unsure how to assess competing claims.

The Bible does not portray God as having left himself without witness. Creation declares his glory, human beings bear his image, and God has spoken through his Word. Yet Scripture also teaches that sin affects the whole person, including the mind and desires. Our difficulty is not only a lack of information; it is also a problem of the heart. We need more than evidence in the abstract. We need God to make himself known and to renew us.

That is why Christian faith is not presented as the achievement of clever people who have solved a puzzle. It is a response to God's self-revelation. We should be patient with honest questions, give reasons for the hope we have, and pray that God would open blind eyes. We should not use the doctrine of sin as an excuse to avoid listening carefully.

10. Has science made God unnecessary?

Science studies the natural world through observation, testing, measurement, and the development of explanations that can be examined by others. It has corrected many mistaken ideas about nature and has given us a deeper understanding of the processes through which the world works. Christians should welcome honest scientific investigation. The world belongs to God, and discovering how it works is not a threat to its Creator.

But science and the question of God's existence are not identical kinds of enquiry. A scientific explanation describes how one physical process relates to another. A philosophical or theological explanation may ask why there is a physical order at all, why it is intelligible, or why anything exists. These questions can overlap, but they should not be confused.

For example, explaining how rain forms through evaporation, condensation, and precipitation does not answer why there is a world with stable laws and water in the first place. A complete account of biological development would not, simply by being complete at its own level, settle whether life ultimately depends on a Creator. Explaining the mechanism of something is not necessarily the same as explaining why the whole system exists.

It is equally mistaken for Christians to invoke God whenever science has not yet explained something. That approach makes faith depend on the gaps in our knowledge, and those gaps can close as research advances. The stronger Christian claim is not that God is needed to patch holes in scientific theories. It is that the natural order, including everything science investigates, exists because God created and sustains it.

11. Do these arguments prove the Christian God?

Not by themselves. An argument that the universe depends on a necessary cause does not yet establish the Trinity, the incarnation, the atoning death of Jesus, or the resurrection. An argument from fine-tuning does not tell us the name of the Creator. The moral argument does not, on its own, explain the gospel. We should be honest about what each argument can and cannot show.

These arguments can help establish that belief in a Creator is reasonable and that the world is not best understood as a self-explanatory collection of impersonal facts. They can point towards a necessary, powerful, intelligent, and morally good source of reality. But to know who this God is and what he has done, we need more than reasoning from the created world. We need God to reveal himself.

Christianity makes a definite historical claim: God has made himself known in Jesus Christ. The central question is therefore not simply whether a generic deity exists, but whether Jesus is who the New Testament says he is. His life, teaching, death, and resurrection must be examined. That is why the question of God's existence naturally leads to the historical case for the resurrection, which is considered in our article Did Jesus Really Rise from the Dead?

The Christian does not believe in God because every philosophical argument has been settled. We believe because God has spoken and acted, supremely in his Son. The arguments help us understand why this confession is not irrational; the gospel tells us the identity and saving purpose of the Creator.

12. What does the Bible say?

The Bible does not begin by trying to prove that God exists. Its opening words declare, “In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth” (Genesis 1:1). God is presented as the One from whom everything else receives its existence. He is not a character within the universe's story; he is its Creator and Lord.

Psalm 19 describes the heavens as declaring God's glory. Romans 1:18–23 teaches that God's eternal power and divine nature are displayed through what he has made, leaving humanity without an excuse for treating creation as though it were ultimate. Acts 17:24–28 presents God as the Maker of the world who gives life and breath to all, and in whom we live and move and have our being.

Scripture also insists that knowing facts about God is not the same as being reconciled to him. We have not honoured him as we ought. We have exchanged his glory for created things and have sinned against our Maker (Romans 1:21–25; 3:9–23). The problem is therefore not merely intellectual. We need forgiveness and new life.

The good news is that God has not left sinners to find their own way back. He sent his Son, Jesus Christ, who lived in perfect obedience, died for the sins of his people, and rose bodily from the dead. Through him, sinners are called to repent and believe, and are reconciled to God (Romans 5:6–11; 1 Corinthians 15:1–4; 2 Corinthians 5:17–21). The God whose existence we have been considering is not distant from the world he made. He has acted in history to save.

13. What should we do with the question?

If you are unsure whether God exists, do not feel that you have to pretend to be convinced. Ask the questions honestly. Read the arguments carefully, look at the objections, and avoid sources that rely on mockery or exaggeration. You should be able to disagree with a writer and still feel that your actual question has been heard.

But do not stop at the most abstract version of the question. If there is a Creator, what would it mean to be accountable to him? If the Christian claim is true, what does the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus mean for you? Read one of the Gospels slowly. Pay attention to what Jesus says about God, sin, mercy, judgement, and the kingdom of heaven. Consider whether the resurrection accounts deserve your attention.

For the Christian, the existence of God is not merely an idea to defend in a discussion. It is the foundation of worship, gratitude, obedience, and hope. We live in God's world, receive every breath from him, and are called to know him. The evidence around us gives us reason to look beyond creation to its Maker; Scripture tells us that this Maker has made himself known in Jesus Christ.

We should therefore speak about these things with conviction but without arrogance. No one is saved by winning an argument. We can give reasons, answer objections, and point people to the truth, but only God can change the heart. The invitation is not simply to admit that some kind of higher power may exist. It is to turn to the living God through Jesus Christ.

Church History

The Early Church and the Council of Nicaea

The Council of Nicaea in AD 325 addressed a controversy over the identity of the Son of God. Its history helps us understand why the church carefully confessed what Scripture teaches about Jesus Christ.

The controversy

In the early fourth century, Christians were debating how to speak faithfully about the relationship between the Father and the Son. Arius, a presbyter in Alexandria, taught that the Son was not eternal in the same sense as the Father and was a creature brought into being by the Father's will. This teaching raised a decisive question: can a created being truly reveal God and save us?

The New Testament calls the Son the Word who was with God and was God (John 1:1), says all things were created through him (John 1:3; Colossians 1:16–17), and presents him as worthy of the same honour as the Father (John 5:23). The dispute was not about an abstract technicality. It concerned the identity of Jesus and the truth of the gospel.

The Council of Nicaea

Emperor Constantine convened bishops at Nicaea in AD 325. The council rejected the claim that the Son was a creature and confessed that the Son is begotten, not made, and of the same essence as the Father. The term homoousios (“of the same essence”) was used to guard the biblical confession that the Son is truly God.

The Nicene confession was later expanded and clarified in the form commonly called the Nicene Creed, especially at Constantinople in AD 381. The historical development should be described carefully: the creed familiar to many churches is not simply a verbatim copy of the original 325 text.

Why it matters

If Christ were merely a creature, he could not be the eternal Lord revealed in Scripture, nor could the church worship him as God. The confession of Christ's full deity safeguards the truth that God himself has come to save. The Son became truly human without ceasing to be truly God (John 1:14; Hebrews 2:14–17).

The council does not stand above Scripture. Its importance lies in its attempt to express the church's reading of Scripture against a teaching it judged to be incompatible with the apostolic witness. Christians should receive creeds with gratitude and examine them by the Word of God.

Lessons from this history

First, doctrinal precision can serve love for Christ and his people. Second, controversy should drive the church back to the biblical text. Third, historical accounts should distinguish what is documented from later legend. Nicaea reminds us that the identity of Jesus is central, not peripheral, to Christian faith.

Christian apologetics · The problem of evil

If God Is Good, Why Is There So Much Evil and Suffering?

A biblical and honest examination of the hardest questions suffering raises about God's goodness, his rule over the world, and the hope Christians have in Jesus Christ.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

There are questions people ask in a classroom, and there are questions they ask beside a hospital bed, at a graveside, or after receiving news that changes their lives. “If God is good, why is there so much suffering?” can be either kind. Sometimes it is a philosophical objection. Sometimes it is the cry of someone who has run out of strength. Often it is both.

We should not answer carelessly. A person who has lost a child does not need a clever slogan. Someone who has been abused does not need to be told that everything happens for a reason as though the evil done to them were small. And a person who doubts Christianity deserves more than the claim that suffering is an illusion or that every tragedy can be explained if we think hard enough.

The Bible does not treat suffering as imaginary. It gives language to grief, records terrible injustice, condemns cruelty, and shows faithful people asking God difficult questions. It also teaches that God is good, wise, and sovereign; that evil is real but not ultimate; and that the death and resurrection of Jesus are central to his answer. This study considers the philosophical problem, the entrance of sin, human responsibility, natural suffering, providence, the cross, and the promised renewal of creation.

A necessary distinction: Explaining why evil exists in God's world is not the same as explaining why a particular person has suffered a particular tragedy. Scripture gives important truths about the first question. It does not give us permission to pretend we know God's hidden reason for every event in the second.

1. What exactly is the problem of evil?

The problem begins with three claims that seem difficult to hold together: God is all-powerful, God is perfectly good, and evil and suffering exist. If God can prevent evil and wants what is good, why is the world full of violence, sickness, grief, injustice, and death?

The logical problem claims that any evil is incompatible with an all-powerful and perfectly good God. The evidential problem argues that the quantity, severity, or apparently pointless character of suffering makes God's existence unlikely. A person may accept that God and some suffering could coexist while still asking why there is so much suffering, and why some of it seems to accomplish nothing good.

These are serious questions. It is not enough to point out that people sometimes learn through hardship. Some suffering destroys rather than visibly strengthens. Some victims die without seeing justice in this life. Some pain is inflicted on people who have done nothing to deserve it. Any answer that ignores these realities is inadequate.

Notice, too, that calling something evil assumes a moral standard. When we call an act wicked, we are not merely saying that we dislike it; we are saying it is truly wrong and that the victim matters. That moral protest itself calls for an explanation. This observation does not make suffering less painful or by itself prove Christianity. It reminds us that the problem of evil is not a one-sided argument that leaves every worldview untouched.

The Christian answer is not that evil is harmless or that every tragedy is easy to explain. It is that evil does not contradict God's existence when we understand the biblical account of creation, the fall, providence, redemption, and final judgment. Scripture gives reasons to trust God's character even where his purposes remain beyond our sight.

2. What do Christians mean when they say God is good?

God's goodness belongs to his very nature. He is not measured by a moral standard above himself, as though someone else had written the rules and God were trying to obey them. Nor does goodness mean that whatever a powerful being happens to do becomes good merely because he did it. Scripture reveals God's character as holy, righteous, faithful, merciful, and just.

God does not delight in wickedness (Psalm 5:4). He is righteous in all his ways and kind in all his works (Psalm 145:17). He commands his people to love what is good, defend the vulnerable, act justly, and show mercy because these things reflect his character (Micah 6:8; James 1:17). His goodness is not sentimental approval of everything that happens. He hates evil and will judge it.

“The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit.”Psalm 34:18 — reference for study

We should distinguish God's goodness from our immediate comfort. A good God is not one who guarantees that every person will have an easy life, avoid loss, or receive everything they want. The Bible never makes that promise. Jesus himself was innocent and yet suffered profoundly. The apostles endured persecution. The Psalms repeatedly show believers waiting for deliverance while surrounded by trouble.

That does not mean suffering is good in itself. Pain belongs to life in a fallen world; cruelty is evil; death is an enemy. The Christian claim is that God's goodness is not defeated by these things and that he can bring about wise and holy purposes without approving the evil itself.

3. Did God create an evil world?

The Bible begins with God creating the heavens and the earth (Genesis 1:1). At the end of the creation account, God calls everything he has made very good (Genesis 1:31). The world was not created as a realm of moral corruption, hatred, injustice, and death. Evil is not an eternal power standing alongside God, nor is matter itself evil.

Christianity is not dualism. It does not teach that the universe is a battleground between two equal gods, one good and one evil. There is one Creator. Everything that exists depends on him for its being, but moral evil is not a created substance God fashioned and placed in human hearts. Evil is a corruption or disorder of what God made good.

Human beings were created in God's image (Genesis 1:26–27), made for life with him, love and obedience, and responsible care of the world. Our bodies, relationships, work, and created world are gifts, not mistakes. The doctrine of creation gives us reason to value human life and the material world even while acknowledging how deeply both have been damaged by sin.

Why, then, did God not create a world in which sin could never occur? Scripture does not give a complete philosophical account of every alternative God could have chosen. It does tell us that God made humanity responsible, that our rebellion was real, and that his plan of redemption was not an improvised response to an unexpected crisis. We must not pretend to know more than God has revealed. We can say that wickedness was not part of the goodness of his original creation.

4. What happened when humanity sinned?

Genesis 3 describes humanity's rebellion against God's command. The serpent questioned God's word and goodness; Adam and Eve distrusted their Creator and chose disobedience. Their sin was not merely a mistake or lack of information. It was a rejection of God's authority and an attempt to define good and evil independently of him.

The consequences were devastating. Shame and fear entered human experience. Relationships were damaged. The ground was cursed, work became painful, and humanity was driven from the garden. God told Adam that he would return to the dust (Genesis 3:14–24). Sin brought alienation from God and death into human life.

Romans 5:12–21 explains that sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin, and that death spread to all because all sinned. Paul describes the universal human condition: the human race is fallen, and death reigns in a world that has turned from its Creator.

This does not mean every individual tragedy is a direct punishment for a particular personal sin. Jesus rejected that simplistic conclusion when people asked about a man born blind and about those killed when a tower fell (John 9:1–3; Luke 13:1–5). The whole world is affected by sin; that does not mean we can identify a person's hidden guilt from their suffering. A sick child, an assault victim, or a grieving family should never be told that their tragedy proves they committed some special sin.

5. Why do people do such terrible things?

Much suffering is caused directly by human choices. War, exploitation, murder, sexual violence, domestic abuse, corruption, greed, oppression, and betrayal are not mysterious natural processes. They are acts for which human beings bear responsibility. We often know what is right and choose what is wrong because we love power, comfort, or desire more than we love God and our neighbours.

Jesus taught that evil actions come from the heart (Mark 7:20–23). He was not saying that every human being is as wicked as they could possibly become. People who do not follow Christ can show real kindness, courage, generosity, and concern for justice. But sin reaches deeper than outward behaviour. It affects our desires, judgments, motives, and relationships. We need more than education or better social arrangements; we need reconciliation with God and a renewed heart.

Human responsibility is essential. We cannot excuse abuse by saying that the perpetrator was simply part of God's plan. We cannot call injustice good because God is able to overrule it. Scripture condemns oppression and commands the protection of the vulnerable (Isaiah 1:16–17; Proverbs 31:8–9; James 5:1–6). Those who do evil are accountable for what they choose and do.

Wrongdoing also creates systems and consequences that outlast the original act. A corrupt official may deprive thousands of basic services; a violent household may wound children for years; a war may leave grief and poverty across generations. Recognising these effects should deepen our commitment to justice, not make us fatalistic. Christianity does not promise that conversion will immediately remove every social injustice. It teaches that the gospel changes people and creates communities shaped by truth, mercy, and love.

6. What about disease, disasters, and suffering in nature?

Not all suffering can be traced to a deliberate human act. People are injured in earthquakes, children are born with serious illnesses, crops fail, animals suffer, and storms destroy homes. This is sometimes called natural evil, though nature is not morally guilty. The phrase describes suffering arising through the processes and conditions of the natural world.

Romans 8:18–25 describes creation as subjected to futility and groaning as it waits for liberation. It gives a broad picture of a creation that is not yet in its promised condition. It does not explain the mechanism behind every disease, earthquake, or animal death, and we should not claim that it does.

We should not assume that a particular hurricane struck a town because of a particular sin, or that a child became ill because God was punishing the family. Scripture sometimes records specific judgments, but it does not authorise us to label every disaster in that way. Jesus warned against drawing confident conclusions about the guilt of those who died in tragedies (Luke 13:1–5).

Some suffering is connected with the regular order of the world. A stable physical environment makes ordinary life possible, but the same natural processes can bring danger. This may help explain part of the question, but it does not explain every instance of suffering or remove its emotional weight. Nor should we assume every painful experience produces a visible lesson. Scripture teaches that trials can produce endurance and maturity (Romans 5:3–5; James 1:2–4), but this is not permission to treat sufferers as objects in a lesson for others. The right response is often practical care, prayer, protection, and patient presence—not speculation.

7. If God rules everything, why does he permit evil?

The Bible teaches that God is sovereign. He is not a distant observer who hopes events turn out well. He created all things, sustains them, and governs history according to his wisdom and purpose (Psalm 103:19; Daniel 4:34–35; Ephesians 1:11). Nothing can overthrow his rule or surprise him.

If God could prevent evil, why does he not prevent it all now? Scripture does not give a complete list of the reasons behind every instance of suffering. It does show that God can permit actions he hates while governing events toward purposes that are good and just.

Joseph's story is one of the clearest examples. His brothers acted out of jealousy and sold him into slavery. Their actions were genuinely wicked, and Joseph did not pretend otherwise. Yet years later he could say, “you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good” (Genesis 50:20). Notice the two intentions in the same event. The brothers meant evil. God intended good. God's purpose did not make their cruelty innocent, and their cruelty did not defeat God's purpose.

The crucifixion is an even greater example. Jesus was handed over and killed through the lawless actions of human beings, yet the New Testament says this happened according to God's definite plan and foreknowledge (Acts 2:23). The event involved human guilt and divine purpose. Those who condemned and crucified Jesus were not excused; God was accomplishing salvation through what they meant for evil.

This is not a formula that allows us to identify the good purpose behind every tragedy. It is a reason not to conclude that evil has placed the world beyond God's control. The Christian believes God can govern even what he forbids, restrain evil, bring good from it, and judge those who commit it. We may not see how in a particular case. The cross gives us reason to trust that his wisdom is not absent even when hidden.

8. Does God's sovereignty make him the author of sin?

If God ordains whatsoever comes to pass, does that mean he is morally responsible for evil? Does it make human choices unreal? The Bible holds together God's sovereign purpose and the genuine responsibility of creatures. It does not allow us to solve the mystery by denying either truth.

God is never tempted by evil, and he himself tempts no one (James 1:13–17). His holiness is absolute. He does not delight in wickedness, approve injustice, or commit sin. Yet Scripture also teaches that nothing falls outside his providence. The actions of sinful people remain under his rule without becoming morally good because he governs them.

We can distinguish between God's holy purpose in governing an event and the sinful purpose of the human agent who commits the evil. Joseph's brothers intended harm; God intended preservation. Those who crucified Jesus acted wickedly; God gave his Son to accomplish redemption. The event is not morally described by only one intention.

Some Christians use the language of primary and secondary causes: God is the sovereign Creator who sustains and governs creation, while creatures are real agents who act according to their own desires and are accountable for their choices. This is not meant to make human beings puppets. It expresses the Bible's teaching that God's providence and human agency are both real.

We should admit that this is a profound mystery. Mystery does not mean contradictions are acceptable or that we can stop thinking. It means God's revealed truth reaches further than our ability to understand every relation within it. We must not accuse God of evil, and we must not deny his rule simply because we cannot explain every detail.

9. Does free will explain the problem?

One familiar response says God allows evil because he created people with free will, and genuine love and moral goodness require the possibility of choosing otherwise. This identifies something important: love cannot be reduced to forced behaviour, and human beings are responsible moral agents. A world in which people make meaningful choices is different from a world of programmed machines.

But a simple free-will explanation does not answer every question. It may help explain why God permits moral evil committed by human beings, but what about suffering that does not appear to result from a human decision? What about the scale of suffering? Could God not have created people who freely chose what was right more often? Could he not prevent the worst consequences of wicked choices without removing human responsibility altogether?

Christians differ in how they describe freedom. Some emphasise the ability to choose between alternatives not determined by prior causes. Others emphasise that people act voluntarily according to their desires, even though their desires and choices remain under God's providence. Historic Christian theology has often affirmed that people are responsible because they willingly do what they choose, not because they are independent of God.

Whatever account of freedom one adopts, we should not make it carry more weight than it can bear. Human freedom is part of the biblical picture, but it is not a complete explanation for every form of suffering. The Christian answer rests on the whole biblical story, not on one philosophical proposal.

10. Can we know why a particular person suffers?

Usually, no. We may sometimes identify ordinary causes: a disease has a known biological process, an accident resulted from negligence, or a person was harmed by someone's deliberate violence. We should investigate those causes and act responsibly. But knowing how something happened is not the same as knowing God's full purpose in permitting it.

The book of Job is a powerful warning against pretending that we know. Job suffers extraordinary losses, and his friends repeatedly try to explain his pain by assuming he must have committed some hidden wrongdoing. Their speeches may sound religious and wise, but their central accusation is false. At the end, God rebukes them for not speaking rightly about him as Job has (Job 42:7–8).

The reader is given information Job himself does not possess. We see a heavenly scene that helps explain the test, but Job is never shown a full account of it. He is not handed a neat explanation that makes every loss feel manageable. Instead, God confronts him with the vastness of divine wisdom and the limits of human understanding (Job 38–41). Job responds in humility, and God remains the one worthy of trust.

That does not mean we should tell sufferers to stop asking questions. The Psalms are full of honest lament. “How long, O LORD?” is a biblical prayer (Psalm 13). Faith can grieve, protest injustice, and ask for deliverance. Lament brings pain before God rather than treating our limited perspective as the final judgment on his character.

When someone suffers, we should be slow to explain and quick to love. We can listen, sit with them, pray, provide practical help, seek medical care, report abuse, and pursue justice. We can say what Scripture says clearly: God sees, God is not indifferent, evil is wrong, and suffering will not have the final word. We should not claim to know God's secret reason for a tragedy when he has not told us.

11. How does the Bible speak about suffering?

The Bible is honest about the pain of life. It does not portray every faithful person as cheerful, healthy, wealthy, or free from fear. Abraham experienced uncertainty. Joseph was betrayed. Naomi grieved. David lamented. Jeremiah wept. Paul endured imprisonment, danger, and physical hardship. Jesus himself was described as a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief (Isaiah 53:3).

The Psalms teach believers to bring fear, confusion, and sorrow into prayer. Psalm 22 begins with the anguished cry, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” Psalm 42 asks why the soul is cast down. Psalm 73 wrestles with the apparent prosperity of the wicked. These prayers are not examples of faithlessness. They show that faith does not require pretending that the world is painless.

Jesus showed compassion toward sufferers. He touched the unclean, welcomed the excluded, healed the sick, fed the hungry, and wept at the tomb of Lazarus (John 11:33–36). His tears are striking because he knew he was about to raise Lazarus. He did not treat grief as an embarrassment to be corrected. He entered into it, even as he demonstrated his authority over death.

The apostles taught that suffering can become an occasion for endurance, tested character, and hope (Romans 5:3–5; 2 Corinthians 4:16–18; James 1:2–4). These passages do not say suffering is pleasant or that every believer will understand its benefit now. They teach that God is at work in his people and that hardship cannot separate them from his love.

Read these passages together. The Bible permits lament and commands perseverance; it condemns injustice and teaches patience; it promises God's presence and looks forward to the day when suffering ends. No single verse should be used to silence the rest of Scripture.

12. What does the cross tell us about God?

The cross is at the centre of the Christian answer to suffering. Christianity does not say that God watched human pain from a safe distance and sent advice. In the person of his Son, God the Son took on a human nature. Jesus entered our world, experienced hunger and exhaustion, knew rejection and betrayal, endured injustice, and suffered a violent death.

His suffering was not meaningless. Jesus died for sinners, bearing judgment in the place of his people and reconciling them to God (Isaiah 53:4–6; Mark 10:45; Romans 3:21–26; 1 Peter 2:24). The cross reveals both the seriousness of evil and the depth of divine love. Sin is not brushed aside as though it does not matter. It is judged. And sinners are not left without hope. God provides the sacrifice we could never provide for ourselves.

At Calvary, human evil was displayed in its terrible form. Religious leaders acted out of envy; political power was used unjustly; a crowd demanded the death of an innocent man; soldiers mocked and abused him. Yet the New Testament teaches that this death took place according to God's saving purpose (Acts 2:23; Acts 4:27–28). The greatest crime in human history became the means by which God accomplished redemption.

The cross does not make betrayal, torture, or murder good. It shows that God can bring saving good out of the worst evil without approving the evil itself. It gives reason to believe that God's purposes are wiser and more powerful than the intentions of those who oppose him.

Christians do not worship a God unfamiliar with suffering. The Son truly suffered in his human nature. He knows what it is to be afflicted. He is a sympathetic high priest who invites his people to draw near for mercy and help (Hebrews 4:14–16). This does not answer every question about why pain is permitted, but it challenges the idea that God is cold or indifferent.

13. Why has God not ended evil already?

If God intends to judge evil and renew creation, why does he wait? The New Testament teaches that Christ has come, died, risen, and ascended, and that he will return. The kingdom has been inaugurated, but its fullness has not yet arrived. Christians live between Christ's first coming and his return. We have forgiveness and the presence of the Spirit, but still experience sickness, persecution, grief, and death.

Peter explains that what some interpret as delay is connected with God's patience and his purpose to bring people to repentance (2 Peter 3:8–10). This is not a claim that God is unable to act or that evil is unimportant to him. It means the final judgment has not yet come, and the present time is still a time in which the gospel is proclaimed and sinners are called to turn to Christ.

When Christ returns, evil will not simply be managed more effectively. It will be judged. The dead will be raised, justice will be done, and God's people will dwell with him in a renewed creation (John 5:28–29; 1 Corinthians 15:20–28; Revelation 21:1–5). The promise is not that history will continue indefinitely with a slightly improved balance of good and evil. The promise is that evil will finally be removed.

We do not know the day of Christ's return. We are not called to calculate it. We are called to be faithful, do good, proclaim the gospel, and wait with hope. God's patience should never be mistaken for indifference, and the delay of judgment should never be used as an excuse for injustice.

14. Does suffering disprove God's existence or goodness?

Suffering is a powerful reason people struggle to believe. It is not, however, a strict logical contradiction of the Christian claim. A perfectly good God may have morally sufficient reasons for permitting suffering for a time, even when those reasons are not fully known to us. Joseph's story and the cross show that God can govern events that include real evil toward a good purpose without making the evil itself good.

The evidential question is more difficult: are there instances or amounts of suffering that make God's existence unlikely? Christians should not dismiss this by saying that we can imagine a possible reason for every tragedy. The honest answer is that we do not know the reason for every instance, and some suffering remains deeply perplexing.

But the argument from apparently pointless suffering depends partly on what we can reasonably expect to know. If God is infinitely wise and we are finite, it is possible we would not be able to see all the connections between events, consequences, and purposes. This does not prove that every instance of suffering has a good reason. It challenges the assumption that because we cannot see a reason, there cannot be one.

More importantly, Christianity does not ask us to infer God's goodness from the world considered in isolation. It directs us to God's self-revelation, supremely in Jesus Christ. We see his holiness in the law, his patience throughout Scripture, his love in the incarnation and cross, and his victory in the resurrection. The resurrection is God's vindication of the crucified Jesus and the pledge that death will not reign forever.

There is also a moral question for the critic: what makes suffering objectively evil? Many non-Christian accounts of morality are thoughtful and sophisticated, and Christians should engage them fairly. But if the universe is ultimately impersonal and human beings are temporary products of natural processes, it is not immediately obvious why human dignity and moral obligations should have the objective weight we instinctively give them. This does not make an atheist's grief or protest insincere. It means the problem of evil is not a one-sided argument that leaves every alternative worldview untouched.

The Christian case is cumulative. Creation, conscience, moral responsibility, the history of Israel, the person of Jesus, his death, and his resurrection belong to one account of reality. Suffering is a serious objection that deserves an honest response, but it does not erase the evidence for Christ or overturn what God has revealed about himself.

15. How should Christians respond to suffering?

First, refuse to call evil good. The fact that God can bring good out of suffering does not mean abuse, oppression, betrayal, or violence should be tolerated. Christians should protect those in danger, expose wrongdoing, support victims, seek justice, and relieve pain where they can. Praying for someone does not excuse us from helping when help is within reach.

Second, learn to lament. There is a place for tears, questions, and grief. Jesus wept. Paul told believers to weep with those who weep (Romans 12:15). A church that rushes people past sorrow may quote true words while failing to love. Sometimes the most faithful thing a friend can do is listen, remain present, and carry practical burdens.

Third, do not judge sufferers. Do not assume illness proves weak faith, poverty proves laziness, or tragedy reveals some special sin. The Bible warns against such judgments. We should examine ourselves, but must not turn another person's suffering into an accusation without biblical warrant.

Fourth, use the means God provides. Seek medical treatment. Ask for help. Report crimes and abuse to appropriate authorities. Support people who are grieving. Work for just laws and honest institutions. Providence does not make action unnecessary; God ordinarily works through means, including the care and service of his people.

Finally, keep your eyes on Christ. This does not mean ignoring the present or pretending pain does not matter. It means suffering is not the whole story. Christ has died, Christ is risen, Christ reigns, and Christ will return. Nothing can separate those who belong to him from the love of God (Romans 8:31–39).

16. What hope does Christianity finally offer?

The Christian hope is not merely that our souls will escape the world and leave its troubles behind. Scripture promises the resurrection of the body and the renewal of creation. God will dwell with his people. Death, mourning, crying, and pain will be no more, because the former things will have passed away (Revelation 21:1–5).

“For I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us.”Romans 8:18

This promise rests on the resurrection of Jesus Christ. His empty tomb is the beginning of the new creation and the guarantee that those who belong to him will also be raised. Paul calls Christ the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep (1 Corinthians 15:20–23). Death is an enemy that will be defeated, not a natural doorway we should learn to welcome.

God will not forget victims. No injustice will be overlooked. No hidden cruelty will remain hidden forever. The Judge of all the earth will do what is right (Genesis 18:25). Those who have suffered will not be asked to pretend evil did not matter. God will judge evil and make all things new.

We should not claim that we can already see the full shape of God's purposes in every painful event. Some questions will remain unanswered in this life. Some wounds are deep, and some losses cannot be repaired by anything we can offer one another now. Christian hope does not minimise those losses. It says they are not beyond the reach of the risen Christ.

If you are suffering, you do not need to solve every philosophical question before you cry out to God. Come to him honestly. If you are unsure whether you believe, take the question seriously and consider Jesus: his compassion, teaching, death, and resurrection. The Christian invitation is not to trust an impersonal principle or our ability to make sense of every tragedy. It is to trust the living God who has made himself known in his Son.

The Bible tells us that God made a good world, humanity rebelled, and creation now groans under the consequences of sin. God remains sovereign and holy; human beings are responsible for their evil; and God can bring good from what he forbids. Above all, Christ entered our suffering, bore sin at the cross, rose from the dead, and will return to judge evil and renew creation. We do not yet know every answer. But we have reason to trust the One who has promised that evil will not have the final word.

Read the surrounding chapters rather than treating individual verses as isolated proof texts.

  2. Job 1–2; 38–42 — undeserved suffering and human limits.

  4. Psalm 73 — wrestling with injustice.
  5. Isaiah 52:13–53:12 — the suffering servant.
  6. Luke 13:1–5 — Jesus rejects simplistic conclusions about tragedy.
  7. John 11 — Jesus weeps and raises Lazarus.
  8. Romans 5:1–11 — suffering, endurance, and hope.
  9. Romans 8:18–39 — creation's groaning and God's love.
  10. Acts 2:22–24 — human guilt and God's purpose in the cross.
  11. 1 Corinthians 15 — resurrection and the defeat of death.
  12. Revelation 21–22 — the renewed creation.

This article distinguishes between what Scripture clearly teaches and what remains hidden to us. It does not claim that every tragedy can be explained by identifying a specific divine purpose. Its central claim is that the biblical account of creation, sin, providence, redemption, judgment, and new creation gives reason to trust God's goodness without calling evil good or minimising suffering.

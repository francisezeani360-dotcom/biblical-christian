Justification: How Are We Saved?

How can guilty people be made right with a holy God? A clear biblical study of grace, the cross of Christ, faith, and the gift of a righteous standing before God.
A Scripture-centred study · Key passages, common questions, and further reading
The central answer. We are saved by God's grace, through faith in Jesus Christ—not by earning acceptance through our works. Christ died for sinners and rose again. Everyone who turns to God and trusts in Christ is forgiven and counted righteous because of what Christ has done. This faith does not remain alone: it produces a changed life of love and obedience.

Imagine standing in a courtroom with no honest defence. The evidence is true. The law is good. The judge is perfectly just. You cannot erase what you have done, offer a payment large enough to undo it, or persuade the judge to pretend that guilt does not matter. What hope would you have?

That is not an exaggerated picture of the human condition before God. Scripture says that all have sinned (Romans 3:23). Our problem is deeper than a few mistakes or a lack of religious knowledge. We have failed to love God as he deserves, broken his good commands, and become unable to make ourselves righteous by our own efforts.

Yet the Bible announces astonishing good news: God justifies the ungodly through Jesus Christ. He does not save us because he discovers that we were secretly good enough. He saves us because of his own mercy and because Christ has done what we could never do. To understand the gospel clearly, we need to understand justification.

1. What is justification?

Justification is God's gracious act of declaring a sinner righteous in his sight because of Jesus Christ. It is a verdict: God pardons our sins and accepts us as righteous, not because of our personal record, but because of Christ and his finished work.

The language comes from the courtroom. A judge justifies when he declares someone to be in the right. In the Bible, justification is not the same thing as gradually becoming a better person. It is God's declaration about a person's standing before him. The change is real and decisive: the guilty person is forgiven and welcomed as righteous.

Romans 4:5 makes the wonder especially clear. Paul speaks of the one who does not work to earn acceptance but believes in the God who justifies the ungodly; that faith is counted as righteousness. God does not call evil good or ignore justice. The following chapters explain how this verdict is possible through Christ.

“Since we have been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.”Romans 5:1

Justification answers the question: How can I stand accepted before God? The answer is not, “By becoming good enough.” It is, “Through Jesus Christ, received by faith.”

2. Why do we need to be justified?

We need justification because God is holy and we are sinners. God is not a flawed human authority whose standards are arbitrary. He is perfectly good, and his law reflects his character. He commands us to love him with our whole being and to love our neighbours as ourselves (Matthew 22:37–40).

Sin is not merely breaking a list of rules. It is rebellion against our Creator: putting ourselves at the centre, withholding the love and obedience that belong to God, and harming others through our desires, words, and actions. Some sins are public and obvious; others are hidden in pride, envy, selfishness, bitterness, lust, and unbelief. We cannot measure our standing before God by comparing ourselves with people who seem worse.

Romans 3:9–20 removes every ground for boasting. Jew and Gentile alike are under sin. The law exposes our guilt; it does not provide a ladder by which sinners can climb into God's favour. If acceptance depended on perfect obedience, none of us could stand.

That does not mean every person is as outwardly wicked as possible, or that human beings are incapable of acts of kindness. It means that no one has lived in the complete, wholehearted righteousness God requires. Even our best deeds cannot erase our guilt or purchase God's verdict.

We therefore need more than advice, inspiration, or a fresh start. We need forgiveness. We need reconciliation with God. We need a righteousness we cannot produce for ourselves.

3. How can God justify sinners and remain just?

This is the great question at the heart of the gospel. If God simply declares guilty people innocent without dealing with their sin, would he still be just? A good judge does not call evil harmless. Scripture's answer is that God has dealt with sin through the death of his Son.

Romans 3:21–26 says that God's righteousness has been revealed apart from the works of the law, although the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it. Sinners are justified freely by grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus. God presented Christ as the atoning sacrifice by his blood, received through faith. In this way God shows himself to be both just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus.

At the cross, Jesus did not die as a helpless victim whose death was meaningless. He willingly gave himself for sinners. He bore the judgment our sins deserved. Isaiah 53 describes the servant who was pierced for transgressions and crushed for iniquities; the Lord laid on him the iniquity of his people. Jesus himself said that the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45).

His resurrection is equally essential. Jesus was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification (Romans 4:25). The resurrection declares that death did not hold him and that his saving work is not defeated. The risen Christ is the living Saviour in whom we trust.

At the cross we see both the seriousness of sin and the depth of God's love. Sin is so serious that it required the death of the Son; God's love is so great that the Son willingly gave himself for his people. The gospel does not ask us to choose between a just God and a merciful God. In Christ, God is just and the one who justifies.

4. Whose righteousness counts?

We are not justified by presenting God with a record of our own moral achievements. The righteousness on which our acceptance rests is Christ's. He lived in perfect obedience to the Father, loved without sin, fulfilled the will of God, and gave himself as the sacrifice for sin. He is the righteous one who died for the unrighteous to bring us to God (1 Peter 3:18).

Paul describes this as receiving righteousness from God through faith in Christ (Philippians 3:8–9). In 2 Corinthians 5:21 he says that the sinless Christ was made sin for us so that in him we might become the righteousness of God. Christians have explained the precise language of this exchange in different ways, but the heart of the text is clear: our hope is found in Christ, not in our own goodness.

This is sometimes called the imputation of righteousness: Christ's saving obedience is counted to those who belong to him, while their sins are dealt with through his sacrifice. The word may be unfamiliar, but the truth is precious. God accepts believers because of Christ. We do not stand before him wearing the thin clothing of our best efforts; we stand clothed in the righteousness he provides.

That does not mean Jesus' character is transferred into us so that we instantly become morally perfect. The verdict of justification and the ongoing transformation of our lives are closely connected, but they are not the same thing. God declares us righteous in Christ, and then, by the Spirit, he teaches us to live in a way that increasingly reflects Christ.

5. How do we receive this gift?

We receive justification through faith in Jesus Christ. Faith is not a payment, a meritorious work, or a spiritual achievement that makes God owe us salvation. Faith is the empty hand that receives Christ. Its value does not lie in how impressive or strong it feels, but in the Saviour to whom it looks.

To believe in Christ is to trust him: to rest your hope on who he is and what he has done. It is more than agreeing that Jesus existed or that certain facts about him are true. Even demons know facts about God (James 2:19). Saving faith entrusts oneself to Christ as the crucified and risen Lord, the only sufficient Saviour.

The New Testament also speaks of repentance. Jesus called people to repent and believe the gospel (Mark 1:15). Repentance is a change of mind and heart that turns from sin to God. It is not a promise that you will never struggle again, nor is it a way of paying for past wrongs. It is the turning that belongs with genuine trust in Christ. We do not repent in order to make ourselves worthy of mercy; we come to the merciful God, confessing that we need it.

Faith and repentance should not be treated as rival ways of earning salvation. They belong together in the response to the gospel. We turn from sin and turn to Christ. Our confidence is not in the quality of our turning, but in the sufficiency of the Saviour.

“For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast.”Ephesians 2:8–9

We should be careful not to turn faith into a new work. A person may wonder, “Have I believed strongly enough?” or “Was my prayer sincere enough?” The gospel directs us away from measuring ourselves and toward Christ. A weak hand can receive a strong Saviour. The question is not whether your faith is impressive, but whether its object—Jesus Christ—is trustworthy.

6. What place do good works have?

If we are saved by grace and not by works, does the way we live matter? Absolutely. The Bible rejects both self-salvation and a faith that makes peace with sin.

Ephesians 2:8–9 says salvation is not the result of works, so no one can boast. But verse 10 immediately says that we are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared for us to walk in. Notice the order: good works are not the root of salvation; they are its fruit. We are not saved by good works, but we are saved for a life of good works.

Good works include love, honesty, sexual purity, generosity, forgiveness, justice, care for the vulnerable, self-control, prayer, worship, and obedience to Christ. They do not buy God's favour. They are the grateful response of people who have received mercy. The Holy Spirit works in believers, changing their desires and enabling them to grow in holiness (Galatians 5:16–25).

Growth is often gradual. Christians still stumble, need correction, confess sin, and depend on grace every day. The presence of struggle does not automatically mean faith is false. But a settled refusal to repent, combined with a claim to belong to Christ, is a serious contradiction that Scripture warns us not to ignore (1 John 2:3–6; 3:4–10).

7. Does James contradict Paul?

Paul teaches that a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law (Romans 3:28). James says that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone (James 2:24). At first glance, these statements can sound opposed. But both writers are confronting a different error.

Paul rejects the idea that sinners can earn a righteous standing before God through their works. James rejects the claim that a bare profession of faith, which produces no love or obedience, is living faith. James asks what good it is if someone says he has faith but has no works (James 2:14). His example is a person who offers religious words to a needy brother or sister but refuses practical help. Such a faith claim is empty.

Paul himself teaches that faith works through love (Galatians 5:6), and that the purpose of salvation is a life of good works (Ephesians 2:10). James points to Abraham's obedience in Genesis 22 as the demonstration and fulfilment of the faith he had already shown in Genesis 15. His works brought his faith to expression; they did not replace trust in God's promise.

The two apostles are not teaching two different gospels. We are accepted by God through faith, not by the merit of our deeds. Yet the faith that receives Christ is never content to remain barren. Works do not make faith saving; they show that faith is alive.

8. Justification and union with Christ

Salvation is not simply receiving a legal document from a distant judge. The believer is united to Jesus Christ. The New Testament describes Christians as being “in Christ”: joined to him by faith, sharing in the benefits of his death and resurrection, and belonging to him as members of his people (Romans 6:1–11; Ephesians 1:3–14).

Because we belong to Christ, his death counts as the answer to our guilt and his risen life is the beginning and guarantee of our new life. In Christ we receive more than justification. We are adopted into God's family, reconciled to him, given the Holy Spirit, and brought into the hope of resurrection and eternal life.

These blessings must be distinguished, but they must not be separated. Justification is God's verdict; sanctification is his work of making us holy; adoption is his welcome of us as children. They are not competing ways to be saved. They are gifts of the one salvation found in Christ.

9. Can a justified person have assurance?

Yes. Christian assurance rests first on the character and promise of God, and on the finished work of Jesus Christ—not on a claim that we have become flawless. Romans 8:1 declares that there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. Jesus promises that whoever comes to him will not be cast out (John 6:37).

Believers may still experience seasons of doubt, grief, temptation, and spiritual weakness. In those moments, look again to Christ. Confess sin honestly. Remember the promises of the gospel. Seek the encouragement and prayer of mature Christians. Do not make your changing emotions the final judge of God's unchanging word.

At the same time, assurance should not be used to excuse indifference to sin. Scripture calls us to examine ourselves (2 Corinthians 13:5), to continue in Christ, and to look for the fruit of genuine faith. The point is not to become obsessed with our performance, but to see whether our profession is accompanied by trust, repentance, love, and perseverance.

If you have sinned, do not imagine that you must first repair yourself before coming to Christ. Come to him because you need mercy. The same grace that justifies also teaches us to reject ungodliness and live a new life (Titus 2:11–14).

10. Questions people often ask

Does justification mean God pretends I never sinned?

No. God does not deny reality or ignore evil. He deals with sin through the real death of Christ. The believer is forgiven because Christ bore sin's judgment, and accepted because of Christ's righteousness.

Is faith itself a good work that earns salvation?

No. Faith receives; it does not purchase. The ground of acceptance is Christ, and the source of salvation is God's grace. Even the ability to come to Christ is dependent on God's gracious work in us.

Must I clean up my life before I can come to Jesus?

No. Come as a sinner who needs a Saviour. You cannot make yourself fit for grace. But do not use grace as an excuse to remain at peace with sin. Christ receives sinners and begins to change them.

Can someone be justified and never show any change?

We should not claim to know the hidden state of another person's heart. But Scripture gives no comfort to a person who claims faith while persistently refusing Christ's rule. Living faith bears fruit, even if that fruit grows slowly and imperfectly.

What about baptism, the Lord's Supper, prayer, and church attendance?

These are important gifts and commands for Christian life. Baptism publicly identifies a believer with Christ; the Lord's Supper proclaims his death and nourishes the church's faith; prayer and fellowship are essential parts of discipleship. But none of these is a payment that earns justification. They are responses of faith and means through which God strengthens his people.

What if my faith feels very small?

Do not confuse the strength of your feelings with the strength of Christ. Bring your doubts and weakness to him. The gospel calls you to trust the Saviour, not to trust in your ability to generate perfect confidence.

11. What should we do with this message?

The gospel calls for a response. Jesus commands us to repent and believe. Do not hide behind religious activity, family background, church membership, moral respectability, or knowledge of Christian language. None of these can remove guilt or reconcile you to God.

Admit the truth about your sin. Stop defending what God calls evil. Turn to him, asking for mercy. Believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God who died for sinners and rose again. Entrust yourself to him—not as one more helper added to your own efforts, but as the only Saviour on whom your hope rests.

If you already trust Christ, let this truth deepen your gratitude and humility. You have no reason to boast over another sinner. You were saved by grace. Let that grace shape how you treat people, how you fight sin, how you forgive, how you worship, and how you speak about the gospel. A justified person has been given peace with God and a new reason to live.

12. The gospel in a few words

God is holy. We are sinners. We cannot make ourselves righteous or erase our guilt. Jesus Christ, the Son of God, lived without sin, died for sinners, and rose again. God justifies freely by grace those who put their trust in Christ. We are not accepted because of our works; we are accepted because of Jesus. And those who are united to him are changed by the Holy Spirit and brought into a life of love and obedience.

Do not look to your goodness as the reason God should receive you. Look to Jesus Christ. His work is sufficient, his promise is sure, and all who come to him in faith will find mercy.

Read these passages for yourself

The best way to test this explanation is to read the biblical passages in context. Begin with these:

  1. Romans 3:9–31 — universal sin, grace, redemption, and justification through faith.
  2. Romans 4 — Abraham, faith, and righteousness counted apart from works.
  3. Romans 5:1–11 — peace with God through Jesus Christ.
  4. Galatians 2:15–21 — justification through faith in Christ.
  5. Galatians 3 — receiving the Spirit and the promise through faith.
  6. Ephesians 2:1–10 — salvation by grace and the good works that follow.
  7. 2 Corinthians 5:17–21 — reconciliation and the saving work of Christ.
  8. James 2:14–26 — the evidence of living faith.
  9. Titus 3:3–8 — salvation by mercy, renewal, and the call to good works.
  10. Romans 8:1–17 — no condemnation, life in the Spirit, and adoption.

This study aims to explain the biblical teaching clearly and to distinguish justification from the transformation that follows it. Read the passages in context, compare Scripture with Scripture, and let the Word of God—not a slogan or a human tradition—shape your understanding of the gospel.

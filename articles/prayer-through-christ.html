Prayer: Coming to God Through Christ

A comprehensive biblical study of Christian prayer: coming to the Father through Jesus Christ, by the help of the Holy Spirit.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

Prayer is one of the most remarkable privileges God gives his people. The Creator of heaven and earth invites sinners who have been reconciled to him through Jesus Christ to draw near, speak honestly, ask for help, confess their sins, give thanks, and worship. We do not come because we have earned an audience with God or because we know the right words. We come because Christ has opened the way.

“Pray then like this: “Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name.”Matthew 6:9 — reference for study

Almost everyone has felt the need to pray at some point. In fear, grief, uncertainty, gratitude, or desperation, people instinctively look beyond themselves for help. Yet Christian prayer is not simply the human desire to reach for something greater. It is a response to the God who has made himself known and who calls his people to approach him through his Son.

That distinction matters. Prayer is not a technique for controlling circumstances, a way of persuading a reluctant God to become kind, or a religious performance by which we try to prove our worth. Nor is it a way of earning salvation. The good news of the gospel is that sinners are reconciled to God through the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus Christ. Those who belong to Christ are welcomed into the Father's presence. Prayer flows from that grace.

To understand Christian prayer, we must therefore begin not with ourselves, our needs, or our feelings, but with God: who he is, what he has done through Christ, and what he has promised to those who come to him.

At the heart of Christian prayer: We come to the Father through Jesus Christ, by the help of the Holy Spirit. Our confidence rests not in the quality of our words, but in the finished work and continuing intercession of Christ.

Why should Christians pray?

God does not command prayer because he lacks information. Jesus says that the Father knows what his children need before they ask him (Matthew 6:8). God is not ignorant of our circumstances, nor does he need us to persuade him to pay attention. He is all-knowing, wise, sovereign, and good.

So why pray? Because God has commanded it, invited us to do it, and chosen prayer as one of the means through which he works in the lives of his people. Prayer expresses dependence. It is an acknowledgement that we are creatures, not our own providers or saviours. We need God's mercy, wisdom, strength, correction, and care.

Prayer is also part of our fellowship with God. Christians do not merely receive gifts from a distant benefactor; through Christ they are brought into a real relationship with the living God. We speak to him because he has spoken to us in his Word. We praise him for who he is, confess our sins, bring our concerns before him, and ask that his will be done.

The Bible repeatedly commands believers to pray (Luke 18:1; Romans 12:12; Colossians 4:2; 1 Thessalonians 5:17). These commands are not burdensome demands from a harsh master. They are instructions from the Father who knows our weakness and calls us to depend on him. Prayer does not make God more sovereign or more loving. It teaches us to live in the light of the sovereignty and love he already possesses.

We come to God through Jesus Christ

This is the foundation of Christian prayer. Sin is not merely a collection of mistakes that can be corrected by becoming more religious. It is rebellion against our holy Creator. By nature, we do not possess the righteousness required to stand before him. Our greatest problem is not that we have failed to pray enough; it is that we are sinners who need reconciliation with God.

God has provided that reconciliation through Jesus Christ. The Son of God took on human nature, lived in perfect obedience, bore the judgment due to sinners in his death, and rose bodily from the grave. Those who trust in him are forgiven and counted righteous before God, not on the basis of their own works, but because of Christ. His sacrifice is sufficient. His work does not need to be completed by our prayers, religious efforts, or personal achievements.

Because of Jesus, believers have access to the Father. Paul writes that through Christ “we both have access in one Spirit to the Father” (Ephesians 2:18). The writer of Hebrews urges Christians to draw near to the throne of grace with confidence because Jesus is our great High Priest (Hebrews 4:14–16). Christ lives to intercede for those who come to God through him (Hebrews 7:23–25).

This means that the Christian's confidence in prayer rests outside the Christian. It rests in Jesus—his righteousness, his sacrifice, his resurrection, and his continuing priestly ministry. We do not approach God presenting a record of good behaviour and asking him to reward us. We approach through the Son whom the Father has given.

Remember this: You do not need to make yourself worthy before you pray. If you are in Christ, you come to the Father because Christ is worthy. When you are conscious of weakness or sin, do not hide from God or try to pay for your failure with religious activity. Come honestly, confess your sin, and depend again on the mercy secured by Christ.

The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit

Christian prayer reflects the truth that the one true God is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Father welcomes his children; the Son is the mediator through whom they have access; and the Holy Spirit helps them in their weakness. This is not a formula we must repeat in every sentence. It is the gracious reality that makes Christian prayer possible.

We pray to the Father

When Jesus taught his disciples to pray, he began, “Our Father in heaven” (Matthew 6:9). Believers are brought into the family of God through Christ and receive the Spirit of adoption, by whom they cry, “Abba! Father!” (Romans 8:15–16; Galatians 4:4–7). God is not simply a powerful being we attempt to contact. He is the Father who has adopted his people and cares for them.

Calling God Father does not mean treating him casually or assuming that he exists to fulfil every desire. Jesus teaches us to honour his name and submit to his will. We approach with the reverence due to the holy God and the confidence appropriate to children who are loved by him.

We come through the Son

Jesus is not merely an example of someone who prayed well. He is the mediator who reconciles his people to God. There is one God and one mediator between God and humanity, Christ Jesus (1 Timothy 2:5). His death removes the barrier of guilt, and his continuing intercession assures believers that their access to God does not depend on their own spiritual performance.

The Spirit helps us

Paul acknowledges that believers do not always know what to pray for as they ought. The Spirit helps us in our weakness and intercedes according to God's will (Romans 8:26–27). This is a profound comfort. Our prayers may be hesitant, incomplete, or burdened by sorrow, but the Spirit is not limited by our inability to express ourselves perfectly.

The Spirit's help does not make Scripture unnecessary or turn every inner impression into a message from God. He is the Spirit of truth, and his work does not contradict the Word he inspired. We grow in prayer as our minds are shaped by Scripture and our hearts are taught to depend on God.

Jesus teaches us to pray

The disciples saw Jesus pray and asked him to teach them (Luke 11:1). Their request is worth noticing. They did not ask for a technique to impress people or a formula that would guarantee success. They wanted to learn from their Lord how to approach God.

Jesus taught them what is commonly called the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13; Luke 11:2–4). It is both a prayer Christians may pray and a pattern that shapes the rest of their prayers. Its order is instructive: God's name, kingdom, and will come before our daily needs. Our concerns matter, but they are placed within the greater purpose of honouring God.

“Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name”

Prayer begins with God. His name represents his character and reputation. To ask that his name be hallowed is to ask that he be honoured as holy—by us, by the church, and throughout the world. We should not rush past worship as though it were an unnecessary introduction before we reach the requests that matter to us.

“Your kingdom come”

We ask God to advance the reign of his Son: that the gospel would be proclaimed, sinners would be brought to faith, churches would be strengthened, and Christ's rule would be acknowledged. We also long for the day when Jesus returns and his kingdom is seen in its fullness.

“Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven”

Prayer is not an attempt to bend God's purposes to our preferences. We ask him to make us willing to obey his revealed commands and to bring about his wise purposes. This does not mean we must pretend that suffering is pleasant or that we have no strong desires. Jesus himself prayed in Gethsemane with deep sorrow, yet submitted to the Father's will (Matthew 26:36–46).

“Give us this day our daily bread”

God invites us to ask for ordinary necessities. Food, shelter, work, health, strength, and the needs of those entrusted to our care are not too small to bring before him. Asking for daily bread teaches dependence: what we have is received from God's hand.

“Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors”

Believers still need to confess their sins. Our daily confession does not mean that Christ's sacrifice was incomplete or that we must repeatedly earn justification. It is the honest response of children who know their Father is holy and who desire restored fellowship and growth in obedience. Those who have received mercy must also be ready to forgive others, while never confusing forgiveness with pretending that evil did not happen or that justice does not matter.

“Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil”

We acknowledge our weakness and ask God to preserve us from sin, protect us from the evil one, and keep us faithful. We should not presume that we can resist temptation by our own strength. We ask for God's help and also take seriously the practical steps Scripture commands: watchfulness, fleeing sin, resisting the devil, and encouraging one another.

The Lord's Prayer teaches us to bring the whole of life before God. It gives shape to our worship, priorities, needs, repentance, relationships, and dependence.

What should we bring to God?

There is no part of a Christian's life that must be kept outside prayer. Scripture gives us several recurring expressions of prayer. They overlap, and a single prayer may include more than one.

Adoration: praise God for who he is

Adoration is worship directed toward God's character and works. Praise him for his holiness, wisdom, power, mercy, justice, faithfulness, and love. Praise is not flattery; it is the fitting response of creatures who recognise the glory of their Creator. Read passages such as Psalms 8, 96, 103, and 145 and let their descriptions of God shape your words.

Confession: acknowledge sin honestly

Do not hide, excuse, or rename sin. Agree with God about what is wrong, ask for forgiveness, and seek grace to turn from it. First John 1:9 assures believers that God is faithful and just to forgive and cleanse those who confess. This promise rests on God's character and Christ's atoning work, not on the emotional intensity of our confession.

Confession should be specific where possible. Instead of saying only, “Forgive me for everything,” acknowledge the actual pride, dishonesty, bitterness, impurity, selfishness, or lack of love that Scripture has exposed. Then trust Christ rather than remaining trapped in self-condemnation.

Thanksgiving: remember God's goodness

Give thanks for salvation, for Christ, for the Scriptures, for the church, for daily provision, for people who have served you, and for mercies you might otherwise overlook. Thanksgiving does not require us to call painful circumstances good. It means recognising that God remains good and faithful even when life is difficult (1 Thessalonians 5:18).

Supplication: ask for what is needed

Bring your needs before God. Ask for wisdom, provision, strength, protection, healing, endurance, and help in decisions. Philippians 4:6–7 teaches believers to bring their requests to God with thanksgiving. The promise is not that every request will be granted exactly as we imagine, but that God's peace guards his people as they entrust their concerns to him.

Intercession: pray for others

Prayer should not revolve only around our own lives. Pray for family, friends, neighbours, church leaders, fellow believers, those who suffer, those who oppose you, and those who have not yet believed the gospel. Paul urges that prayers and intercessions be made for all kinds of people, including those in authority (1 Timothy 2:1–4). Ask God to save, strengthen, correct, protect, and provide for them.

Lament: bring grief and confusion honestly

The Bible does not teach us to conceal sorrow behind religious language. Many psalms express grief, fear, questions, and the painful feeling that God seems far away. The writers bring these struggles to God rather than turning away from him. Biblical lament speaks honestly about suffering while continuing to seek the Lord and remember his character (Psalms 13; 42; 77).

What does it mean to pray in Jesus' name?

Jesus tells his disciples to ask in his name (John 14:13–14; 16:23–24). This is sometimes misunderstood as though adding the words “in Jesus' name” to the end of a request guarantees that God must grant it. But Jesus' name is not a password, and prayer is not a mechanism for getting whatever we want.

“Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need.”Hebrews 4:16

To pray in Jesus' name is to come on the basis of who he is and what he has done, relying on his authority and seeking what is consistent with his character and purposes. It means that our requests should be shaped by our relationship to him. We cannot appeal to Jesus' name while knowingly seeking what contradicts his Word.

First John 5:14 describes confidence in prayer in this way: God hears us when we ask according to his will. That does not mean we must know every detail of God's secret plan before we pray. It means we trust his revealed goodness and submit our desires to his wisdom. We ask boldly, but not presumptuously.

What about unanswered prayer?

One of the hardest experiences in the Christian life is praying earnestly and not receiving what we asked for. A loved one remains ill. A door stays closed. A relationship is not restored. A burden continues longer than we expected. We may wonder whether God has heard us or whether our faith is too weak.

Scripture does not promise that every request will be answered in the way or at the time we desire. God is sovereign and wise; we are not. Sometimes he says no. Sometimes he makes us wait. Sometimes he answers in a way we did not anticipate. We may not understand his reasons in this life, but we are never asked to believe that he has ceased to be good.

Consider Paul's repeated request for the removal of his “thorn in the flesh.” The Lord did not remove it. Instead, he answered, “My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:7–10). Paul was not abandoned. He received sustaining grace, though the circumstance remained.

Jesus also prayed in Gethsemane that the cup might pass from him, yet submitted to the Father's will (Matthew 26:36–46). His prayer shows that honest desire and submission are not enemies. We may tell God exactly what we long for while entrusting the outcome to him.

There are also biblical warnings that our conduct matters. Cherished sin, hypocrisy, selfish motives, and refusal to listen to God's instruction are serious obstacles to a life of prayer (Psalm 66:18; Proverbs 28:9; James 4:3). These warnings should lead us to repentance, not to the conclusion that every unanswered prayer proves a particular hidden sin. We must not presume to explain another person's suffering or claim certainty where God has not given it.

When an answer does not come as you hoped: keep bringing your grief to God. Ask for what you need. Seek help from mature believers. Examine your heart by Scripture without inventing accusations against yourself. Remember Christ's finished work, and trust that the Father knows what you need even when you cannot understand what he is doing.

Prayer, faith, and the gospel

Prayer is important, but prayer itself does not save. A person can pray often, use religious language, attend church, and still remain unreconciled to God. The foundation of salvation is not the sincerity, frequency, or eloquence of our prayers. It is Jesus Christ and his finished work.

The gospel calls sinners to repent and believe in Christ. We are justified by God's grace through faith, not by works (Romans 3:21–28; Ephesians 2:8–10). Repentance is not a payment for sin or an attempt to make ourselves worthy. It is a turning from sin and false confidence to God, inseparable from genuine faith. Those who trust Christ are welcomed by the Father.

Prayer is therefore a privilege of those who have been brought near through Christ. If you have not trusted him, your greatest need is not to master a prayer routine. Your need is to be reconciled to God. Turn to Christ, who died for sinners and rose again. Do not place your confidence in religious activity. Trust him.

And if you are a Christian, do not treat prayer as a way of keeping God's acceptance by your own performance. You are not accepted because you have prayed enough today. You are accepted in Christ. Prayer is one of the ways you enjoy and express the relationship he has secured for you.

Pray privately and sincerely

Jesus warns against praying in order to be seen and admired by other people (Matthew 6:5–6). Public prayer has a proper place, but it must not become a performance. The purpose is to address God, not to impress an audience.

Private prayer gives room for honest confession, quiet reflection, and personal dependence. Jesus himself withdrew to pray (Mark 1:35; Luke 5:16). Find a regular place and time where you can give attention to God without unnecessary distraction. The point is not that a particular location is more spiritual, but that we should make room for this important part of Christian life.

Do not confuse sincerity with a requirement to produce a certain feeling. Some days prayer will be full of joy; other days you may come tired, anxious, or unsure what to say. Speak truthfully. Use the words of Scripture when your own words fail. God does not measure the value of prayer by how impressive it sounds.

Pray with the church

Christian prayer is personal, but it is not meant to be isolated. In the New Testament, believers pray together. The early church devoted itself to prayer (Acts 2:42), prayed for boldness in witness (Acts 4:23–31), and prayed for those in danger and need (Acts 12:5). Churches are instructed to pray for one another, for their leaders, and for the spread of the gospel.

Praying with other Christians reminds us that we belong to one body. It teaches us to carry one another's burdens, to rejoice with those who rejoice, and to weep with those who weep (Romans 12:15). It also helps correct the narrowness of prayers that never move beyond our own concerns.

Do not neglect gathered worship and the ordinary life of the local church. Pray with fellow believers, receive biblical teaching, share needs wisely, and encourage others. You do not have to be eloquent to take part. A simple, sincere prayer grounded in Scripture is far better than a performance designed to attract attention.

How can you grow in prayer?

There is no secret technique that makes someone a mature Christian overnight. Growth usually comes through ordinary faithfulness: hearing God's Word, praying regularly, worshipping with the church, confessing sin, and learning to depend on the Lord in every season.

1. Let Scripture lead your prayers

Read a passage and turn its truths into prayer. If you read of God's holiness, praise him for it. If you read a command, ask for grace to obey. If you read a promise, thank him and ask for faith to trust it. The Psalms are especially helpful because they teach us how to praise, lament, confess, and hope.

2. Establish a regular time

Choose a time you can sustain. It may be in the morning, during a quiet part of the day, or before bed. A regular pattern helps prayer become part of ordinary life rather than something reserved for emergencies. Do not be discouraged if your routine is interrupted; begin again.

3. Pray specifically

Instead of speaking only in general terms, bring particular needs before God. Pray for a person by name, a difficult responsibility, a temptation you are facing, a church member who is suffering, or an opportunity to speak about Christ. Specific prayers also help you recognise and give thanks for God's answers.

4. Keep a simple prayer list

Write down people and concerns you want to remember. Include your church, family, friends, leaders, missionaries, those who are sick, and those who need the gospel. Review the list regularly, but allow room for immediate needs and spontaneous prayer.

5. Include praise and thanksgiving

Do not let every prayer become a list of requests. Remember who God is and what he has done. Thank him for the gospel, for daily mercies, for answered prayers, and for his faithfulness when circumstances remain hard.

6. Confess sin and seek practical obedience

When Scripture exposes sin, confess it and take the steps of obedience that are needed. If you have wronged someone, seek to make it right where possible. If you are caught in a pattern of sin, seek help from trustworthy mature Christians. Prayer should not become a way of avoiding the actions God commands.

7. Persevere without pretending

Jesus taught his disciples to pray and not lose heart (Luke 18:1–8). Perseverance does not mean repeating words mechanically or believing that enough repetition forces God's hand. It means continuing to depend on him, even when the answer is delayed and our feelings change.

8. Pray for others beyond your immediate circle

Remember the church throughout the world, those who proclaim the gospel, those suffering persecution, civil authorities, and people with whom you disagree. Ask God to make you more concerned with his glory and the good of others.

What if you do not know what to say?

There will be moments when words seem inadequate. Grief can leave a person silent. Anxiety can make thoughts feel scattered. A sense of guilt can make someone afraid to approach God. Scripture does not tell us to wait until we can produce a perfect prayer.

Read a psalm aloud. Pray the Lord's Prayer slowly. Repeat a biblical promise while considering its meaning. Ask a trusted Christian to pray with you. You may simply say, “Father, help me.” The God who knows the heart is not dependent on our ability to express ourselves elegantly.

Romans 8:26–27 is especially precious in such moments: the Spirit helps believers in their weakness. Our hope is not that we will always pray perfectly, but that God is gracious and that our access to him rests in Christ.

Misunderstandings about prayer to avoid

“If I pray with enough faith, God must give me what I ask.”

Faith is not confidence in our ability to obtain a desired outcome. It is trust in God himself. Biblical faith takes God's promises seriously, but it does not turn our wishes into promises God never made. We ask boldly and submit to his wisdom.

“Long prayers are always better.”

Jesus warns against empty repetition and prayer intended to impress (Matthew 6:7). Scripture also records extended prayers. The issue is not simply length, but whether we are sincerely addressing God. A short prayer can be faithful; a long prayer can be sincere or self-serving.

“If I struggle to pray, God must be rejecting me.”

Weakness in prayer is not proof that God has abandoned a believer. Christians can become distracted, weary, or discouraged. The answer is not to trust our performance but to return to the Lord, use the help he has provided, and keep seeking him.

“Prayer replaces responsible action.”

Prayer and obedience belong together. We may pray for daily bread while working honestly, pray for reconciliation while taking steps toward peace, and pray for the spread of the gospel while faithfully bearing witness. Dependence on God is not an excuse for laziness.

“I must use special words or a particular posture.”

The Bible shows people praying while standing, kneeling, sitting, and even lying prostrate. Posture can express reverence, but no physical position or special phrase earns God's attention. What matters is coming to him through Christ with a sincere heart shaped by his Word.

A simple biblical pattern for prayer

The following is not a formula that must be repeated word for word. It is a practical outline drawn from the priorities Scripture teaches:

  1. Worship: Father, you are holy, wise, powerful, merciful, and faithful. Help me honour your name today.
  2. Submission: Let your kingdom advance and your will be done. Correct my desires where they do not agree with your Word.
  3. Dependence: Provide what I need for today. Give me wisdom for my responsibilities and strength to serve faithfully.
  4. Confession: Show me where I have sinned. Forgive me through Christ and help me turn from what dishonours you.
  5. Intercession: Please care for my family, church, neighbours, leaders, those who suffer, and those who do not yet know Christ.
  6. Perseverance: Keep me from temptation, strengthen me in weakness, and help me trust you whatever this day brings.

Pray with the freedom Christ has secured, and let Scripture continually correct and deepen what you ask.

Come to the Father through Christ

Prayer is not a performance for people, a payment for sin, or a means of taking control of God. It is the gracious privilege of coming to the Father through Jesus Christ, by the help of the Holy Spirit. We come because God has spoken, because Christ has opened the way, and because the Father welcomes those who belong to his Son.

So pray when you are joyful, and pray when you are grieving. Pray when you understand what is happening, and when you do not. Give thanks for what God provides. Confess the sins he exposes. Ask for help with ordinary needs and extraordinary burdens. Pray for your church, your neighbours, your enemies, and the advance of the gospel. Keep coming—not because your words are powerful in themselves, but because the God who hears is faithful.

And if you have never trusted Christ, do not mistake prayer itself for reconciliation with God. Your deepest need is to be made right with him. Jesus Christ came to save sinners. He died and rose again, and all who trust in him are forgiven and welcomed by God. Come to Christ. The access Christians enjoy in prayer is the gift of the Saviour who brings his people near.

Passages for further study

  • Jesus' teaching on prayer: Matthew 6:5–13; Luke 11:1–13; Luke 18:1–8.
  • Access to God through Christ: Ephesians 2:11–22; Hebrews 4:14–16; Hebrews 7:23–28; 1 Timothy 2:1–6.
  • The Spirit's help: Romans 8:14–27; Galatians 4:4–7.
  • Prayer and thanksgiving: Philippians 4:4–7; Colossians 4:2–4; 1 Thessalonians 5:16–18.
  • Confession and forgiveness: Psalm 32; Psalm 51; 1 John 1:5–2:2.
  • Prayer in suffering: Psalms 13, 42, and 77; 2 Corinthians 12:7–10; James 5:13–18.
  • Prayer with the church: Acts 2:42; Acts 4:23–31; 1 Timothy 2:1–4; Ephesians 6:18–20.

Questions for reflection: Do I approach God on the basis of Christ's finished work or my own performance? Do my prayers reflect the priorities Jesus taught? Am I willing to bring both gratitude and grief honestly before God? Who needs my prayers beyond my own household? What would it look like to trust God's wisdom when his answer is not the one I hoped for?

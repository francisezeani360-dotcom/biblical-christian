Christian apologetics · The resurrection of Jesus

Did Jesus Really Rise from the Dead?

An examination of the historical testimony, the empty tomb, the resurrection appearances, the earliest Christian proclamation, and the significance of the risen Christ.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

The resurrection of Jesus Christ is the foundation of the Christian faith. The claim is extraordinary: the man who was publicly crucified, died, and was buried was raised bodily by God on the third day. Christians do not mean merely that Jesus' influence survived his death or that his disciples found comfort in remembering him. They mean that Jesus himself rose from the dead, never to die again.

Because this claim is central to Christianity, it deserves more than a slogan or a dismissive response. We should examine what the earliest sources say, consider the historical setting, distinguish what is well established from what is debated, and ask whether the resurrection best explains the testimony. The Christian case does not depend on pretending that every detail is beyond discussion. It rests on the conviction that the evidence, read honestly and alongside the biblical witness, points to the action of God in raising his Son.

This study considers the historical claims and then turns to the deeper meaning of the resurrection. The aim is not simply to win an argument, but to understand why the apostles proclaimed that Jesus is alive—and why their message still demands a response.

A note on historical method: Historical sources can be examined for their date, context, testimony, and explanatory power. The resurrection, if it happened, was an act of God—not an ordinary event that can be repeated in a laboratory. We should neither claim more than the sources establish nor rule out a miracle before considering the evidence.

1. The resurrection is the heart of Christianity

Christianity stands or falls on a claim about something that happened in history: Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified and buried, was bodily raised from the dead on the third day. The first Christians did not merely say that Jesus' influence continued, that his teaching remained inspiring, or that his spirit lived on in their memories. They announced that the crucified Jesus was alive again.

Paul makes the importance of this claim unmistakable. In 1 Corinthians 15:14–19 he says that if Christ has not been raised, Christian preaching is empty, faith is futile, believers remain in their sins, and those who have died trusting Christ have perished. The resurrection is not an optional detail that can be removed while leaving the Christian faith intact. It is essential to the gospel.

That is why the apostles repeatedly proclaimed it. Peter declared that God raised Jesus, whom men had crucified (Acts 2:22–36). The apostles testified to the resurrection with great power (Acts 4:33). Paul told the Athenians that God had appointed a day of judgment and given assurance of this by raising Jesus from the dead (Acts 17:30–31).

The question deserves serious attention. Did Jesus really rise? What reasons do Christians have for believing this? What can historical investigation establish, and where does the Christian claim go beyond the limits of historical method? We should not be afraid of those questions. The New Testament itself presents the resurrection as an event proclaimed in public, connected to identifiable people and places, and offered as the foundation of Christian hope.

2. Jesus really died by crucifixion

The resurrection claim begins with the death of Jesus. If he did not truly die, there was no resurrection from the dead. The New Testament accounts describe his arrest, condemnation, scourging, crucifixion, death, and burial (Matthew 27; Mark 15; Luke 23; John 19). The Gospels do not portray the cross as an apparent death or a narrow escape. Jesus was executed.

This is not a detail found only in Christian writings. The Roman historian Tacitus, writing in the early second century, says that Christus—the figure from whom Christians received their name—suffered the death penalty under Pontius Pilate during the reign of Tiberius. Tacitus was no Christian apologist; he viewed the Christian movement with contempt. His brief notice is valuable as non-Christian testimony that Jesus was executed under Pilate. You can read the relevant passage in Tacitus, Annals 15.44.

The historian Josephus also contains a passage about Jesus in Jewish Antiquities 18.63–64, commonly called the Testimonium Flavianum. Its wording has been debated because the surviving text includes statements that sound like a Christian confession. Scholars have proposed different explanations: that Josephus wrote the passage substantially as transmitted, that Christian copyists modified it, or that the passage was more extensively altered. It should therefore be used with care, not presented as an uncontested independent proof of the resurrection. The debate is outlined in the Posen Library's discussion of the Testimonium Flavianum.

The historical point is modest but important: Jesus' execution under Roman authority is not merely a late Christian invention. The earliest Christian proclamation and non-Christian historical references agree that Jesus was put to death. The resurrection claim is that the one who truly died was raised by God.

3. The resurrection message was proclaimed very early

One of the most important passages for understanding the earliest Christian belief is 1 Corinthians 15:3–8. Paul reminds the church of the message he had received and passed on: Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, was buried, was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and appeared to Cephas, then to the Twelve, and to many others.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians in the middle of the first century, within a few decades of Jesus' death. But the tradition he quotes is older than the letter. He says he had received it and then delivered it to the Corinthians. The wording, structure, and list of appearances have led many scholars to recognise that Paul is preserving an earlier confession of Christian faith. The exact origin and boundaries of the formula are discussed, but it is widely treated as an early tradition rather than a story first invented generations later. James Ware's scholarly study examines the debated details of the formula in “The Resurrection of Jesus in the Pre-Pauline Formula of 1 Cor 15.3–5”.

This matters because it places resurrection belief close to the beginning of the Christian movement. The claim was not slowly added after the original followers had forgotten what happened. The earliest Christians were already proclaiming that Jesus had died, been buried, been raised, and appeared to witnesses.

Paul also names people and groups. Cephas (Peter) is named; the Twelve are named as a group; more than five hundred are mentioned; James is named; and Paul includes himself. Paul says that many of the five hundred were still alive when he wrote. His point is that the proclamation was connected to people who could, in principle, be questioned. This does not mean that every appearance can now be independently verified, but it does show that the earliest message was presented as testimony to encounters, not simply as a timeless religious idea.

4. The empty tomb and the burial of Jesus

The Gospel accounts report that Jesus was buried and that his tomb was found empty. Joseph of Arimathea, a member of the council, is named as the man who arranged Jesus' burial (Mark 15:42–47; Matthew 27:57–61; Luke 23:50–56; John 19:38–42). The women who had followed Jesus are also described as observing where he was laid. On the first day of the week, women came to the tomb and found that the stone had been moved and Jesus' body was no longer there (Mark 16:1–8; Luke 24:1–12; John 20:1–10).

The empty tomb is significant because the apostles proclaimed a bodily resurrection in the very place where Jesus had been executed and buried. Their message was not that Jesus had merely survived in the hearts of his followers. They claimed that God had raised him. The resurrection body was transformed and glorified, but it was not simply an apparition: the risen Jesus could be seen, touched, and recognised, and he ate with his disciples (Luke 24:36–43; John 20:24–29; 21:9–14).

The Gospel accounts differ in some details of the visits to the tomb: they name different women, describe events with different emphases, and do not all narrate every detail in the same order. These differences deserve careful reading. They should not be dismissed with the claim that every account says precisely the same thing. At the same time, differences in selection and arrangement do not by themselves establish that the central claim is false. Historical witnesses can describe the same event from different perspectives without giving identical narratives.

It is also important to be precise about what the empty tomb can prove. An empty tomb, considered by itself, does not explain why the tomb was empty. The Christian argument does not rest on the emptiness alone. It considers the burial and empty-tomb testimony together with the early resurrection proclamation and the reported appearances of Jesus.

5. The disciples believed they encountered the risen Jesus

The New Testament repeatedly reports that Jesus appeared to his followers after his death. The accounts include Mary Magdalene (John 20:11–18), the women returning from the tomb (Matthew 28:8–10), two disciples on the road to Emmaus (Luke 24:13–35), Peter (Luke 24:34; 1 Corinthians 15:5), the gathered disciples (Luke 24:36–49; John 20:19–23), Thomas (John 20:24–29), and a group of disciples in Galilee (John 21).

The accounts emphasise both continuity and transformation. The risen Jesus is the same Jesus who was crucified: he speaks with his followers and shows them his wounds. Yet he is not simply restored to ordinary mortal life, as though he were returning to the same condition he had before. His resurrection is the beginning of the new creation and the pattern of the future resurrection of his people (1 Corinthians 15:20–23, 42–49).

The disciples' conviction is historically important even for someone who does not accept their conclusion. Something happened that persuaded these followers that Jesus was alive and that they had encountered him. The earliest sources do not portray them as people who expected a resurrection and simply imagined what they hoped to see. The crucifixion had shattered their expectations (Luke 24:17–21; John 20:19). The resurrection appearances, as they understood them, changed their message and their lives.

It is sometimes said that the disciples were willing to die for what they believed, and therefore their testimony must be true. That argument needs care. People in many religions have died for beliefs that cannot all be true. Sincerity alone does not prove a claim. But the willingness of the earliest witnesses to suffer for their testimony is relevant to whether they were knowingly promoting a story they had invented. The evidence supports the conclusion that they sincerely believed their proclamation. The Christian case then asks what best explains that conviction together with the other evidence.

6. The transformation of the first witnesses

Before the crucifixion, the disciples are shown as confused, fearful, and often slow to understand Jesus. At his arrest they fled (Mark 14:50). Peter denied knowing him (Mark 14:66–72). After the crucifixion, the disciples gathered behind locked doors because they were afraid (John 20:19). Yet soon they were publicly announcing in Jerusalem that the man who had been crucified had been raised by God (Acts 2:22–36; 3:12–16; 4:18–20).

A change in conviction does not by itself prove a miracle. People can become courageous for many reasons. But the change invites an explanation. The disciples did not merely continue teaching that Jesus was a good teacher whose cause should survive his death. They proclaimed that God had vindicated the crucified Messiah by raising him bodily. Their confidence was tied to what they believed they had seen and heard.

Paul's own conversion is another part of the early Christian story. He had opposed the church and sought to persecute its members (Galatians 1:13–14; Acts 8:1–3; 9:1–19). He later became one of the movement's most influential missionaries, explaining that he had seen the risen Christ (1 Corinthians 15:8–10). James, the brother of Jesus, is also named among those to whom the risen Jesus appeared (1 Corinthians 15:7); elsewhere the New Testament indicates that Jesus' brothers had not initially believed in him (John 7:5; compare Acts 1:14).

These accounts are Christian sources and must be read with historical judgement. Still, they provide evidence that the resurrection belief was not confined to one individual or one kind of experience. The early movement included former opponents and members of Jesus' own family, as well as his first disciples. The resurrection proclamation became the centre of their public message.

7. Do alternative explanations account for the evidence?

A serious case should not pretend that questions have never been raised. Several alternative explanations have been proposed. Each should be considered according to how well it accounts for the evidence, rather than rejected merely because it is not Christian.

“The disciples stole the body.” This proposal appears in Matthew 28:11–15 as an accusation circulated by the authorities. It would explain an empty tomb if the disciples had taken the body, but it does not readily explain why the disciples came to believe that Jesus had appeared to them, or why the resurrection proclamation included experiences attributed to individuals and groups. It also requires evidence that the disciples carried out a deception for which the sources provide no convincing support. The proposal is possible to state, but it is not a complete explanation of the testimony.

“Jesus did not really die.” Sometimes called the swoon theory, this suggestion says Jesus survived crucifixion and later appeared to his followers. It faces the problem that crucifixion was a brutal Roman execution, and the sources describe Jesus as dead and buried. A severely wounded survivor would also not naturally explain the disciples' conviction that he had conquered death and entered transformed resurrection life. This theory does not fit the evidence well.

“The disciples had visions or hallucinations.” Grief can involve vivid experiences of the deceased, and human perception is not infallible. It is therefore too simplistic to say that hallucinations are impossible. But a general appeal to hallucination does not explain all the features of the accounts: the variety of reported appearances, the claims involving groups, the empty-tomb tradition, and the early proclamation of bodily resurrection. Modern psychological categories also cannot simply be applied to ancient testimony as though we had examined the witnesses ourselves. The proposal needs to explain the whole pattern, not only the possibility of private experiences.

“The resurrection developed as a legend over time.” Religious stories can develop, but the early tradition in 1 Corinthians 15 places the resurrection proclamation very close to the beginning of the movement. The core claim that Jesus died, was buried, was raised, and appeared to witnesses was not first recorded centuries later. This does not settle every question about how each Gospel account developed, but it makes a long, slow invention of the central resurrection belief difficult to maintain.

“The disciples sincerely believed, but were mistaken.” This is more careful than accusing them of deliberate fraud. It recognises that sincerity does not guarantee accuracy. Yet it still must account for what the disciples believed they encountered, the empty-tomb testimony, the early confession, and the inclusion of people such as Paul, who had opposed the movement. It may describe a possible category of explanation, but simply saying “they were mistaken” does not show how the mistake arose or why it took this particular form.

No historical argument should claim that every alternative has been disproved beyond all possible doubt. The question is which explanation best accounts for the evidence. Christians argue that the resurrection explains the convergence of Jesus' death, the early proclamation, the empty-tomb testimony, the reported appearances, and the transformation of the witnesses more coherently than the alternatives. The decisive Christian claim is that God raised Jesus; it is not merely that no one has yet proposed another theory.

8. What can historical investigation establish?

Historical investigation works with sources, testimony, context, and explanations. It can examine whether Jesus lived, whether he was crucified, how early Christians described their experiences, how soon the resurrection message appeared, and how the movement developed. It can assess the reliability and limitations of particular sources. It cannot place God in a laboratory or repeat the resurrection as a controlled experiment.

That limitation cuts both ways. A historian should not claim that historical method has scientifically demonstrated the resurrection in the way one demonstrates a chemical reaction. But neither should someone assume in advance that God cannot act in history and then rule out the resurrection before examining the evidence. That would be a philosophical commitment, not a neutral historical conclusion.

The Christian claim is that the resurrection was an act of God. If God exists and has made himself known, a miracle is not logically impossible. Whether one believes the resurrection occurred depends not only on how one assesses the sources, but also on whether one is willing to consider the reality and action of God. Historical evidence matters, but our deepest assumptions about the world also shape how we interpret it.

The apostles did not present the resurrection as a private symbol meaningful only to believers. They proclaimed it as God's public vindication of Jesus. Their message invited examination and demanded a response (Acts 2:32; 17:31).

9. Why the resurrection matters

The resurrection is not simply proof that Jesus was an extraordinary person. It is God's declaration that the crucified Jesus is Lord and Christ (Acts 2:32–36). It confirms that his death was not a defeat and that his saving work has not failed.

It confirms Christ's victory over sin. Paul connects Jesus' death and resurrection directly to justification: Christ “was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification” (Romans 4:25). The resurrection does not add a second payment to the cross. It is God's vindication of the Son and the public confirmation that the crucified Saviour has triumphed. The risen Christ is the living Lord who saves all who come to God through him (Hebrews 7:25).

It guarantees the future resurrection of believers. Paul calls Christ “the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep” (1 Corinthians 15:20). Firstfruits are the beginning and pledge of the harvest to come. Because Christ has been raised, death will not have the final word over those who belong to him. The Christian hope is not merely that the soul survives, but that God will raise his people bodily and make them fit for the life of the age to come (1 Corinthians 15:42–57; Philippians 3:20–21).

It establishes Jesus as the coming Judge. The risen Jesus has been appointed by God as judge of the living and the dead (Acts 10:42; 17:31). The resurrection is therefore not only comforting; it is a summons. Every person must reckon with the authority of the risen Christ.

It gives meaning to Christian obedience and suffering. Because Jesus lives, labour in the Lord is not in vain (1 Corinthians 15:58). Christians do not follow a dead hero whose ideals they try to preserve. They belong to a living Lord who reigns, intercedes for his people, and will return (Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25; Acts 1:9–11).

10. The risen Christ and the good news

The resurrection cannot be separated from the cross. Jesus did not die merely as an example of courage or as a victim of injustice. He died for the sins of his people, bearing judgment in their place (Isaiah 53:4–6; Mark 10:45; 1 Peter 2:24; 3:18). He was buried, and on the third day he was raised. This is the gospel announced by the apostles (1 Corinthians 15:1–4).

Our problem is deeper than ignorance or a lack of religious activity. We have sinned against our Creator. We have broken his commandments in thought, word, and deed, and we cannot make ourselves righteous by good works, religious ceremonies, or promises to do better (Romans 3:10–23; Ephesians 2:1–3). We deserve God's just judgment.

But God, in mercy, sent his Son. Jesus Christ is truly God and truly man. He lived without sin, obeyed the Father perfectly, and willingly gave himself on the cross as the sacrifice for sin. He was raised bodily, and he now reigns. Salvation is not earned by our efforts. It is God's gift, received through faith in Jesus Christ (Romans 3:21–26; Ephesians 2:8–10).

The call of the gospel is to repent and believe in Christ (Mark 1:15; Acts 17:30). Repentance is not a payment for sin, nor is it an attempt to make ourselves worthy before coming to Jesus. It is a change of mind and heart that turns from sin and false confidence to God. Faith is not confidence in our own goodness; it is resting upon Christ alone and receiving him as he is offered in the gospel. Good works follow as the fruit of genuine faith, not as the price of acceptance with God (Ephesians 2:8–10; James 2:14–26).

The risen Jesus is not merely an interesting subject for debate. He is the Lord who commands all people everywhere to repent and the Saviour who promises life to all who trust in him. Do not put your confidence in your morality, religious background, knowledge, or sincerity. Come to Christ. His death is sufficient for sinners, and he lives for ever.

11. A conclusion: the question demands an answer

The evidence should be considered honestly. Jesus was crucified under Pontius Pilate. The earliest Christians proclaimed his resurrection very soon after his death. The New Testament preserves an early confession that Jesus died, was buried, was raised, and appeared to witnesses. The Gospel accounts report an empty tomb and encounters with the risen Jesus. The first witnesses became convinced that God had raised him, and that conviction shaped the message and mission of the early church.

These facts do not force every reader to accept the Christian conclusion. People interpret historical evidence through broader convictions about God, miracles, and the reliability of testimony. But the resurrection should not be dismissed as though it were a late legend with no early roots, or as though the apostles merely meant that Jesus' influence continued. They claimed that the crucified Jesus had been raised bodily.

The Christian confession is clear: Jesus Christ is risen. He is not in the grave. He is the living Lord, the Saviour of sinners, the firstfruits of the resurrection, and the coming Judge of the world. The proper response is not simply to admire the courage of the first Christians, but to hear their message, turn to God, and trust the risen Christ.

Read the accounts for yourself. Consider the claims in context. Ask whether the evidence is best explained by the resurrection. Above all, do not treat Jesus as though he were only a figure from the past. The claim of the gospel is that he lives today.

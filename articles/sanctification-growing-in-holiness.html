Sanctification: Growing in Holiness

What does it mean to become more like Christ? A careful biblical study of God's work in his people, the Spirit's power, the fight against sin, and a life of growing obedience.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian
The central truth. Sanctification is God's gracious work by which those who belong to Christ are set apart for him and increasingly made holy in heart and life. God works in believers by his Spirit, and believers therefore actively pursue obedience. We do not become accepted by God through spiritual progress; because we have been accepted in Christ, we are called and enabled to grow in holiness.

A person becomes a Christian by the grace of God, through faith in Jesus Christ. But what happens after that? Does conversion mean that a believer immediately stops struggling with sin? Does Christian growth happen automatically? Is holiness mainly a matter of trying harder, following religious rules, or having a powerful spiritual experience?

“For this is the will of God, your sanctification.”1 Thessalonians 4:3 — reference for study

These questions touch the ordinary experience of every Christian. We know that we should love God and our neighbour, resist temptation, forgive those who wrong us, speak truthfully, and live with purity. Yet we also know the frustration of repeated failures, selfish motives, weak faith, and desires that do not honour God. Sometimes growth seems slow. At other times, we may mistake outward religious activity for genuine spiritual maturity.

The Bible does not leave us to guess what the Christian life is meant to be. It teaches that salvation is entirely of grace, that those who are united to Christ are given new life, and that the Holy Spirit works in them so that they increasingly reflect the character of their Saviour. This work is called sanctification. It is not the price we pay for salvation, but one of the gracious results of belonging to Christ.

To understand sanctification properly, we must keep the whole work of salvation in view. We need to distinguish justification from sanctification without separating them, recognise both God's work and our responsibility, and learn what Scripture says about the daily struggle against sin.

1. What is sanctification?

The word sanctification is related to the biblical idea of holiness. To sanctify something is to set it apart as holy, belonging to God and devoted to his purposes. In Scripture, holiness is not simply a religious feeling or a reputation for being respectable. God himself is holy: perfectly pure, righteous, and distinct from all evil. His people are called to belong to him and to reflect his character.

Sanctification has both a decisive and an ongoing aspect. When a person is brought to faith in Christ, that person is set apart as belonging to God. The New Testament can therefore speak of believers as sanctified people (1 Corinthians 1:2; 6:11; Hebrews 10:10). At the same time, Christians are repeatedly commanded to pursue holiness and are described as being transformed (2 Corinthians 3:18; 1 Thessalonians 4:3; Hebrews 12:14). The Bible is not contradicting itself. It is speaking of a real change of status and belonging, and also of the continuing transformation of a believer's life.

In this study, when we speak of growing in sanctification, we are referring especially to that ongoing work. God progressively renews believers in the whole person—mind, affections, desires, words, choices, and conduct—so that they increasingly hate what is evil and love what is good. This growth is real, but it is not complete in this life.

Holiness is not merely avoiding a list of forbidden actions. It includes love for God, delight in his truth, compassion for others, humility, honesty, patience, sexual purity, contentment, courage, forgiveness, and faithfulness. It concerns who we are becoming, not only what we are seen doing.

2. Sanctification and the whole of salvation

It is helpful to place sanctification alongside the other parts of God's saving work. Scripture speaks of God's gracious purpose, Christ's redeeming work, the Spirit's application of salvation, the believer's justification and adoption, the new life of regeneration, sanctification, perseverance, and final glorification. These are not competing ways of being saved. They are distinguishable aspects of the one salvation God gives in Christ.

In justification, God declares the believer righteous on the basis of Christ's righteousness, received through faith. In adoption, God welcomes believers as his children. In regeneration, the Spirit gives new life. In sanctification, God renews those who belong to Christ and teaches them to live as his people. In glorification, he will finally and completely remove sin and bring his people into the fullness of life with him.

These benefits must not be confused, but neither should they be pulled apart. The person whom God justifies, he also begins to sanctify. The Christian's obedience does not purchase God's acceptance, but God's saving grace does not leave a person content to remain in rebellion against him.

Paul brings together God's saving mercy and the new life it produces in Ephesians 2:8–10. We are saved by grace through faith, not as a result of works; yet we are also created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand for us to walk in. Good works are not the root of salvation. They are its fruit.

3. Justification is not sanctification

Justification and sanctification are inseparable in the Christian life, but they are not the same thing. Confusing them can damage the way we understand both the gospel and our growth in holiness.

Justification is God's judicial declaration that a sinner is righteous in his sight because of Jesus Christ. It is grounded in Christ's obedience and atoning death, not in the moral progress of the believer. It is received through faith, not earned by a gradual improvement in character. A Christian's standing before God rests on Christ, not on a spiritual performance review.

Sanctification, by contrast, is God's work of making the believer holy in actual life. It involves renewal and transformation. Unlike justification, which is a definitive declaration, progressive sanctification unfolds throughout the Christian life and varies in visible pace and maturity among believers.

This distinction protects the believer from two opposite errors. The first is legalism: imagining that God accepts us because we have become sufficiently obedient. The second is antinomianism: imagining that because we are justified by grace, holiness and obedience do not matter. The gospel rejects both. We are not justified by our works, but the faith that receives Christ does not make peace with sin.

When a Christian sins, the answer is not to try to earn back justification by accumulating good deeds. The answer is to confess sin, turn again to God, remember the sufficiency of Christ, and walk in the obedience to which grace calls us (1 John 1:8–2:2; Romans 8:1–4).

4. United to Christ

The Christian life cannot be understood apart from union with Jesus Christ. Salvation is not merely receiving a collection of benefits from a distance. By faith, believers belong to Christ. His death and resurrection define their new identity and the new life they are called to live.

In Romans 6, Paul answers the suggestion that grace gives people permission to continue in sin. His answer is emphatic: those who have been united to Christ have been united to his death and resurrection. They are no longer to present themselves to sin as though it were their master. They are to consider themselves dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus (Romans 6:1–14).

Paul is not saying that Christians never sin again. The rest of the New Testament would make that impossible to claim. He is saying that sin no longer has rightful dominion over those who belong to Christ. The believer has entered a new realm of life and a new relationship to sin. We must not treat what Christ has broken as though it still owns us.

Jesus describes himself as the vine and his disciples as branches. A branch bears fruit by remaining in the vine; it does not produce life independently (John 15:1–8). The image teaches dependence, not passivity. Apart from Christ we can do nothing, yet those who abide in him are called to bear much fruit. Christian growth comes from life in Christ, not self-sufficient moral effort.

This is why the gospel must remain central in sanctification. We do not move beyond Christ once we become Christians. We continue to depend on him, trust his promises, receive his Word, and follow him.

5. The work of the Holy Spirit

Sanctification is not a project we carry out by natural strength. The Holy Spirit, who gives new life, also works in believers to renew them and produce godly character. Scripture calls him the Spirit of holiness and describes the fruit he produces in those who belong to Christ.

Galatians 5 contrasts the works of the flesh with the fruit of the Spirit. The works of the flesh include sexual immorality, jealousy, fits of anger, rivalries, envy, and other expressions of life ruled by sinful desire. The fruit of the Spirit includes love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control (Galatians 5:16–23). These qualities are not a personality type or a natural temperament. They are the evidence of the Spirit's transforming work.

To walk by the Spirit is not to abandon careful thought, moral responsibility, or the written Word. The Spirit who inspired Scripture does not lead believers into conduct that contradicts it. His work produces love for God's truth and a growing willingness to obey it. We should therefore be wary of claims that a private impression, unusual experience, or supposed spiritual revelation gives permission to disregard what God has clearly commanded.

The Spirit also helps believers in weakness. He intercedes for God's people according to God's will (Romans 8:26–27), assures them of their adoption (Romans 8:15–16), and empowers them to put sinful deeds to death (Romans 8:12–13). We are dependent on him from beginning to end.

6. God works, and believers work

One of the clearest statements about sanctification is Philippians 2:12–13. Paul tells Christians to work out their salvation with fear and trembling, because God is at work in them, both to will and to work for his good pleasure. The two statements belong together. Because God is at work, believers are to act. God's gracious activity is the foundation of Christian effort, not a reason to neglect it.

Working out salvation does not mean working to earn salvation. Paul is writing to people who already belong to Christ. He calls them to live out the implications of the salvation God has given. The Christian does not sit back and wait for holiness to happen without effort; neither does the Christian imagine that personal effort can create spiritual life apart from grace.

We read Scripture, pray, resist temptation, seek forgiveness, serve others, and practise obedience. Yet even our willingness and ability to do these things depend on God's sustaining grace. This should produce humility rather than pride. When we grow, we give thanks to God. When we fail, we do not despair as though his grace has disappeared; we repent and seek his help again.

There is a mystery here, but not a contradiction. God is sovereign in sanctification, and believers are genuinely responsible. Scripture teaches both without embarrassment. We should not use God's sovereignty as an excuse for laziness or human responsibility as a reason for boasting.

7. The place of God's Word

God uses his Word to teach, correct, expose, encourage, and train his people. Jesus prayed that the Father would sanctify his disciples in the truth, adding, “your word is truth” (John 17:17). The Bible does not merely give information about holiness. Through the Spirit, it shapes our understanding of God, reveals our sin, directs our choices, and holds out the promises by which we learn to live.

We cannot grow in holiness while deliberately neglecting the truth that shows us what holiness is. This does not mean that reading a certain number of chapters automatically makes someone mature. We can read quickly, forget what we have read, or use biblical knowledge to feed pride. The goal is to receive the Word with faith, understand it in context, and respond with obedience.

Regular, thoughtful reading is more valuable than occasional bursts of religious enthusiasm. Read whole passages rather than collecting isolated phrases. Consider what the text teaches about God, humanity, sin, Christ, and the life of faith. Pray for understanding, discuss difficult passages with mature Christians, and allow Scripture to challenge assumptions rather than merely confirm them.

Memorising Scripture can also help. When temptation comes, God's truth can correct deceptive thoughts and remind us of what is good. Psalm 119 repeatedly connects the Word with a life kept from sin. The point is not to treat verses as magical formulas, but to let God's truth dwell richly in us.

8. The ordinary means of growth

Christians sometimes look for a hidden technique or dramatic experience that will make growth easy. Scripture instead directs us to ordinary means through which God nourishes his people. These are sometimes called the means of grace: the Word, prayer, the fellowship and worship of the church, and the ordinances Christ gave to his people.

The Word preached and read

God gathers and builds his people through the public reading and preaching of Scripture as well as through personal reading and study. Faithful preaching explains and applies the biblical text, directing hearers to God and his grace rather than entertaining them or flattering their preferences (2 Timothy 4:1–5; Romans 10:14–17).

Prayer

Prayer is not a way of earning spiritual maturity. It is the child's dependence on the Father through the Son. We confess sin, ask for wisdom and strength, give thanks, intercede for others, and learn to bring our desires under God's will. Jesus taught his disciples to pray, and the apostles repeatedly urged believers to continue in prayer (Matthew 6:9–13; Colossians 4:2).

The gathered church

God did not design Christians to mature in isolation. Believers need teaching, encouragement, correction, accountability, mutual service, and the shared worship of the church. We are commanded to stir one another up to love and good works and not to neglect meeting together (Hebrews 10:24–25).

Baptism and the Lord's Supper

Christ gave baptism and the Lord's Supper to his church. They do not earn salvation or work as mechanical rituals. Baptism publicly identifies a believer with Christ and his people. The Lord's Supper is a continuing ordinance in which the church remembers and proclaims Christ's death, examines itself, and shares in a visible expression of fellowship in him (Matthew 28:19–20; 1 Corinthians 11:23–29). These ordinances direct our faith to Christ and remind us that the Christian life is lived as members of his body.

None of these means should be treated as a shortcut that guarantees instant maturity. Their value is not in ritual performance but in God's appointed use of them as his people receive his truth in faith.

9. The continuing struggle with sin

Why do Christians still struggle with sin if they have been made new? The Bible's answer is realistic. Believers truly belong to Christ and have received new life, yet they have not reached perfection. They still experience the conflict between the desires of the flesh and the desires of the Spirit (Galatians 5:16–17).

Romans 7 is often discussed in this connection, and Christians differ over the precise identity and experience of the person Paul describes in that chapter. Whatever conclusion one reaches about that debated passage, the wider New Testament is clear: Christians must resist sin, confess their failures, and depend on the Spirit. First John warns against claiming to be without sin, while also insisting that those who know Christ must not make a settled practice of lawlessness (1 John 1:8–2:6).

There is a difference between struggling against sin and making peace with it. A believer may fall seriously, sometimes repeatedly, and need patient help and correction. But the direction of the Christian life is no longer one of willing surrender to sin as master. Grace teaches us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives (Titus 2:11–14).

We should not measure spiritual health only by how easy temptation feels. Some believers face persistent and painful struggles. Nor should we excuse sin because a struggle is difficult. We must bring sin into the light, seek help where needed, and keep turning to Christ.

10. Putting sin to death

The New Testament uses strong language for the Christian response to sin. Believers are told to put to death what is earthly in them (Colossians 3:5) and, by the Spirit, to put to death the deeds of the body (Romans 8:13). This is not self-hatred or an attempt to punish ourselves into holiness. It is a decisive refusal to nourish what God calls evil.

Putting sin to death begins with honest recognition. We must stop renaming greed as ambition, bitterness as strength, lust as harmless curiosity, or pride as confidence. God's Word teaches us to call sin what it is. We then confess it to God, turn from it, and take practical steps to avoid feeding it.

For example, someone who repeatedly falls into a particular temptation may need to change patterns of internet use, avoid compromising situations, set wise boundaries, seek accountability, and ask trusted believers for help. Someone who struggles with anger may need to identify the fears and desires beneath angry reactions, learn to listen, make restitution, and practise patient speech. The practical steps do not replace the heart's need for grace; they are part of taking obedience seriously.

Putting sin to death also involves replacing sinful habits with godly ones. Scripture does not merely say, “Stop stealing”; it says that the thief should work honestly and have something to share with anyone in need (Ephesians 4:28). We put away falsehood and speak truth. We put away corrupt speech and speak what builds others up. We put away bitterness and practise kindness and forgiveness (Ephesians 4:25–32).

Do not confuse this work with earning God's favour. We fight sin because we belong to Christ and because sin dishonours the God who has shown us mercy. We fight with seriousness, but not as people who have no hope.

11. The fruit of a changed life

Jesus taught that a tree is known by its fruit (Matthew 7:15–20). The fruit does not make the tree alive; it reveals the kind of tree it is. In the same way, good works do not purchase salvation, but a changed life gives evidence of living faith.

The fruit of sanctification is broader than public ministry, religious vocabulary, visible confidence, or knowledge of theological terms. It includes the ordinary conduct of life: truthfulness when lying would be easier, faithfulness in marriage, patience with children, generosity toward the poor, purity when no one is watching, humility when corrected, and forgiveness when wronged.

Love is central. The New Testament repeatedly joins love for God with love for neighbour. A person may speak impressively about doctrine and still behave with cruelty, arrogance, or contempt. Biblical truth should deepen love, not become a weapon for self-importance (1 Corinthians 13:1–7; James 3:13–18).

Growth also includes increasing discernment. Christians learn to recognise what pleases the Lord, test their desires, and distinguish what is good from what is harmful (Romans 12:1–2; Hebrews 5:14). Maturity is not merely knowing more facts, although sound knowledge matters. It is learning to think, desire, and act in ways shaped by God's truth.

Fruit takes time to grow. A young believer may show real signs of grace while still needing instruction in many areas. A mature Christian may be strong in one area and still need correction in another. We should look for genuine direction and fruit without pretending that all Christians mature at the same pace.

12. Grace, obedience, and God's commands

Some people assume that emphasising grace weakens the call to obedience. Others speak about holiness in a way that makes the Christian life sound like an attempt to gain God's acceptance through rule-keeping. Both approaches misunderstand the relationship between grace and God's commands.

God's law reveals his righteous character and exposes our sin. It cannot justify sinners, because none of us has kept it perfectly. But the inability of the law to justify does not mean God's commands are evil or irrelevant. The New Testament teaches that the law is holy, righteous, and good, while showing that salvation and new obedience come through Christ and the Spirit (Romans 7:12; 8:1–4).

Christians do not obey in order to become children of God. They obey because, through Christ, they have been adopted as God's children. Obedience is not a payment offered to a reluctant Father; it is the response of those who have received mercy. Jesus said that those who love him will keep his commandments (John 14:15). The apostle John likewise connects knowing Christ with a life that takes his commands seriously (1 John 2:3–6).

This obedience is not flawless. It is sincere, dependent, and repentant. When we discover that we have sinned, we do not defend ourselves by appealing to grace as permission. We confess, receive the comfort of the gospel, and seek to walk in the light.

13. Suffering and spiritual maturity

Sanctification does not mean that life becomes easy or that Christians are spared grief. Scripture teaches that trials can become occasions in which faith is tested and perseverance is formed (James 1:2–4; Romans 5:3–5). God can use hardship to expose misplaced trust, deepen dependence, teach patience, and make believers more like Christ.

That does not mean suffering is always easy to explain, or that we should tell a grieving person that we know exactly why a particular tragedy happened. The Bible gives us permission to lament. The Psalms contain cries of confusion, sorrow, and fear. Jesus himself wept at the tomb of Lazarus (John 11:32–36). Christian hope does not require pretending that pain is small.

Nor should we assume that a person's suffering proves that they have failed spiritually. Job's friends made that mistake. The Lord may use suffering for our good, but we must be careful not to claim knowledge of his hidden purposes in a particular circumstance. We can encourage sufferers with God's character, promises, presence, and the hope of resurrection without offering explanations Scripture has not given.

In hardship, sanctification may look less like visible achievement and more like continuing to trust God, asking for help, refusing bitterness, receiving the care of the church, and holding on to Christ when answers are not clear.

14. Why growth happens in the church

The New Testament does not picture the Christian life as a private project. Christ saves individuals, but he also joins them to his people. The church is described as a body whose members need one another (1 Corinthians 12:12–27). Each believer has a place in the life of the body, and every member is called to use what God has given for the good of others.

We need faithful preaching and teaching, but we also need ordinary relationships in which Christians encourage, correct, forgive, bear burdens, and serve. A believer who refuses all meaningful fellowship loses gifts God intends to use for their good. Isolation can also allow sin and discouragement to grow unchecked.

Church life is not always easy. Churches are made up of people who are themselves being sanctified. There will be misunderstandings, weaknesses, disappointments, and the need for repentance. The answer is not to expect a perfect congregation or to abandon the church at the first difficulty. Scripture calls believers to patience, truth, forgiveness, wise correction, and commitment to the good of Christ's body.

We should also remember that church attendance by itself does not prove spiritual maturity. The question is not simply whether we are present, but whether we receive God's Word, worship sincerely, love the brethren, serve faithfully, and pursue peace and holiness.

15. Growth, weakness, and assurance

Some Christians become deeply troubled because they can see how much sin remains in them. They compare themselves with others, remember repeated failures, or assume that genuine believers should experience steady visible progress every week. This can lead to discouragement and an unhealthy inward fixation.

Scripture does call us to examine ourselves, but it does not teach us to make our fluctuating performance the foundation of our acceptance with God. Our confidence rests first in Christ and his promises. Those who come to him will not be cast out (John 6:37). There is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1).

At the same time, assurance is not a reason to ignore the evidence of our lives. The New Testament points to faith, love, obedience, repentance, and perseverance as marks associated with genuine Christian life (1 John 2:3–6; 3:14). These marks do not replace Christ as the ground of assurance. They serve as evidence that our profession is not merely empty words.

When growth seems slow, ask honest questions. Am I resisting correction? Am I protecting a sin I do not want to surrender? Have I neglected the means God gives for growth? Do I need help from a mature Christian? But also ask whether you are overlooking small but real signs of grace: a growing hatred of sin, a willingness to confess, a new concern for others, or a renewed desire to hear God's Word.

Do not measure your standing with God by comparing your hidden struggles with someone else's public strengths. Look to Christ, make use of the help he provides, and keep walking in faith.

16. Perseverance and the hope of completion

The Bible gives believers strong encouragement that God will not abandon the work of grace he has begun. Paul expresses confidence that the God who began a good work in the Philippians will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ (Philippians 1:6). Jesus promises that he will keep his sheep and that no one will snatch them from his hand (John 10:27–30).

This assurance does not make perseverance unnecessary. Scripture repeatedly warns Christians against falling away, calls them to remain in Christ, and urges them to continue in faith. God's preserving grace works through these warnings and exhortations, not in opposition to them. Believers persevere because God preserves them, and God's people are therefore called to keep trusting and obeying.

Christians may stumble and need restoration. Peter denied Jesus, yet the Lord restored him. The Bible does not teach that every serious failure proves a person was never a believer. It does teach that a settled, unrepentant abandonment of Christ is spiritually dangerous and must not be treated lightly. We should encourage the weak, correct the wandering, and call all who profess faith to continue in Christ.

The final hope of sanctification is glorification. When Christ appears, believers will be made fully like him and will no longer struggle with sin (1 John 3:2–3; Romans 8:29–30). The work that is incomplete now will be completed by God. This hope encourages present holiness: those who hope in Christ seek to purify themselves as he is pure.

17. Common misunderstandings about sanctification

“If I were truly saved, I would never struggle with sin.”

The New Testament expects believers to fight sin, confess it, and grow. The presence of a struggle does not by itself prove the absence of spiritual life. The important question is whether we are bringing sin to Christ and seeking to put it to death, or defending it and making peace with it.

“God accepts me more when I perform better.”

God's justifying acceptance rests on Christ, not on our changing record. Our obedience pleases our Father as the fruit of grace, but it is not the foundation of our righteous standing. We must not turn sanctification into a second method of justification.

“Grace means holiness is optional.”

Grace is not permission to remain in sin. The grace that saves also trains believers to reject ungodliness and live self-controlled lives (Titus 2:11–14). A claim to know Christ while refusing his lordship is not a healthy understanding of the gospel.

“Spiritual growth is measured by extraordinary experiences.”

God may give unusual encouragements, but Scripture directs us to the ordinary fruit of the Spirit, faithful obedience, love, endurance, and sound doctrine. Experiences must be tested by God's Word, not treated as a substitute for it.

“Mature Christians no longer need correction.”

All believers remain learners until glory. Mature Christians should be increasingly teachable, not increasingly defensive. A willingness to receive correction is itself an important expression of humility.

“If I make enough effort, I can change myself.”

Discipline matters, but self-reliance cannot produce the life of the Spirit. We act in dependence on God, asking him to change our desires and strengthen us to obey.

18. Pursuing holiness in everyday life

Sanctification is worked out in ordinary decisions. It concerns how we speak at home, how we treat people who cannot benefit us, what we do with our time, how we respond when criticised, what we look at when alone, and whether we keep our word. The Christian life is not divided into a religious part and an ordinary part. Every part belongs to Christ.

A helpful pattern is to begin with God's grace, examine life by his Word, confess what is wrong, and take the next obedient step. This is not a formula that guarantees a perfect day. It is a way of learning to live dependently and honestly before God.

Read and respond to Scripture

Choose a regular time to read a passage carefully. Ask what it teaches, what it reveals about God, what sin it exposes, what promise it offers, and what obedience it calls for. Avoid turning reading into a race to finish a schedule. Let the text shape prayer and action.

Pray honestly and specifically

Bring your actual weaknesses to God. Ask for help with particular temptations, for love toward difficult people, for wisdom in decisions, and for courage to obey. Give thanks for signs of grace and confess sin without making excuses. Remember that prayer is possible because of Christ, not because you have had a particularly good day.

Take practical steps against temptation

Do not deliberately place yourself in situations that repeatedly lead you into sin. Remove avoidable occasions for temptation, seek wise accountability, and replace harmful habits with good ones. If a struggle involves addiction, abuse, compulsive behaviour, or serious harm, seek appropriate pastoral and professional help rather than trying to manage it alone.

Practise love in concrete ways

Look for opportunities to serve, forgive, give, encourage, listen, and tell the truth. Love is not merely warm feeling. It seeks the good of another person in ways shaped by God's commands. Begin with the people and responsibilities God has already placed before you.

Stay connected to a faithful church

Gather for worship, receive biblical teaching, participate in the ordinances, and build relationships in which you can both give and receive encouragement. Do not wait until you feel spiritually strong before seeking fellowship. The church is one of the places where God strengthens weak believers.

Review your life without despair or pride

From time to time, consider where you have grown and where you need to repent. Thank God for progress without boasting. Acknowledge failure without surrendering to hopelessness. Seek help where a pattern is beyond what you can wisely address alone.

19. Questions Christians often ask

Can a Christian become perfectly sinless in this life?

No. Scripture calls believers to holiness and real growth, but it also teaches that Christians continue to need confession, forgiveness, and the cleansing of Christ (1 John 1:8–2:2). Complete freedom from sin belongs to the final state, when Christ brings his people to glory.

Does sanctification happen at the same pace for everyone?

No. Christians differ in maturity, background, responsibilities, trials, knowledge, and areas of weakness. Growth is real but not identical in its visible pace. We should not excuse sin, but neither should we judge another believer's entire spiritual condition by a single comparison.

Are spiritual disciplines necessary for growth?

They are important means of growth, but they are not a way to earn grace. Reading Scripture, prayer, worship, fellowship, and service help us attend to God's Word and depend on him. Their value lies in faith-filled use, not in treating them as a scorecard.

What if I keep falling into the same sin?

Do not hide it or rename it. Confess it to God, seek the help of mature believers, identify patterns and triggers, and take practical steps to turn away from it. If the pattern is severe or compulsive, seek appropriate professional support as well. Do not conclude that repeated struggle makes repentance pointless; neither should you use repeated forgiveness as an excuse to stop fighting.

Can good works increase my standing before God?

They cannot improve the basis of your justification. Christ is the ground of acceptance. Yet the good works of believers matter: they glorify God, serve neighbours, strengthen the church, and provide evidence of living faith. They are the fruit of salvation, not its purchase price.

How can I know whether I am growing?

Look for a growing pattern of faith, repentance, love, humility, obedience, and dependence on Christ. You may notice that you respond differently to correction, become quicker to confess sin, care more about the good of others, or increasingly desire God's Word. Do not expect sinless perfection, and do not make your progress the foundation of assurance.

20. The goal: becoming like Christ

Sanctification is not an attempt to build an impressive religious identity. It is God's gracious work in people who belong to Jesus Christ. He sets them apart for himself, gives them new life, and by his Spirit renews them so that their lives increasingly reflect the character of their Saviour.

This growth involves real effort, but never self-sufficiency. We are called to pursue holiness, put sin to death, love our neighbours, receive God's Word, pray, worship with the church, and persevere in faith. Yet in all of it we depend on the grace of God. He works in his people, and they work because he is at work in them.

Do not confuse the slow progress of sanctification with the basis of your acceptance before God. Your hope is not that you will become good enough to deserve Christ. Your hope is Christ himself: his perfect obedience, his atoning death, his resurrection, and his faithful promise to keep his people. At the same time, do not use that hope to make peace with sin. The Saviour who justifies his people also calls them to follow him.

The Christian life is therefore a life of grateful dependence and active obedience. We stumble, confess, receive mercy, and continue. We look to Christ, and by the Spirit we seek to become more like him. One day, the work will be complete. Until then, let us pursue holiness—not to earn God's love, but because in Christ we have received it.

The following passages provide a foundation for studying sanctification, holiness, the work of the Spirit, and perseverance. Read each in its surrounding context rather than treating references as isolated proof texts.

  • Leviticus 19:1–2; 1 Peter 1:13–16 — God's holiness and the call for his people to be holy.
  • John 15:1–11; Romans 6:1–14 — Union with Christ and newness of life.
  • Romans 8:1–17, 26–30 — Life in the Spirit and God's preserving purpose.
  • Romans 12:1–2 — Renewal of the mind and a life offered to God.
  • 1 Corinthians 1:2, 6:9–11 — Believers set apart and washed in Christ.
  • Galatians 5:16–26 — The works of the flesh and the fruit of the Spirit.
  • Ephesians 2:1–10; 4:17–32 — Grace, good works, and the new life.
  • Philippians 1:3–11; 2:12–13 — God's continuing work and the believer's obedience.
  • Colossians 3:1–17 — Putting off the old life and putting on the new.
  • 1 Thessalonians 4:1–8; 5:23–24 — God's will for holiness and his faithfulness.
  • Titus 2:11–14 — Grace training believers to live godly lives.
  • Hebrews 10:10–14; 12:1–14 — Christ's work and the pursuit of holiness.
  • James 1:2–5, 19–27 — Trials, perseverance, and doing the Word.
  • 1 John 1:5–2:6; 3:1–10 — Confession, obedience, and the marks of new life.

This study aims to explain the biblical teaching on sanctification while distinguishing what Scripture states plainly from areas where Christians may differ in their interpretation. All doctrine must be tested by the Word of God.

The Atonement: What Did Jesus Accomplish on the Cross?

The cross stands at the centre of the Christian faith. But what happened there? Why did Jesus have to die, and what did his death actually achieve for sinners?
A Scripture-centred study · The meaning, necessity, and accomplishment of Christ's atoning work
The central claim. Jesus Christ, the eternal Son of God made man, willingly gave himself as the once-for-all sacrifice for sin. In the place of his people he bore judgment, satisfied divine justice, secured redemption, reconciled sinners to God, and triumphed over the powers that held them. His resurrection vindicated his saving work. The cross is not merely an example of love or a moving tragedy; it is God's decisive saving act.

People often speak of the cross as the place where Jesus showed how much he loved us. That is true, but it does not go far enough. The New Testament says that Jesus died for our sins. His death was not simply an illustration of courage, compassion, or solidarity with suffering people. It was an achievement: something was done there that we could never do for ourselves.

To understand the cross, we must take seriously both sides of the Bible's message. God is holy, righteous, and just. Human beings are sinners who stand accountable to him. Yet God is also rich in mercy and has acted in love to save. If we soften God's holiness, the cross becomes unnecessary. If we minimise his mercy, the cross becomes incomprehensible. At Calvary, we see the seriousness of sin and the greatness of divine love together.

Christians use the word atonement for the work by which Christ deals with sin and brings his people into peace with God. Scripture describes this work through several connected pictures: sacrifice, substitution, redemption, propitiation, reconciliation, victory, and covenant. These are not rival explanations. Each reveals a different aspect of the one saving work of Jesus Christ.

This study follows the Bible's own language, including its Old Testament foundations, and considers common questions without pretending that every Christian tradition explains every detail in the same way.

1. What is atonement?

Atonement refers to Christ's work through which sin is dealt with and sinners are brought into a right relationship with God. The New Testament does not reduce this work to one image. Jesus is the sacrifice for sin, the ransom who sets captives free, the mediator who establishes a covenant, the one who bears our sins, and the victor who defeats the powers that held us.

Sacrifice tells us that guilt and uncleanness require cleansing. Substitution tells us that Christ acts on behalf of others and bears what they could not bear. Redemption speaks of release through a costly price. Reconciliation describes the end of hostility and the restoration of peace. Victory announces that sin, death, and the devil do not have the final word. Each image is biblical, and none should be discarded simply because another is more familiar.

The question is not whether we prefer one theological term, but whether our understanding does justice to all that the apostles say Jesus accomplished.

2. Why was atonement necessary?

The cross was not an arbitrary demand imposed by a cruel deity, nor was it an accident God had to turn into something useful. It was God's purposeful answer to the real problem of human sin. God is perfectly righteous and does not treat evil as harmless. His judgment is not a defect in his character; it is the proper response of the holy Creator to rebellion and injustice.

Sin is more than a collection of unfortunate mistakes. It is failure to honour God, refusal to trust him, and the turning of his good gifts towards our own ends. It damages our relationships, corrupts our desires, and leaves us accountable to the One who made us. Romans 1:18–32 describes humanity's suppression of truth and exchange of God's glory for created things. Romans 3:9–20 concludes that all are under sin and that no one can establish a righteous standing by works of the law.

Our problem is therefore moral and relational. We are guilty before the Judge and alienated from our Maker. We need more than education, improved circumstances, or religious motivation. We need forgiveness that does not compromise justice, cleansing that reaches the heart, and peace with God that we cannot manufacture.

Could God simply overlook sin? A human judge who knowingly lets serious wrongdoing go unanswered would not be praised as perfectly just. God's mercy cannot mean that he ceases to be righteous. The gospel announces that God himself provides the way in which sin is judged and sinners are forgiven. The cross is necessary because sin is truly evil and because God saves without denying his holiness.

3. The Old Testament foundations

Sacrifice and the seriousness of sin

Leviticus presents sacrifices within a system of holiness, guilt, cleansing, and access to God. The worshipper does not approach the Holy One casually. Blood is associated with life, and the sacrificial system teaches that sin brings death and that cleansing requires a God-provided means. Yet Hebrews 10:1–4 insists that the blood of bulls and goats could not finally take away sins. These sacrifices pointed beyond themselves to a better offering.

The Passover

In Exodus 12, Israel is delivered from judgment through the blood of the Passover lamb. The blood is not a human achievement; it is the sign God commanded, and those sheltered under it are spared. Paul draws the connection directly: “Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed” (1 Corinthians 5:7). The image joins judgment and rescue: God provides the means of deliverance.

The Day of Atonement

Leviticus 16 describes the high priest entering the Most Holy Place with sacrificial blood. The sins of the people are confessed over the live goat, which is sent away into the wilderness. The ceremony portrays both the removal of guilt and the need for cleansing before a holy God. Hebrews 9 explains that Jesus, our superior high priest, enters the heavenly presence by means of his own blood, securing eternal redemption.

The suffering servant

Isaiah 52:13–53:12 is one of the clearest passages illuminating the cross. The servant is despised and rejected, yet his suffering is not merely the result of human cruelty. He is wounded for transgressions, bears the iniquities of others, and makes many to be accounted righteous. His suffering is joined repeatedly to the sins of his people. Jesus and the apostles understood his mission in the light of this Scripture (Luke 22:37; Acts 8:32–35; 1 Peter 2:22–25). Psalm 22 also gives language to the suffering righteous one. At the cross, Jesus fulfils the story and promises of Scripture.

4. Who died for us?

The one who died for sinners was not merely a good man, prophet, or heroic martyr. He was Jesus Christ, the eternal Son of God who truly became human. The incarnation matters because the Saviour had to represent humanity, obey in our place, suffer, and die. His deity matters because the one who gives himself is the divine Son, whose person and work are of immeasurable worth.

Jesus did not cease to be God when he became man, nor did he merely appear to have a human body. The Word became flesh (John 1:14). He experienced hunger, weariness, grief, temptation without sin, and real bodily death. The one person of Christ possesses a truly divine nature and a truly human nature. We must not divide him into two persons, as though a human Jesus suffered while the Son of God remained uninvolved. The Son himself took our nature and gave himself for us.

Because Jesus is truly human, he can represent his people. Because he is sinless, he does not die for his own guilt. Because he is the divine Son, his sacrifice is not merely one ordinary sinner offered for another. God the Son truly died according to his human nature; the divine nature did not cease to exist or become mortal. The Father did not die on the cross, and the Trinity was not divided. The Father sent the Son, the Son willingly offered himself, and the Spirit was active in his offering (John 3:16–17; Galatians 2:20; Hebrews 9:14).

5. Christ our substitute

One of the most direct ways Scripture explains the cross is substitution: Jesus died for us, in our place, bearing what belonged to us. This is not a minor detail added by later theologians. It is woven into the biblical announcement of the gospel.

Isaiah 53 says the servant was pierced for our transgressions and crushed for our iniquities. The punishment that brought us peace was upon him, and by his wounds we are healed. The repeated contrast is striking: our sins, his suffering; our guilt, his burden; our peace, his chastisement. The servant does not merely suffer alongside the guilty; he bears their wrongdoing.

Jesus describes his mission in similar terms. The Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45). At the Last Supper, he speaks of his blood of the covenant, poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins (Matthew 26:28). Paul says Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures (1 Corinthians 15:3). Peter writes that Christ suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God (1 Peter 3:18).

“He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree, that we might die to sin and live to righteousness.”1 Peter 2:24

Substitution does not mean Jesus became morally corrupt or was personally wicked. Christ remained the spotless Son. He voluntarily identified himself with his people, took responsibility as their representative, and bore the judgment due to their sins. He did not become sinful in character; he became the sin-bearing Saviour.

Nor was the Father reluctant and persuaded by an unwilling Son. The cross is the united saving purpose of the triune God. The Father gives the Son in love; the Son lays down his life willingly; and the Spirit sustains his offering. Jesus says, “I lay down my life” (John 10:17–18).

6. Penal substitution and divine justice

Penal substitution is the teaching that Christ, as the representative substitute of his people, bore the penalty of their sin in order to satisfy God's justice and secure their forgiveness. “Penal” refers to judgment due to wrongdoing; “substitution” means Christ bears it on behalf of others. The phrase is a theological summary, but the reality is drawn from Isaiah 53, Romans 3:21–26, Galatians 3:13, and 1 Peter 2:24.

Romans 3 is especially important. Paul has shown that all people are guilty and that no one can be justified by works of the law. He announces that sinners are justified by grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus. God put Christ forward as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith. Paul explains that this demonstrates God's righteousness: God is both just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus.

How can God forgive the guilty and remain perfectly righteous? The answer is not that guilt is imaginary or justice suspended. God has acted in Christ. The penalty is not ignored; it is borne by the appointed substitute. Forgiveness is free to the sinner, but it is not costless. The cost falls upon Christ, who gives himself willingly.

Some object that punishment should never fall on an innocent person. That objection is right if we mean an unwilling stranger arbitrarily punished for someone else's crime. But Christ is not an unrelated third party. He is the divinely appointed mediator and covenant head who freely gives himself for those he represents. His action is not a miscarriage of justice; it is God's gracious provision. The one who bears the penalty is also the one who willingly undertakes the work.

Substitution guards the meaning of forgiveness. God does not forgive because sin does not matter. He forgives because Christ has dealt with it. The cross does not make God loving at the expense of justice, or just at the expense of love. It reveals his love in a way that honours his righteousness.

7. Propitiation: God's righteous wrath satisfied

The Bible speaks not only of guilt but of God's wrath against sin. This is not uncontrolled temper. Divine wrath is God's settled, holy opposition to evil. If God were indifferent to cruelty, exploitation, idolatry, and wickedness, he would not be good. His wrath is the judicial expression of his righteousness.

The New Testament uses the language of propitiation for Christ's work (Romans 3:25; 1 John 2:2; 4:10). Propitiation means that God's righteous anger against sin is dealt with through the sacrifice he himself provides. Notice the initiative: “In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins” (1 John 4:10). God is not persuaded to love by the cross; the cross is the expression of his prior love.

This guards against two mistakes. We must not imagine a cruel Father who delights in suffering or a reluctant Son who must change the Father's attitude. The Father and Son share one divine purpose. Neither should we imagine that God simply sets aside justice. At the cross, God's love provides what his justice requires. Propitiation is not the whole atonement, but it tells us that the cross deals with God's judgment, not merely our feelings of guilt.

8. Redemption: bought out of slavery

Redemption is the language of release through a price. Scripture uses this picture to describe our bondage to sin and the costly liberation Christ has secured. Jesus gives his life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45). Paul says believers have redemption through Christ's blood, the forgiveness of trespasses (Ephesians 1:7). Peter says we were not redeemed with silver or gold, but with Christ's precious blood, like that of a lamb without blemish (1 Peter 1:18–19).

We should not press the metaphor beyond what Scripture teaches. The Bible does not present Satan as a rightful owner to whom God must pay a ransom. Our bondage is real, but Satan has no legitimate claim to receive Christ's blood. Redemption means that Christ's costly death secures release from sin's guilt and enslaving power, and from the condemnation under which we stood.

Redemption is already possessed and still awaits its final completion. Believers have forgiveness now, yet await the redemption of their bodies (Romans 8:23; Ephesians 1:13–14). The cross has secured the future; the resurrection and return of Christ will bring deliverance to its full visible completion.

9. Reconciliation: brought near to God

Sin creates hostility between humanity and God. We do not naturally approach him as friends who simply need encouragement. We are alienated in mind and practice, and need peace with the One against whom we have sinned. Reconciliation describes the restoration of relationship through Christ's death.

Romans 5:8–11 says that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. Having been justified by his blood, believers are saved from wrath through him. While enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son. We did not first make ourselves agreeable and then receive God's welcome. His love moved towards us when we were helpless and hostile.

Second Corinthians 5:18–21 describes God as the one who reconciles us to himself through Christ, not counting our trespasses against us. The same passage speaks of Christ, who knew no sin, being made sin for us so that in him we might become the righteousness of God. Reconciliation does not mean God changes from unjust hostility to benevolence. Through Christ, the real barrier of guilt and judgment is removed and peace is established. Those who were far off are brought near by his blood (Ephesians 2:13–18).

10. Sacrifice, cleansing, and forgiveness

Scripture describes sin as defilement as well as guilt. We need not only a changed legal standing but cleansing from sin's pollution. The sacrificial language of the Bible explains this aspect of Christ's work. His blood cleanses; his offering is once for all; and those who trust him are brought into God's presence.

Hebrews 9–10 contrasts repeated sacrifices under the old covenant with Christ's definitive offering. The former could not perfect the conscience or finally remove sin. Christ entered the holy places once for all by means of his own blood, securing eternal redemption. By one offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified (Hebrews 10:14).

“Once for all” matters. Jesus does not need to be sacrificed again. His death is not repeated in the Lord's Supper, nor is another priestly offering required to complete it. The Supper is a remembrance and proclamation of his death (1 Corinthians 11:23–26). It directs faith to the finished sacrifice, not to a new one. Forgiveness is not God pretending sin never happened; it is the removal of guilt on the basis of Christ's real and sufficient offering.

11. The cross as victory over evil

The New Testament presents the cross as Christ's decisive victory over the powers of darkness. This does not compete with substitution or sacrifice; it tells us what Christ's death accomplished against the enemies that enslaved humanity.

Colossians 2:13–15 says God made believers alive with Christ, forgiving their trespasses and cancelling the record of debt that stood against them. He set it aside, nailing it to the cross, and disarmed the rulers and authorities. Notice the connection: the powers are disarmed because the condemning debt has been cancelled. The cross defeats the accuser by removing the guilt with which he could accuse.

Hebrews 2:14–15 says that through death Jesus destroyed the one who has the power of death, the devil, and delivered those held in lifelong slavery through fear of death. Christ entered the condition that terrified us and broke its dominion. Christians still face temptation, persecution, and physical death, but the victory is real; its final public manifestation awaits Christ's return. Death itself will be abolished (1 Corinthians 15:20–26).

12. The love of God displayed at the cross

It is sometimes suggested that penal substitution makes the cross about divine anger rather than divine love. Scripture refuses that contrast. The cross is the supreme demonstration of God's love precisely because it shows what he was willing to give to save sinners.

“God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us” (Romans 5:8). The measure of love is not that God decided sin was unimportant. It is that he gave his Son for people who had no claim on his favour. John 3:16–17 joins the giving of the Son with God's love for the world and his purpose to save through him. First John 4:9–10 says love was made manifest when God sent his only Son so that we might live through him.

The Son's love is equally personal and willing. Galatians 2:20 speaks of the Son of God who loved me and gave himself for me. The cross is not the Father loving at the expense of the Son, or the Son loving in opposition to the Father. The triune God saves in perfect unity. And the cross teaches us that Christian love is not sentiment detached from truth: it acts for the good of others, bears cost, and seeks reconciliation.

13. For whom did Christ die?

Christians have long discussed how to understand the Bible's statements that Jesus died for “the world,” “all,” and “many,” alongside passages that speak of his laying down his life for his sheep or giving himself for the church. These texts should be read together rather than allowing one set to silence another.

Scripture teaches that Christ's death is of infinite worth and fully sufficient to save every sinner who comes to him. The gospel is to be proclaimed to all people without distinction. No one who repents and believes will be turned away (John 6:37; Revelation 22:17). We must not narrow the invitation, speculate about hidden election, or tell any sincere seeker that Christ is unavailable to them.

Scripture also describes Christ's death as accomplishing a definite redemption for the people the Father gave him. Jesus says he lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11, 14–16). He gives himself for the church (Ephesians 5:25). The angel announces that he will save his people from their sins (Matthew 1:21). The Good Shepherd does not merely make salvation possible; he secures the salvation of those he came to save.

These truths are held together by distinguishing the infinite sufficiency of Christ's sacrifice and the particular saving intention and effect of his death. Christ's sacrifice is entirely sufficient, and the gospel invitation is genuinely for everyone who hears it. Yet Christ gives himself as the covenant representative of his people and secures for them the blessings of salvation. The Spirit applies this work by bringing people to repentance and faith.

Some Christians understand the relevant passages differently and argue that Christ died with the same saving intention for every individual, while the benefits are received only through faith. They appeal especially to 1 Timothy 2:1–6, Hebrews 2:9, and 1 John 2:2. These passages deserve serious attention. The word “all” can refer to all kinds of people or humanity without distinction, and “world” can emphasise the worldwide reach of Christ's mission rather than guarantee the salvation of every individual. Context matters.

Wherever readers land in this debate, three truths must remain clear: Christ's sacrifice is fully sufficient; the gospel must be offered freely and sincerely to all; and everyone who is saved owes salvation entirely to God's grace in Christ. We should speak with conviction where Scripture is clear and humility where faithful Christians differ.

14. The sufficiency and finality of the cross

When Jesus cried, “It is finished” (John 19:30), he declared that the work the Father had given him had reached its appointed completion. The sacrifice was offered. The debt was dealt with. The mission was fulfilled.

Hebrews repeatedly stresses the finality of Christ's offering. Old covenant priests stood daily offering sacrifices that could never finally take away sins. Christ offered one sacrifice for sins and sat down at God's right hand (Hebrews 10:11–14). His seated posture signifies the completion and sufficiency of his priestly offering. No additional sacrifice can improve it.

This does not mean Christ has no continuing ministry. He is risen, reigns, intercedes for his people, and will return. His intercession applies the benefits of the work he has completed; it does not add a missing payment to the cross (Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25). Nor does finality mean believers have no need to confess sin or pursue holiness. It means we do not repeat or supplement his atoning sacrifice.

Good works matter deeply, but they are fruit of salvation, not a contribution to the price of redemption. We obey because Christ has saved us, not to finish what he left undone.

15. Why the resurrection matters

The cross must never be separated from the resurrection. The apostles did not preach a dead hero whose memory inspires us. They proclaimed the crucified and risen Lord. If Christ remained in the grave, death would still have claimed him and the gospel would be empty (1 Corinthians 15:12–19).

The resurrection is God's public vindication of Jesus. It declares that the one condemned by human authorities is God's righteous Son and appointed Messiah. It demonstrates that his sacrifice has not failed and that death's power has been broken. Romans 4:25 connects Jesus being delivered up for our trespasses with his being raised for our justification. The risen Christ is the living guarantee that those united to him will also be raised.

Our salvation is not only cancellation of past guilt. It includes new life now and bodily resurrection in the age to come. Believers are united to Christ in his death and resurrection, so they may walk in newness of life (Romans 6:3–11). Christian hope is the renewal of God's creation and the resurrection of his people, free from sin, corruption, and death.

16. The cross and the new covenant

At the Last Supper, Jesus interpreted his approaching death in covenant language: his blood was the blood of the covenant, poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins (Matthew 26:28; Luke 22:20). He was explaining that his death would establish the promised new covenant.

The prophets spoke of a covenant in which God would forgive the sins of his people, write his law on their hearts, and bring them into a renewed knowledge of himself (Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:25–27). Hebrews 8–10 presents Jesus as the mediator of this better covenant. His sacrifice secures forgiveness, and the Spirit brings the promised inward renewal.

The cross does not merely make individuals feel better about private guilt. Christ is gathering a people for himself: forgiven, cleansed, indwelt by the Spirit, and called to live as God's covenant community. Baptism and the Lord's Supper visibly proclaim the gospel and identify the gathered people with Christ, but neither ordinance replaces the once-for-all sacrifice that established the covenant.

The new covenant explains why forgiveness and transformation belong together without being confused. God pardons his people and gives them new hearts. We are not accepted because we have already become holy; we are made holy because we have been brought into the saving relationship Christ secured.

17. Common objections and misunderstandings

“Isn't the cross divine child abuse?”

This objection imagines a powerless child forced by an abusive parent to suffer. That is not the biblical account. The Son is not a creature or unwilling victim. He shares the divine nature with the Father and willingly gives himself. The Father sends the Son in love, and the Son lays down his life freely (John 10:17–18; 1 John 4:10). The cross is the self-giving action of the triune God, not the abuse of an unwilling third party.

“Why can't God simply forgive without a sacrifice?”

God is merciful, but he never acts against his own righteousness. Human forgiveness can be costly: the injured person may choose not to retaliate, but the wrong remains real. At the cross, God does more than announce that sin no longer matters. He provides the sacrifice that deals with guilt and demonstrates his justice (Romans 3:25–26). Forgiveness is free to us, not proof that evil is trivial.

“Does substitution mean God punished an innocent person?”

Jesus is innocent, but he is not an unwilling stranger. He voluntarily takes the place of those he represents. As mediator and covenant head, he assumes the burden of their guilt and bears its judgment. His willingness shows the depth of his love. Scripture presents his offering as obedience and self-giving (Philippians 2:5–8; Hebrews 10:5–10).

“Isn't the cross only an example of love?”

It is an example. Peter says Christ suffered, leaving believers an example to follow (1 Peter 2:21). But the same passage says he bore our sins in his body on the tree (2:24). The cross both saves us and teaches us. If it is only an example, it cannot answer our guilt or reconcile us to God. We need more than inspiration; we need a Saviour.

“If Christ paid for sin, why do Christians still suffer and die?”

Christians still live in a fallen world and await the resurrection. The cross removes condemnation for those in Christ, but it does not mean every consequence of a broken creation disappears immediately. Believers may suffer sickness, persecution, and physical death. Yet none can separate them from God's love, and death itself will finally be destroyed (Romans 8:18–39; 1 Corinthians 15:20–26).

“Does this mean everyone will automatically be saved?”

No. Scripture calls people to repent and believe and warns of final judgment. The sufficiency of Christ's sacrifice does not mean every person is united to Christ regardless of their response. The gospel is proclaimed to all, and all who come to Christ are received; those who reject him remain accountable (John 3:18–21, 36; Acts 17:30–31).

“Do good works add anything to the cross?”

No work of ours can add to Christ's sacrifice or earn God's verdict. We are saved by grace through faith, not as a result of works (Ephesians 2:8–9). Yet the next verse says we are created in Christ Jesus for good works. Obedience is the fruit of salvation, not its purchase price.

18. What does the cross mean for believers?

Forgiveness rather than condemnation

Those who are in Christ are not under condemnation (Romans 8:1). Their sins are not overlooked or hidden beneath religious performance; they have been dealt with by Christ. This gives a real basis for peace with God, even as believers continue to confess sin and grow.

Access to God

Through Christ we have access to the Father by one Spirit (Ephesians 2:18). We do not need another mediator to make the Son willing to receive us. Jesus is the one mediator between God and humanity (1 Timothy 2:5), and he invites his people to draw near with confidence (Hebrews 4:14–16; 10:19–22).

Freedom from sin's dominion

Christ did not die merely to change our future destination while leaving us content to serve sin. Those united to him have died to sin's dominion and are called to live in newness of life (Romans 6:1–14). The struggle remains real, but sin is no longer the rightful master of the believer.

A new people and a sure hope

The cross removes hostility that divides people and creates one new humanity in Christ (Ephesians 2:11–22). Christians are united not by ethnicity, class, culture, or achievement, but by grace. And because Jesus died and rose, those who belong to him will also be raised. Their future rests on the faithfulness of the risen Lord.

These blessings should produce humility, gratitude, holiness, courage, and love. The person who understands the cost of grace has no reason to boast over others and no reason to treat sin lightly.

19. The proper response to the cross

The message of the cross calls for more than admiration. Jesus commands people to repent and believe the gospel (Mark 1:15). Repentance means turning from sin and self-rule towards God. Faith means entrusting yourself to Jesus Christ, resting your hope on who he is and what he has done. These are not payments offered to earn salvation; they are the response of a sinner who receives God's mercy.

You cannot make yourself worthy before coming to Christ. You do not need to repair your record, overcome every temptation, or become religious enough to qualify. Come as someone who needs forgiveness. Confess your sin honestly. Stop making excuses for what God calls evil. Trust the crucified and risen Lord, who receives all who come to him.

If you already belong to Christ, do not move beyond the cross as though it were only the beginning of the Christian life. Return to it in faith and gratitude. Let the love shown there teach you to forgive. Let the holiness displayed there make you hate sin. Let the finished work of Christ quiet your attempts to earn acceptance. Let his victory give you courage in suffering and hope in death.

We do not proclaim a message of human superiority. We announce mercy for guilty people, because we too stand by grace. Speak truthfully about sin, but never as though you were beyond the need of the same Saviour. Invite people to Christ with patience, clarity, and compassion.

20. The heart of the matter

Why did Jesus die? He died for our sins, in accordance with the Scriptures. The eternal Son of God became truly human, lived in perfect obedience, and willingly offered himself as the substitute for sinners. He bore judgment, satisfied divine justice, secured redemption, cleansed guilt, reconciled his people to God, established the new covenant, and triumphed over the powers of darkness. He did not remain in the grave: he rose bodily and reigns as the living Lord.

The cross is not one theme among many that Christians may safely neglect. It is central to the apostolic gospel. It tells us sin is more serious than we imagined, God's holiness is not negotiable, and his love is greater than we could have hoped. It humbles human pride because salvation is not our achievement. It gives hope because the work belongs to Christ.

Do not measure the love of God by guessing how worthy you are. Look at the cross. There the Son of God gave himself for sinners. Come to him, trust his finished work, and find in the crucified and risen Christ the mercy you cannot provide for yourself.

  2. Leviticus 16 — the Day of Atonement.
  3. Mark 10:35–45 — the Son of Man gives his life as a ransom.
  4. Matthew 26:26–29 — the blood of the covenant.
  5. Romans 3:21–26 — God just and the justifier through Christ.
  6. Romans 5:6–11 — Christ dies for the ungodly and reconciles enemies.

  8. Galatians 3:10–14 — Christ bears the curse.
  9. Colossians 2:13–15 — the debt cancelled and powers disarmed.
  10. Hebrews 9–10 — Christ's once-for-all sacrifice.
  11. 1 Peter 2:21–25 — Christ bears our sins.
  12. 1 John 4:7–12 — God's love revealed in the sending of his Son.

This study summarises several biblical themes without claiming every Christian tradition uses the same theological vocabulary. Read the cited passages in context, consider the whole witness of Scripture, and test every explanation by God's Word. The central Christian confession is that salvation is found in Jesus Christ, crucified and risen.

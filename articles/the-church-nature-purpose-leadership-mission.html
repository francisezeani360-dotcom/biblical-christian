The Church: Its Nature, Purpose, Leadership, and Mission

What is the church according to Scripture? Why did Christ establish it, how should it be ordered, and what has he sent it into the world to do?
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian
The central truth. The church belongs to Jesus Christ. He purchased it with his blood, rules it by his Word, gives it shepherds and servants, and gathers believers into a people who worship God, build one another up, guard the truth, and make disciples of all nations. The church is not a human invention or merely a religious organisation; it is Christ's people, gathered under his authority.

People use the word church in different ways. Sometimes they mean a building, a denomination, a ministry, a Sunday service, or simply people who share a religious belief. These uses may point to something connected with church life, but they do not by themselves explain what the New Testament means when it speaks of the church.

“I will build my church, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against it.”Matthew 16:18 — reference for study

What makes a group of people a church? Is it enough for Christians to meet occasionally? Who has authority to lead a congregation? What is the relationship between pastors and members? Why do baptism and the Lord's Supper matter? Should a church discipline its members? And is the church's mission mainly to improve society, provide religious services, or proclaim the gospel and make disciples?

These are not merely administrative questions. They concern the people Christ loves and gave himself for. A confused understanding of the church can lead to unhealthy leadership, shallow membership, neglected worship, division, or a weakened witness. We must allow Scripture to define the church, rather than allowing tradition, personal preference, or popular church culture to have the final word.

1. What is the church?

The New Testament word translated church refers to an assembly or congregation. In Christian use, it describes the people whom God has called to himself through Jesus Christ. The church is not fundamentally a building, religious brand, or programme. It is a people who belong to the Lord.

Jesus promised, “I will build my church” (Matthew 16:18). The church is his possession and his work. Paul tells the Ephesian elders to care for the church of God, which he obtained with his own blood (Acts 20:28). The church is precious because Christ gave himself for it.

Scripture uses several pictures. The church is the body of Christ, with Christ as its head and believers as members who need one another (1 Corinthians 12:12–27; Ephesians 1:22–23). It is God's household, built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ as the cornerstone (Ephesians 2:19–22). It is a flock under the care of the Chief Shepherd (1 Peter 5:1–4), and a people belonging to God, called to proclaim his excellencies (1 Peter 2:9–10).

These pictures teach unity, mutual dependence, belonging, care, holiness, and witness. The church is made up of people reconciled to God through Christ, not people who have earned their place by moral achievement. Yet not every group that calls itself Christian is therefore faithful: its confession, worship, and practice must be tested by Scripture.

2. The universal church and the local church

Scripture speaks of the church in a broad sense and in a local, gathered sense. The universal church consists of all who truly belong to Christ across every place and generation. Jesus knows his sheep and has one people whom he is gathering (John 10:14–16; Ephesians 5:25–27).

The New Testament also speaks of identifiable local congregations: the church in Jerusalem, the church in Corinth, the churches of Galatia, and churches meeting in particular homes (Acts 8:1; 1 Corinthians 1:2; Galatians 1:2; Romans 16:5). These congregations gathered, received teaching, appointed leaders, observed the ordinances, cared for members, and were addressed as churches.

The universal church is not a substitute for the local church. A Christian cannot ordinarily obey the commands to gather, serve, encourage, share the Lord's Supper, and bear one another's burdens while deliberately remaining detached from any congregation. Nor should a local church imagine that it is the whole people of Christ. Faithful congregations belong to a much larger family, and this should produce humility and love for genuine believers beyond our own circle.

3. Christ is the head of the church

Christ's authority over the church is absolute. God gave him as head over all things to the church, which is his body (Ephesians 1:22–23). He is not merely a founder whose ideas a congregation may adapt at will. He is the living Lord who continues to rule his people.

Christ exercises his authority through his Word. The apostles were authorised witnesses whose teaching is preserved in Scripture. The church is built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ as the cornerstone (Ephesians 2:20). No pastor, council, denomination, or modern personality may claim authority equal to Christ's Word.

Churches must make practical decisions about meeting times, budgets, buildings, and ministry arrangements. Scripture does not specify every detail. But such decisions must be consistent with biblical principles and must never be treated as divine commands merely because leaders prefer them. Leaders are servants, not owners; members are disciples of Jesus, not personal followers of a pastor.

4. The marks of a faithful church

A congregation should not be judged chiefly by size, popularity, wealth, music style, social influence, or the personality of its leader. These things do not establish faithfulness. The church must be evaluated by its relationship to Christ and his Word.

“And they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers.”Acts 2:42

Faithful teaching stands at the centre. The early believers devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayers (Acts 2:42). A church must proclaim the gospel clearly, teach the whole counsel of God, and reject doctrines that contradict the faith once delivered to the saints (Acts 20:27; Jude 3). Preaching should explain and apply Scripture rather than use verses as decorations for ideas formed elsewhere.

The ordinances Christ gave—baptism and the Lord's Supper—belong to the church's ordinary life. They visibly declare the gospel and identify the gathered people with Christ. A faithful church also pursues prayer, holiness, mutual care, discipline, and love. Members should bear burdens, forgive, serve the weak, and live as people who belong to Christ (John 13:34–35; Galatians 6:1–2; Hebrews 10:24–25).

These marks do not mean a true church is flawless. Every congregation needs correction and growth. The question is whether it is committed to Scripture, the gospel, the ordinances, and obedience to Christ.

5. Church membership

The New Testament does not prescribe a modern membership form, but it clearly describes believers being known, received, and held accountable within particular churches. In Acts 2, those who received Peter's message were baptised and added to the community (Acts 2:41–42). Paul addressed identifiable congregations and expected them to act together. The responsibility to address serious, unrepentant sin in 1 Corinthians 5 assumes that a church knows who belongs to its fellowship.

Membership is not an attempt to improve the gospel by adding a human requirement to justification. It is a public commitment to walk with a particular congregation under Christ's Word. Members gather, receive care, use their gifts, submit to biblical oversight, pursue peace, and accept responsibility for the church's faithfulness.

Healthy membership is neither consumerism nor control. A church is not a shop where people attend only while the experience suits them. Neither is membership permission for leaders to dominate people's lives. It is a commitment to love and serve one another as disciples of Jesus. Churches should receive members carefully and pastorally, seeking a credible profession of faith, an understanding of the gospel, baptism as a believer, and a willingness to live under Scripture.

6. Baptism

Baptism is an ordinance commanded by Jesus. In the Great Commission, he commands his disciples to make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey everything he commanded (Matthew 28:18–20). Baptism belongs to the making and identifying of disciples.

The pattern in Acts is that those who receive the apostolic message are baptised (Acts 2:41; 8:12; 18:8). Baptism is therefore properly administered to those who credibly profess repentance and faith in Christ. It does not regenerate a person or earn forgiveness. Salvation is by grace through faith; the outward act must not be confused with the inward work of the Spirit.

The New Testament presents baptism as immersion: the person is brought into the water and raised from it. This fittingly portrays burial and resurrection with Christ (Romans 6:3–4; Colossians 2:12). Baptism publicly confesses allegiance to Christ and identifies the believer with his people. It does not make the baptised person sinless or guarantee that a profession is genuine; the reality signified must be present.

Christians differ over the proper subjects and mode of baptism. Some traditions baptise infants based on their understanding of the covenant and the relationship between the old and new covenant signs. Others understand the New Testament pattern to require a personal profession of faith before baptism. The case for believer's baptism rests on the repeated connection between hearing the gospel, believing, and being baptised, and on baptism's meaning as a confession of union with Christ. The discussion should be conducted honestly and respectfully.

7. The Lord's Supper

On the night before his crucifixion, Jesus gave his disciples bread and the cup, commanding them to do this in remembrance of him (Luke 22:14–20; 1 Corinthians 11:23–26). The Lord's Supper is an ordinance for the church, proclaiming the Lord's death until he comes.

The Supper remembers Christ's once-for-all sacrifice. The bread and cup direct faith away from human merit and towards the Saviour who gave himself for sinners. It is also an expression of communion in Christ and fellowship with his people. Paul says that the one bread signifies that we, though many, are one body (1 Corinthians 10:16–17).

The elements do not become a new sacrifice of Christ. His offering was sufficient and complete (Hebrews 10:10–14). Nor should the Supper be treated as an empty ceremony. It is a serious act of worship in which believers remember Christ, proclaim his death, examine themselves, and express their unity in him.

Paul warns against eating and drinking in an unworthy manner (1 Corinthians 11:27–32). He is not teaching that only Christians who have achieved sinless perfection may come. The warning concerns careless, selfish, divisive participation that dishonours the Lord and disregards fellow believers. The proper response is repentance, faith, reconciliation, and reverent participation. The Supper also points forward to the return of Christ and the promised feast in his kingdom (Matthew 26:29; 1 Corinthians 11:26).

8. Church leadership: elders and deacons

Christ cares for his churches through qualified servants. The New Testament uses the terms elder, overseer, and pastor to describe aspects of the same shepherding office. Elders oversee and shepherd the congregation, teaching God's Word, guarding sound doctrine, praying for the flock, and setting an example (Acts 20:17, 28; Titus 1:5–9; 1 Peter 5:1–4).

The qualifications focus heavily on character: an overseer must be above reproach, faithful in marriage, self-controlled, respectable, hospitable, able to teach, not violent or greedy, and able to manage his household well (1 Timothy 3:1–7). Public gifting or popularity cannot compensate for a life that contradicts the gospel. A pastor is not qualified merely because he speaks confidently or attracts a crowd.

The New Testament presents a plurality of elders in local churches (Acts 14:23; 20:17; Philippians 1:1; James 5:14). Shared shepherding provides mutual accountability and guards against one-man rule. Elders are under-shepherds who will give account to Christ.

Deacons are appointed servants of the church. The qualifications in 1 Timothy 3:8–13 emphasise dignity, sincerity, self-control, faithfulness, and tested character. Deacons serve the practical needs of the congregation and support its healthy life. The offices are distinct, but both are valuable forms of service.

Scripture's qualifications for the office of elder/overseer describe a man who is able to teach and is the husband of one wife (1 Timothy 3:1–7; Titus 1:5–9). The office is therefore entrusted to qualified men. This does not mean women are less valuable, less intelligent, or less gifted. Women are essential to the church's life and ministry, serving through discipleship, teaching other women and children, prayer, hospitality, mercy, generosity, and gospel labour within the boundaries Scripture gives (Romans 16:1–6; Titus 2:3–5).

Leaders must not dominate those entrusted to their care. Peter commands elders to shepherd willingly and by example, not as lords over the flock (1 Peter 5:2–3). Christ is the Chief Shepherd, and every earthly shepherd remains accountable to him.

9. The responsibility of the congregation

Biblical leadership does not make church members passive spectators. The New Testament addresses commands to the congregation as a whole. Members are to preserve unity, guard the gospel, care for one another, practise discipline, recognise faithful leaders, and use their gifts for the common good.

In Matthew 18:15–20, Jesus describes a process for addressing sin that ultimately involves the church. In 1 Corinthians 5, Paul holds the congregation responsible for responding to serious, public, unrepentant sin. In Galatians 1:8–9, churches are warned not to accept a different gospel, even if the messenger appears impressive. The congregation has a real responsibility to test teaching and maintain the faith.

Members are also instructed to respect and submit to faithful leaders who labour among them in the Lord (1 Thessalonians 5:12–13; Hebrews 13:17). This is not blind or absolute submission. No leader may require disobedience to Christ, conceal wrongdoing, or claim immunity from correction. Church life requires responsible leadership and responsible membership.

10. Church discipline and restoration

Some assume love means never confronting sin. Others use discipline as a weapon to silence questions or protect leaders. Neither approach reflects Christ's teaching.

Jesus gives a process for addressing a brother or sister who sins: speak privately first; if there is no repentance, take one or two others; if the matter remains unresolved, tell it to the church (Matthew 18:15–20). The aim is to gain the person, not humiliate them. The process should be patient, truthful, proportionate, and attentive to evidence.

Paul instructs the Corinthian church to remove from its fellowship a man who persisted in serious, public immorality without repentance (1 Corinthians 5). Yet discipline is not vindictive. In 2 Corinthians 2:5–11, Paul urges the church to forgive and comfort a disciplined person who has repented, so that he is not overwhelmed by excessive sorrow.

Discipline seeks the honour of Christ, the purity and protection of the church, the warning of others, and the restoration of the sinner. It must never be used to punish someone for raising a legitimate concern, reporting abuse, asking for accountability, or disagreeing over a matter of conscience. Allegations of abuse or criminal conduct require appropriate safeguarding and civil authorities; church processes must not conceal wrongdoing or obstruct justice.

11. Worship and the gathering of the church

The church gathers to worship God, not to perform for an audience. The gathered church reads Scripture, hears it preached, prays, sings, gives, observes the ordinances, and encourages one another (Acts 2:42; 1 Timothy 4:13; 1 Corinthians 14:26).

Preaching has a central place because God uses his Word to teach, correct, encourage, and equip his people. The preacher must explain the text faithfully and apply it carefully. Authority belongs to God's Word, not the preacher's personality.

Congregational singing is mutual ministry. Christians sing to the Lord and to one another, letting the word of Christ dwell richly among them (Colossians 3:16; Ephesians 5:18–20). Music should serve truthful worship, not manipulate emotions or draw attention away from God. Different cultures and musical forms may be used, provided the content is true and fitting.

Prayer expresses dependence on God. Public prayer should reflect the concerns Scripture gives the church: God's glory, the spread of the gospel, the holiness of believers, the suffering, and the good of rulers and neighbours (Matthew 6:9–13; 1 Timothy 2:1–4). Christians are commanded not to neglect meeting together, but to encourage one another towards love and good works (Hebrews 10:24–25).

12. Fellowship, holiness, and unity

Christian fellowship is more than friendliness or spending time with people who share our interests. It is shared life in Christ. Believers are joined to one another because they belong to the same Lord. They bear burdens, encourage the weak, forgive as they have been forgiven, and use their gifts to serve (Galatians 6:1–2; Ephesians 4:1–6, 32; 1 Peter 4:10).

Unity does not mean pretending doctrine is unimportant. The church must preserve the unity of the Spirit and grow into maturity so that it is not carried about by every wind of teaching (Ephesians 4:1–16). Truth and love belong together. We must not sacrifice the gospel for superficial peace, nor use doctrinal conviction as an excuse for harshness and pride.

Holiness is a shared calling. The church is to be distinct from the world in worship, conduct, relationships, and loyalties. This does not mean withdrawing from neighbours or treating unbelievers with contempt. It means refusing the world's patterns of greed, immorality, dishonesty, vengeance, and self-exaltation. Conflict will sometimes arise; Scripture calls believers to speak truth, listen, pursue reconciliation, and forgive, without using unity to silence correction or protect destructive behaviour.

13. The mission of the church

The risen Lord sends his church into the world. Jesus commands his disciples to make disciples of all nations, baptising them and teaching them to observe everything he commanded (Matthew 28:18–20). This mission includes proclaiming the gospel, calling people to repentance and faith, baptising disciples, and teaching them to live under Christ's authority.

The church must be a witnessing people. Jesus promised that his followers would receive power from the Holy Spirit and be his witnesses (Acts 1:8). The gospel is not merely advice for improving life. It is the announcement that the holy God saves sinners through the death and resurrection of his Son, and commands all people everywhere to repent and believe (Acts 17:30–31; 1 Corinthians 15:1–4).

Mission does not end with a moment of response. The church teaches, baptises, nurtures, sends, and supports disciples. It trains believers to speak the truth, live faithfully, and take the gospel to those who have not heard. This work belongs to the whole church, not only pastors or professional missionaries.

The church also demonstrates God's mercy through practical love. Christians care for the poor, sick, vulnerable, and distressed (James 1:27; Galatians 6:10). Such works do not replace the gospel or earn salvation; they adorn the gospel and express love for neighbours made in God's image. The church must not confuse its mission with gaining political power or building a cultural empire. Its distinctive commission is to make disciples through the gospel, teaching obedience to Christ.

14. Authority, conscience, and accountability

Because Christ alone is Lord of the church, human authority must remain limited and accountable. Pastors have real responsibility to teach, lead, and shepherd, but they do not own the consciences of believers. Their authority is ministerial: they serve by explaining and applying God's Word. They may not bind the conscience where Scripture has not spoken, demand unquestioning loyalty, or place preferences on the level of divine commands.

Churches should make decisions transparently and honestly. Finances should be handled with integrity and appropriate accountability. Leaders should be above reproach and open to correction. Members should be able to raise concerns without intimidation. Serious allegations require fair investigation and proper safeguarding.

Each local congregation is directly accountable to Christ under Scripture; no outside organisation or church hierarchy may take Christ's place as its head. Churches may cooperate in missions, teaching, mutual aid, and counsel, but such cooperation should serve rather than erase the responsibility of each congregation before the Lord.

Christian liberty does not mean every preference is equally wise or that believers may disregard the good of others. Scripture calls us to consider our neighbours and pursue what builds up the church (Romans 14:1–19; 1 Corinthians 10:23–33). But liberty also means human traditions must not be imposed as God's law. The Word of God, not a leader's personal standard, is final authority.

15. Common misunderstandings about the church

“The church is just a building.”

Buildings can serve the church, but they are not the church. The church is the people Christ has redeemed and gathered. A congregation may meet in a home, rented room, school, or purpose-built building without changing its essential identity.

“I can follow Jesus without belonging to a church.”

Illness, persecution, distance, or other serious circumstances may prevent attendance for a season. But deliberate, long-term isolation is not the New Testament pattern. Christ joins believers to his body, where they worship, serve, receive care, and encourage one another.

“The pastor is the church's unquestionable authority.”

Christ alone is head of the church. Pastors are under-shepherds who must meet biblical qualifications and remain accountable to Scripture. Members should respect faithful leaders, but no leader is above correction or entitled to control another believer's conscience.

“A large church must be a faithful church.”

Numerical growth and visible success do not prove doctrinal faithfulness. A church must be judged by its teaching, worship, ordinances, character, care, and obedience to Christ. Small congregations can be faithful, and large congregations can drift.

“Church discipline is unloving.”

Discipline can be abused, but Christ commands a process intended for truth, protection, repentance, and restoration. Refusing to address destructive sin is not love. Faithful discipline must be humble and fair, and must never conceal abuse or silence legitimate concerns.

“The church's mission is mainly social improvement.”

Mercy and good works matter, but the Great Commission centres on making disciples through the gospel, baptism, and teaching obedience to Christ. The church should serve its neighbours without losing the message it has been sent to proclaim.

16. What faithful church life looks like

If you are looking for a congregation, do not begin only with the building, music, activities, or popularity of the preacher. Ask whether the gospel is clearly proclaimed, Scripture is taught in context, Christ is honoured, and the congregation seeks to obey the Word.

Look for leaders whose lives and teaching are consistent with 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1. Ask how the church understands baptism and the Lord's Supper, how members are cared for, how decisions are made, and how concerns about sin or leadership are handled. A healthy church will not claim to be perfect, but it should be willing to explain its convictions and submit its practices to Scripture.

If you are already part of a church, consider your own responsibility. Are you gathering faithfully? Do you receive the Word with humility? Do you pray for leaders and fellow members? Are you using your gifts to serve, or treating church as a service you consume? Are you pursuing reconciliation where there is conflict? Do you support the spread of the gospel?

Church life is sustained through ordinary faithfulness: the Word preached and obeyed, prayers offered, ordinances observed, burdens shared, sins confessed, forgiveness extended, the vulnerable cared for, and the gospel proclaimed. Do not expect a church without weakness, but do not settle for a church that refuses correction or treats Scripture as secondary.

17. Questions Christians often ask

Must every Christian be a member of a local church?

The New Testament pattern is that believers are gathered into identifiable congregations where they worship, receive teaching, share the ordinances, serve, and care for one another. Exceptional circumstances may prevent regular participation, but a settled refusal to belong to any church conflicts with ordinary Christian discipleship.

Can a church exist without a pastor?

A congregation may pass through a season without an appointed pastor, and churches can be at different stages of maturity. But Christ intends his churches to be shepherded by qualified elders. A congregation without elders should seek faithful, orderly provision rather than treating the absence of pastoral oversight as an ideal.

Can women serve and teach in the church?

Yes. Women are vital members of Christ's body and serve through prayer, evangelism, discipleship, hospitality, mercy, and teaching other women and children. The question of who may hold the governing and teaching office of elder must be answered from the relevant biblical texts, especially 1 Timothy 2:11–3:7 and Titus 1:5–9. Distinguishing roles does not imply unequal worth before God.

Should a Christian leave an unhealthy church?

Not every disappointment or disagreement justifies leaving. Christians should seek peace, speak truth, pursue correction, and avoid needless division. However, persistent false teaching, serious unrepentant misconduct, abuse, or refusal to submit to Scripture may make departure necessary. Seek wise counsel, protect those at risk, and do not allow appeals to loyalty to keep people in danger.

Is a church the same as a denomination?

No. A denomination is a wider association of congregations that share convictions and cooperate in ministry. A local church is a gathered congregation with its own membership and responsibilities under Christ. Associations can encourage accountability and cooperation, but they do not replace the local church or become its head.

What if I cannot find a faithful church nearby?

Continue seeking carefully, ask mature Christians for help, and examine teaching and practice by Scripture. Where distance or serious circumstances make gathering difficult, pursue every reasonable opportunity for Christian fellowship while seeking a stable congregation. Online content is not a complete replacement for embodied church life.

18. Christ's church and our commitment

The church is not ours to redesign according to personal taste. It belongs to Jesus Christ, who loved it and gave himself for it. He gathers his people through the gospel, joins them together as one body, gives them his Word and ordinances, and appoints shepherds to care for them. He calls the whole congregation to worship, holiness, mutual love, and mission.

This gives us reasons for commitment and humility. We should not idolise a congregation, pastor, denomination, or ministry. Every human church is imperfect and must remain under Scripture. Yet neither should we treat the church lightly. Christ's people are precious to him, and he has given the gathered church an important place in the life of every believer.

Seek a church where the gospel is clear, Scripture is faithfully taught, the ordinances are rightly practised, leaders are qualified and accountable, members care for one another, and making disciples is taken seriously. Then do not remain a spectator. Join in the worship, service, fellowship, holiness, and witness to which Christ calls his people.

The church's hope is not its own strength. Its hope is the Lord who promised to build it and who will present it to himself in splendour. Until that day, let every congregation hear his Word, follow his commands, love his people, and proclaim his gospel.

Passages for further study

Read these passages in context as you study the church's identity, worship, leadership, ordinances, discipline, and mission.

  • Matthew 16:13–20; 18:15–20; 28:18–20 — Christ's church, discipline, and the Great Commission.
  • Acts 2:37–47; 6:1–7; 14:21–23; 20:17–35 — The gathered church, service, and shepherding.
  • Romans 12:3–13; 14:1–19 — Members serving one another and Christian liberty.
  • 1 Corinthians 5; 10:14–22; 11:17–34; 12:12–27; 14:26–40 — Discipline, the Supper, the body, and gathered worship.
  • Ephesians 1:15–23; 2:11–22; 4:1–16; 5:22–33 — Christ's headship, unity, maturity, and love for the church.
  • 1 Timothy 2:1–15; 3:1–13; 4:11–16; 5:17–22 — Prayer, leadership qualifications, teaching, and care.
  • Titus 1:5–9; 2:1–10 — Elders and sound teaching in the church.
  • Hebrews 10:19–25; 13:7, 17 — Gathering, encouragement, and faithful leadership.
  • 1 Peter 2:4–12; 5:1–5 — God's people, holy witness, and shepherding.

This study seeks to distinguish the clear teaching of Scripture from areas where Christians differ in interpretation and practice. The church must continually test its doctrine and life by the Word of God, under the lordship of Jesus Christ.

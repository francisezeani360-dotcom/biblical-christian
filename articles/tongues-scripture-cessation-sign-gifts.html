Tongues, Scripture, and the Cessation of the Sign Gifts

A biblical examination of the gift of tongues, its purpose in the early church, and the question of whether it continues today.
An educational theological essay · Scripture references and further reading included
Purpose and approach. This study examines what Scripture teaches about tongues, spiritual gifts, the apostolic foundation of the church, and the work of the Holy Spirit. Its conclusions are argued from the biblical text, with historical sources offered as supporting context rather than as the authority for Christian belief. Readers are encouraged to examine the passages in context and test every claim by Scripture.

The question of tongues is often framed as a choice between believing in the power of the Holy Spirit and believing that certain miraculous gifts belonged especially to the apostolic age. That is a false choice. A Christian may confess the Spirit's present power while holding that particular revelatory and sign gifts served a foundational purpose in the establishment of the church.

The central question is not whether God can act extraordinarily. He is sovereign and free. The question is whether Scripture teaches that the extraordinary sign gifts given in the foundational apostolic period were intended to continue as ordinary features of church life throughout the whole age.

The biblical case developed here is that tongues were genuine gifts of the Spirit, served a sign function in the history of redemption, were regulated for the church's edification, and were not given to every believer. When these passages are read together with the teaching about the apostolic foundation, they support the conclusion that these particular sign gifts had a foundational purpose in the early church.

1. What does the New Testament mean by tongues?

Acts 2 provides the clearest starting point. When the Spirit came at Pentecost, the disciples spoke in other tongues, and visitors from many regions heard them speaking in their own native languages. Luke names the peoples represented. The miracle was not merely that the disciples produced ecstatic or unintelligible sounds; the hearers recognised languages they knew. They heard the mighty works of God being declared in their own tongues (Acts 2:6–11).

This event has a redemptive-historical significance. The gospel was being announced across linguistic and national boundaries. Pentecost signals that the good news of Christ is for the nations. It also recalls Babel: where human pride had been met with the confusion of languages, God now gathers people from many languages through the proclamation of one gospel.

In 1 Corinthians 12–14, Paul treats tongues as a genuine spiritual gift, but insists that gifts must build up the church. In the gathered congregation, speech that is not understood cannot edify others unless it is interpreted. Paul therefore says that only two or at most three should speak, each in turn, and that someone must interpret. If there is no interpreter, the speaker must remain silent in the church (1 Corinthians 14:27–28).

Paul does not commend disorder or unintelligibility as a spiritual virtue. He regulates the gift for the good of the body. “God is not a God of confusion but of peace” (1 Corinthians 14:33). The chapter's repeated concern is intelligible edification, not spectacle.

Some interpreters understand 1 Corinthians 14:2 as describing a private heavenly prayer language. But the immediate context contrasts speech the congregation cannot understand with prophecy that builds the church up. The fact that hearers do not understand a language does not prove it is not a human language; it demonstrates why interpretation is necessary. Even if one concludes that some Corinthian tongues were not ordinary human languages, that alone does not establish that every believer should cultivate a private stream of syllables or that tongues are the necessary evidence of Spirit baptism.

2. Tongues were a sign, not a universal test of being a Christian

Paul directly challenges the idea that every Christian receives the same gift. In 1 Corinthians 12:29–30 he asks: “Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Are all teachers? Do all work miracles? Do all possess gifts of healing? Do all speak with tongues? Do all interpret?” The expected answer is no. The Spirit distributes gifts according to his will (1 Corinthians 12:11).

The body has many members, and their gifts differ. No single gift is the universal badge of spiritual life or maturity. The New Testament directs believers to look to Christ, the fruit of the Spirit, faith, love, and perseverance—not to one particular manifestation as the proof that someone has received the Spirit.

Acts records occasions when the Spirit's coming was accompanied by visible signs. These moments occur at important stages in the expansion of the gospel: Jerusalem, Samaria, the Gentiles, and people associated with John the Baptist. They demonstrate that the same Spirit incorporates diverse peoples into the one church. Narrative events must be read alongside Paul's explicit teaching that not all believers possess the same gifts.

3. Why the apostolic foundation matters

Ephesians 2:19–22 describes the household of God as “built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets,” with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone. A foundation is laid at the beginning of a building project; it is not repeatedly relaid as though the structure never advances. The apostles had a unique, unrepeatable role as authorised witnesses of the risen Christ and foundational ministers of the new-covenant church.

Hebrews 2:3–4 connects the message of salvation with its confirmation by those who heard the Lord, while God bore witness through signs, wonders, various miracles, and gifts of the Holy Spirit. The passage does not by itself settle every question about the duration of every gift, but it places signs within the historical confirmation of the apostolic testimony.

Taken together, these passages point to a distinctive foundational role for revelatory and sign gifts. The apostles were appointed as foundational witnesses to Christ, and their testimony was confirmed in the period in which the church was established. The church continues to be built, but it is built on the foundation already laid—not on a succession of new apostles or repeated foundations.

4. What about 1 Corinthians 13 and “the perfect”?

Those who believe the gifts continue commonly argue that “when the perfect comes” in 1 Corinthians 13:10 refers to Christ's return or the final state, and therefore tongues and prophecy must continue until then. Others understand the immediate passage to contrast partial modes of revelation with the fullness or maturity toward which they point. The text's language of knowing “in part” and then knowing more fully invites careful interpretation; it should not be reduced to a slogan.

There is disagreement among those who hold this view about how this passage relates to the timing of the gifts' cessation. The argument made here does not depend on claiming that “the perfect” simply means the completed New Testament canon. Rather, the broader argument draws on the apostolic foundation, the confirmatory function of signs, the nature of revelation, and the historical pattern of the church. The interpretation of 1 Corinthians 13 should be made in the context of the whole argument of chapters 12–14.

5. What does church history show?

Historical claims should be made carefully. It is not accurate to say that nobody anywhere reported tongues after the apostles. Irenaeus, writing in the second century, refers to people in the church who speak in various kinds of languages through the Spirit. His testimony deserves attention. By itself, however, it does not establish that the New Testament gift continued uninterrupted as a widespread, ordinary feature of Christian worship.

Later writers are often cited in discussions about the history of tongues. John Chrysostom, commenting on 1 Corinthians, speaks of the obscurity surrounding the relevant gifts in his own day and treats them as belonging to an earlier period. Augustine also reflects on the sign of languages in relation to the church's beginnings and the spread of the gospel among the nations. Their comments are not a substitute for exegesis, but they are significant witnesses to how prominent Christian teachers understood the gifts in later centuries.

There have been scattered reports of extraordinary speech and prophetic phenomena in different periods, including among groups whose teachings were disputed by the wider church. The existence of a report is not the same as proof that it is identical to the apostolic gift. Each claim must be examined in its context, and the historical record should not be made to say more than the sources permit.

A careful summary of the historical evidence is therefore modest: the New Testament gift does not appear as a stable, universally recognised feature of ordinary church life across the centuries; several influential later writers describe it as having ceased or as belonging to the church's earlier period; and the modern Pentecostal movement developed in a distinct form in the early twentieth century.

6. The modern Pentecostal movement and its historical origins

Modern Pentecostalism is diverse, and not every Pentecostal or charismatic church teaches exactly the same thing. Nevertheless, its recognisable modern beginnings are commonly associated with the Bible school led by Charles F. Parham in Topeka, Kansas, and the events surrounding Agnes Ozman in 1901. The Azusa Street revival in Los Angeles, associated with William J. Seymour from 1906, became a major centre for the movement's international spread.

This historical background does not prove that every modern claim is false. Nor does a movement's recent institutional origin automatically settle a theological question. It does, however, challenge the claim that modern Pentecostal practice has been continuously and plainly visible in the church in the same form from the apostolic age onward. The biblical question remains decisive: does the practice match the gift and the instructions described in Scripture?

7. Common objections and biblical responses

“God has not changed, so the gifts have not ceased.”

God has not changed in his being, power, or faithfulness. But he has administered his redemptive plan through different historical stages. The sacrificial system and priesthood were instituted by God and later fulfilled in Christ. Their ending did not mean God had changed; it meant their appointed purpose had been fulfilled. The question is whether a particular gift had a particular role in a particular stage of redemptive history.

“First Corinthians 14 describes a private prayer language.”

The chapter's main concern is the edification of the gathered church. Paul requires interpretation, limits the number of speakers, and commands silence when no interpreter is present. The passage cannot justify disorderly or uninterpreted public vocalisation, nor does it teach that all believers should speak in tongues.

“Tongues are the evidence of Spirit baptism.”

Acts records significant moments in the expansion of the gospel. Those narratives must be interpreted alongside 1 Corinthians 12:29–30, where Paul expects the answer “no” to the question whether all speak in tongues. A sign accompanying some pivotal events is not thereby made a universal requirement for every Christian.

“Questioning tongues quenches the Spirit.”

Scripture commands believers not to quench the Spirit, but also to test everything and hold fast what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:19–22). It tells believers to test the spirits (1 John 4:1). Testing a spiritual claim is not unbelief; it is obedience.

“People are genuinely changed after speaking in tongues.”

People may experience relief, strong emotion, community belonging, or renewed religious commitment in charismatic settings. Some are sincere Christians who love Christ. None of this, by itself, proves that a vocal phenomenon is the New Testament gift. Experiences must be evaluated by Scripture rather than authenticated by emotional intensity.

“those who hold this view deny miracles.”

The view defended in this study is that certain revelatory and sign gifts associated with the apostolic foundation are not promised as ordinary features of church life today. This does not deny God's providence, answered prayer, divine healing, or his freedom to act extraordinarily. God remains sovereign.

8. How should modern claims be tested?

The standard is not whether a meeting feels powerful, whether a speaker is sincere, or whether a congregation is growing. The standard is the Word of God.

  • If someone claims the gift described in Acts 2, ask whether an actual language is being spoken and whether competent hearers can identify it.
  • If someone claims a public tongue in the church, ask whether the biblical requirements of order and interpretation are followed.
  • If someone teaches that every Christian must speak in tongues, compare that claim with 1 Corinthians 12:29–30.
  • If someone claims a private prayer language, ask where Scripture clearly commands or promises that practice to all believers.

This is not an invitation to mock people, imitate their speech, or assume that everyone involved is consciously pretending. Some may be repeating patterns they have been taught; others may sincerely interpret an unusual experience as a gift from God. We should be truthful about practices while remaining charitable toward people.

Charity, however, must not become silence. If a teaching burdens consciences, creates a spiritual hierarchy, or attributes an unbiblical phenomenon to the Holy Spirit, it must be challenged. The church is not served by pretending every religious experience is harmless.

9. The Holy Spirit's work in the church today

A the view argued here does not believe the church is spiritually abandoned or that Christians must live on memories of the apostolic age. The Holy Spirit is fully active in the church. He gives new birth, unites believers to Christ, assures them of adoption, illuminates Scripture, empowers prayer, produces holiness, distributes gifts for service, and equips the church for ministry.

The Spirit's ordinary work is not less supernatural because it is not spectacular. The resurrection of a spiritually dead sinner is a greater work than the production of an impressive religious experience. A person who once hated God and loved sin, but now trusts Christ, loves holiness, forgives enemies, and perseveres in faith, is evidence of the Spirit's power.

The Spirit glorifies Christ (John 16:14). He inspired the Scriptures and works through them. The church should pray earnestly, seek holiness, use its gifts faithfully, and depend entirely on the Spirit. None of this requires claiming that the apostolic sign gifts continue as ordinary practices.

10. Conclusion: Scripture must judge the experience

The central issue is authority. Does Scripture define the gift, or do modern experiences redefine Scripture? Does the church submit its practices to apostolic teaching, or reinterpret apostolic teaching to defend practices it has already embraced?

The biblical gift of tongues was not a badge of superior spirituality. It was distributed by the Spirit, regulated by the apostle, and intended for the good of the church. At Pentecost it served as a sign that the gospel was going to the nations. In Corinth it had to be interpreted so the congregation could be edified. Paul never taught that every believer must possess it.

The conclusion argued for here is that the extraordinary sign gifts belonged to the foundational apostolic period and are not promised as ordinary features of the church throughout the present age. The historical record is not a simple story of absolute silence after the apostles: there are scattered early reports. But later writers explicitly describe tongues as having passed away, and the modern Pentecostal movement's distinctive form emerged in the early twentieth century. That history supports, but does not independently prove, the biblical case.

We should refuse both extremes: deny neither God's power nor the need to test unusual claims. We should not ridicule sincere people, and we should not allow sincerity to replace discernment. Our assurance rests not on an experience, but on Jesus Christ, who saves sinners by grace through faith. The gospel is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes (Romans 1:16).

Let the Word of Christ dwell richly among us. Let worship be reverent and intelligible. Let every gift serve the body in love. And let no experience, however moving, be placed above the authority of Scripture.

Scripture must remain the final authority by which every teaching, tradition, and spiritual experience is examined.

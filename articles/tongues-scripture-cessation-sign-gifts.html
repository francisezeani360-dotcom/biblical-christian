What the Bible Says About Tongues

The New Testament gift, the modern practice, the history behind the movement, and the responsibility of the church to test every spiritual claim.
A biblical and historical examination · Scripture references and further reading included

Few subjects in the life of the church prompt as many questions, experiences, and strongly held convictions as speaking in tongues. For some Christians, it is an important part of prayer and worship. Others have encountered it in church meetings, heard different explanations of what it means, or wondered why Christians understand the subject so differently. Many simply want to know what the Bible says.

When the New Testament speaks about tongues, what exactly is being described? What happened when the disciples spoke in tongues at Pentecost? Why did Paul give the Corinthians such detailed instructions about their use in the church? How should we understand the relationship between tongues, the Holy Spirit, prayer, and the building up of believers?

These are not merely historical questions. They affect how Christians worship, how they understand the work of the Holy Spirit, and how they evaluate claims about spiritual gifts today. Personal experiences and the teaching of trusted leaders can shape our convictions, but Scripture calls us to examine spiritual matters carefully and hold fast to what is true (1 John 4:1; 1 Thessalonians 5:21).

In the pages that follow, we will trace the subject through the relevant biblical passages, consider the purpose and practice of tongues in the early church, look at how Christians have understood the gift throughout history, and examine claims made about the phenomenon in modern times. Along the way, we will take common questions and objections seriously and seek to understand the passages in their proper context.

Whether you have spoken in tongues, belong to a church where it is practised, have questions about it, or have never encountered it before, you are invited to consider the evidence carefully. The question is not what we have been accustomed to believe, or what we would most like the answer to be, but what God has made known in his Word.

1. What do we mean by speaking in tongues?

The English word tongue can refer to the organ of speech or to a language. The Greek word glōssa has the same range of meaning. In the New Testament, therefore, the phrase “speaking in tongues” refers to a form of speech described as tongues or languages. The question is what kind of speech the gift involved and whether the modern phenomenon is the same thing.

Two terms are often used in this discussion. Xenolalia refers to speaking a real human language that the speaker has not learned. Glossolalia is the term commonly used for religious speech consisting of syllables that are not recognisable as an ordinary human language. Acts 2 clearly describes recognisable languages. The precise nature of the Corinthian gift is debated, because Paul discusses speech that requires interpretation and is not understood by the congregation.

We should be careful not to claim more than the text says. Yet uncertainty about some details in Corinth does not give us permission to identify any unintelligible religious speech as the biblical gift. The practice must be measured against the whole of Paul's teaching: its source, purpose, interpretation, use in the church, and contribution to the understanding and edification of others.

2. What happened at Pentecost?

Acts 2 gives us the clearest description of tongues. When the Holy Spirit came upon the disciples at Pentecost, they began to speak in other tongues as the Spirit gave them utterance. Jews from many nations were in Jerusalem, and each heard the disciples speaking in his own native language. Luke names the regions represented, and the hearers recognised that they were hearing the mighty works of God in their own languages (Acts 2:1–11).

The miracle was not simply that the disciples made unusual sounds. People from different language communities understood what was being said. Galileans were proclaiming God's mighty works in languages they had not learned. The repeated emphasis on the hearers' own languages makes the nature of the event clear: Pentecost involved intelligible human languages.

This event also belongs to the unfolding history of redemption. At Babel, human rebellion was judged through the confusion of languages and the scattering of peoples (Genesis 11:1–9). At Pentecost, people from many nations heard the mighty works of God proclaimed. The gospel does not erase the nations or make everyone culturally identical; it gathers people from every nation into one people through Jesus Christ.

Notice, too, where the sign leads. Peter does not invite the crowd to pursue the experience or to measure their spirituality by reproducing it. He preaches the death, resurrection, and lordship of Jesus Christ (Acts 2:14–36). The sign draws attention to the apostolic message. When the people are convicted, Peter calls them to repent and be baptised in the name of Jesus Christ (Acts 2:37–41). The centre of Pentecost is the risen Christ proclaimed in the power of the Spirit.

This gives us a fair question to ask whenever the gift of Acts 2 is claimed today: are identifiable human languages being spoken by people who have not learned them, and can speakers of those languages understand them? If someone claims that an utterance is Arabic, Chinese, or another human language, that claim can be examined by competent speakers. An impression that speech sounds foreign, or a later guess about what it supposedly meant, is not sufficient evidence of the miracle Luke describes.

3. What did Paul teach the Corinthians?

First Corinthians 12–14 is essential because Paul addresses a church in which spiritual gifts had become entangled with pride, rivalry, and disorder. He reminds the Corinthians that gifts come from the same Spirit, are distributed according to his will, and are given for the common good (1 Corinthians 12:4–11). No Christian should use a gift to exalt himself or make another believer feel inferior. The church is one body with many members, and every member matters (1 Corinthians 12:12–27).

Paul then places love above every gift. Even extraordinary speech, knowledge, faith, and sacrifice are nothing without love (1 Corinthians 13:1–3). That is a searching test for any spiritual practice. Does it lead us to love and serve others, or does it become a way to display our experience and distinguish ourselves from other believers?

In chapter 14, Paul repeatedly stresses understanding and edification. Prophecy strengthens, encourages, and comforts the congregation because people understand what is said. Uninterpreted tongues do not build up the gathered church in the same way, because the hearers cannot understand the message (1 Corinthians 14:1–5). Paul compares speech to musical instruments and a trumpet: if the sound is unclear, no one can know what tune is being played or what signal is being given (1 Corinthians 14:6–12).

Paul's own preference is striking. Although he says he speaks in tongues more than the Corinthians, he would rather speak five intelligible words in the church to instruct others than ten thousand words in a tongue (1 Corinthians 14:18–19). He is not dismissing the gift as it existed in his day. He is showing that public worship must be governed by the good of the congregation, not by the speaker's desire to express something extraordinary.

Paul's instructions are specific. Only two or at most three may speak in a tongue in the gathering, each in turn, and someone must interpret. If there is no interpreter, the speaker must remain silent in the church (1 Corinthians 14:27–28). These commands are not optional details. They show that the gift was never a licence for everyone to speak at once or for unintelligible speech to fill the meeting without interpretation.

Some understand 1 Corinthians 14:2 as teaching a private prayer language, because Paul says that the tongue-speaker speaks to God and that no one understands him. But the chapter is addressing the church's use of gifts, and Paul's concern throughout is the edification of the gathered body. The passage does not command every believer to develop a private tongue, nor does it establish modern glossolalia as a practice beyond examination. Even if one understands the Corinthian gift as speech not ordinarily understood by those present, Paul still requires interpretation when it is used publicly.

Nor does 1 Corinthians 14:14 teach that setting the mind aside is a higher form of prayer. Paul says that if he prays in a tongue, his spirit prays but his mind is unfruitful. He then gives his conclusion: he will pray and sing with both spirit and mind (1 Corinthians 14:15). His aim is not mindless worship, but worship in which the whole person is engaged and the church can understand and say “Amen” (1 Corinthians 14:16–19).

Paul closes by insisting that God is not a God of confusion but of peace, and that everything must be done decently and in order (1 Corinthians 14:33, 40). These instructions deserve to be taken seriously by every church, whatever its tradition. A practice that regularly ignores them cannot be defended by appealing to the same chapter.

4. What was the purpose of the sign?

In 1 Corinthians 14:20–25, Paul quotes Isaiah 28:11–12 and explains that tongues are a sign for unbelievers. In Isaiah's setting, foreign speech was associated with God's judgment on a people who had refused to listen to his word. The strange language of foreign invaders was a sign that judgment had come. Paul's use of this passage gives tongues a serious redemptive-historical function; they are not presented as a badge of spiritual achievement or a way to create an atmosphere in worship.

The passage also warns against confusing a sign with the ordinary work of building up the church. If an outsider enters a gathering where everyone is speaking unintelligibly, that person may conclude that the congregation is mad. When God's truth is clearly proclaimed, however, the hearer can be convicted and brought to recognise God's presence (1 Corinthians 14:23–25). Paul wants worship that communicates truth.

Hebrews 2:3–4 connects signs, wonders, miracles, and gifts of the Holy Spirit with God's confirmation of the salvation first announced by the Lord and attested by those who heard him. Second Corinthians 12:12 speaks of the signs of a true apostle, performed among the Corinthians with signs, wonders, and mighty works. Taken together, these passages place miraculous signs within the unique period in which Christ's authorised witnesses were establishing and confirming the apostolic gospel.

This does not mean that God has become unable to act extraordinarily. It means that we should ask what purpose a particular gift served in the unfolding plan of God. A sign can be genuinely miraculous and still belong to a particular stage of redemptive history. The fact that God once gave a gift does not by itself prove that he has promised it as a normal feature of every later generation.

5. Tongues were never required of every Christian

Some Christians have been taught that speaking in tongues is the necessary initial evidence of receiving the Holy Spirit. If you have been told this and have struggled because you have not had the experience, consider Paul's direct questions in 1 Corinthians 12:29–30: “Do all speak with tongues?” The expected answer is no. Not all are apostles, not all are prophets, not all work miracles, and not all speak in tongues. The Spirit distributes different gifts to different members of Christ's body (1 Corinthians 12:11, 27–30).

If tongues were the required evidence that every believer had received the Spirit, Paul's teaching would be contradictory. Instead, he presents a body in which members have different gifts and different functions. No Christian should be made to feel incomplete because the Spirit has not given that person a particular gift.

The New Testament teaches that everyone who belongs to Christ has the Spirit (Romans 8:9). By one Spirit all believers are baptised into one body (1 Corinthians 12:13). The Spirit's presence is not measured by a person's ability to produce a particular vocal experience. His work is seen in faith in Christ, the fruit of holiness, love for God and neighbour, and perseverance in the truth (Galatians 5:16–25; Ephesians 1:13–14).

Acts records tongues at significant moments as the gospel moves from Jerusalem to Samaria and then to the Gentiles. These events demonstrate that people from different backgrounds are being welcomed into the one church. They are important moments in the expansion of the gospel, not a formula requiring every Christian in every place to repeat the same sign.

6. Why the sign gifts belonged to the apostolic foundation

The New Testament describes the church as a building founded on the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone (Ephesians 2:19–22). A foundation is laid at the beginning of a building's construction; it is not continually relaid as though the building had no foundation. The apostles had a unique role as witnesses of the risen Christ and authoritative messengers through whom the church received the teaching on which it is built.

This does not mean that the church no longer needs the apostles' teaching. Quite the opposite: the church continues to live under their teaching preserved in Scripture. But the foundational office and witness of the apostles are not repeated in every generation. The church is built on the foundation already laid.

Miraculous signs served to confirm the apostolic witness. Hebrews 2:3–4 describes God bearing witness to the message of salvation through signs, wonders, various miracles, and gifts of the Holy Spirit. Paul points to the signs of an apostle in 2 Corinthians 12:12. These passages do not describe miracles as mere decorations of Christian meetings; they connect them to the authentication of the gospel delivered through Christ and his authorised witnesses.

First Corinthians 13:8–10 also teaches that prophecies, tongues, and knowledge will pass away when the perfect comes. Christians differ over the precise reference of “the perfect,” and the passage should not be treated as though every interpretive question were settled by a slogan. Some understand it as the completed state of the church in glory; others connect it with the completion of the apostolic revelation. The cessationist case does not rest on this text alone. It considers it alongside the unique apostolic foundation, the confirming purpose of signs, and the way the New Testament describes the church's continuing life.

The conclusion argued here is that the extraordinary sign gifts belonged to that foundational period and are not promised as ordinary gifts for the church throughout the ages. Christians who disagree should still recognise that the foundational role of the apostles was unique and that no claimed gift today can add to, correct, or rival the authority of Scripture.

7. “When the perfect comes”

First Corinthians 13:8–13 says that prophecies will pass away, tongues will cease, and knowledge will pass away. Paul contrasts what is partial with what is complete, using the picture of childhood giving way to maturity and of seeing dimly now but face to face then. The passage points beyond the temporary and partial condition of the present age to the fullness of what God has promised.

Some continuationists argue that “the perfect” must refer to Christ's return and the final state, because believers will not see face to face until then. That is a serious argument and should not be dismissed. Cessationists respond that the passage's immediate contrast between partial revelation and completeness must be read alongside the foundational role of apostles and prophets and the purpose of confirming signs. The timing and scope of the passage remain debated among Christians who take Scripture seriously.

For that reason, the case for cessation should not be made to depend on one disputed phrase. The wider biblical pattern matters: Christ appointed foundational witnesses; their message was confirmed by signs; the church is built on their completed foundation; and the apostolic teaching remains sufficient and authoritative for the church. That cumulative case is the basis for the conclusion presented here.

8. What happened after the apostles?

Church history is useful, but it must be handled honestly. It would be inaccurate to claim that nobody ever reported tongues after the apostolic age. Irenaeus refers to people in his time speaking in various kinds of languages, and there are reports associated with movements such as the Montanists. Later Christian writers also discuss the subject. These references should be examined in their own historical settings rather than ignored.

At the same time, the historical record does not show an uninterrupted, ordinary, widely recognised continuation of the New Testament gift throughout the centuries. Some early reports are brief or difficult to interpret. Other writers describe the gift as no longer commonly present. John Chrysostom, commenting on 1 Corinthians, speaks of the obscurity of the passage because the events it describes were no longer occurring in the same way in his day. Augustine also reflects on the sign in relation to the spread of the gospel among the nations.

These sources do not settle the biblical question by themselves. History is a witness, not the final authority. But the pattern is significant: the modern Pentecostal movement did not simply continue an uninterrupted and universally recognised practice that had been central to the church in every century. Its recognisable form emerged in a particular historical setting in the early twentieth century.

9. The origins of modern Pentecostalism

The modern Pentecostal movement took recognisable shape in the early twentieth century. Charles Fox Parham's Bible school in Topeka, Kansas, became associated with a reported outpouring in 1901, during which Agnes Ozman was said to have spoken in tongues. Parham and others understood tongues to be miraculous human languages that could equip missionaries to preach without first learning those languages.

William J. Seymour, who had been influenced by Parham's teaching, became a central figure in the Azusa Street revival in Los Angeles beginning in 1906. The meetings attracted people from different backgrounds and helped spread Pentecostal teaching internationally. The movement grew rapidly and later developed a range of views about the nature and purpose of tongues.

The early missionary-language claim encountered serious practical difficulties. Missionaries who expected to preach in languages they had not learned found that their utterances were not generally understood by the people they hoped to reach. As the movement developed, many Pentecostals increasingly described tongues as heavenly speech or prayer language rather than as ordinary foreign languages. That historical change matters because Acts 2 plainly describes hearers recognising human languages.

The origins of a movement do not by themselves prove that its theology is false. Nor should the conduct or mistakes of particular leaders be used as a substitute for biblical argument. The important point is that modern practice has a traceable history and that its claims about the nature of tongues have changed. We should therefore test the practice by Scripture rather than assume that its modern form is identical to the apostolic gift.

10. What linguistic research tells us

Linguists have studied glossolalia in different religious settings. Research by William J. Samarin and later studies has examined its sounds, patterns, and relationship to known languages. A recurring finding is that the utterances studied generally do not display the vocabulary and grammatical structure of an identifiable natural language. They tend to draw on the speaker's familiar sound patterns while lacking the stable linguistic features needed for ordinary translation.

That finding should be stated carefully. Linguistic studies examine particular samples and cannot pronounce on every utterance ever made or on every claim of divine action. They do not tell us what is in a speaker's heart, and they do not prove that every participant is consciously pretending. But they do challenge the claim that the common modern phenomenon is a miraculous ability to speak real human languages unknown to the speaker.

It is also important to distinguish an interpretation offered in a meeting from a translation that can be independently checked. If an utterance is claimed to be a human language, someone competent in that language should be able to identify and assess it. If no such verification is possible, we should not present the claim as though it were the same public linguistic miracle described in Acts 2.

Research cannot decide the meaning of Scripture. It can, however, help us evaluate factual claims made about speech. The decisive standard remains the Word of God, and the evidence from language study should be considered in its proper, limited place.

11. Where common modern practice departs from Scripture

Not every Pentecostal or charismatic church practises tongues in the same way. Some insist on order and interpretation; others encourage many people to speak at once. Some teach that tongues are necessary evidence of Spirit baptism; others reject that teaching. It is important to represent people fairly. Nevertheless, wherever these practices occur, they should be examined by the apostolic instructions.

Making tongues the test of receiving the Holy Spirit

If you have been told that you must speak in tongues to prove that you have received the Holy Spirit, compare that claim with 1 Corinthians 12:29–30. Paul teaches that not all speak in tongues. The Spirit's presence is promised to all who belong to Christ (Romans 8:9), not only to those who have had a particular experience. No pastor should burden a believer's conscience with a test that the apostles did not impose.

Teaching people how to produce the sounds

In some settings, people are told to repeat a syllable, speak faster, loosen their tongues, stop thinking, or imitate sounds made by others. It is worth asking why a miraculous gift distributed by the sovereign Spirit would need to be produced through a learned technique. The New Testament describes the Spirit giving gifts as he wills (1 Corinthians 12:11), not leaders training people to reproduce the outward appearance of a gift.

Research has documented that glossolalic speech patterns can be learned and shaped through participation, imitation, and practice. That does not establish that every person involved is consciously pretending. It does show that the ability to produce these sounds is not, by itself, evidence of a supernatural gift.

Everyone speaking at once

Paul's instruction is plain: two or at most three, one at a time, with interpretation; without an interpreter, the speaker must remain silent in the church (1 Corinthians 14:27–28). A gathering in which many people speak simultaneously and no interpretation is given does not follow this instruction. A church should be willing to correct its practice when Scripture exposes a contradiction.

Calling unintelligibility a higher form of worship

Paul does not teach that the mind is an obstacle to worship. He says that prayer and singing should involve both spirit and mind (1 Corinthians 14:14–19). Christian worship is heartfelt, but it is not mindless. We should not regard confusion or unintelligibility as proof of a deeper spiritual experience.

Using tongues to create spiritual status

If those who speak in tongues are treated as more spiritual than those who do not, the church has created a division that Paul rejects. The body has many members, and none can say to another, “I have no need of you” (1 Corinthians 12:14–26). Love is the more excellent way (1 Corinthians 12:31–13:13). A believer's standing in Christ is not measured by a dramatic manifestation.

Giving messages that cannot be tested

Any message claimed to come from God must be tested. Scripture commands believers to test the spirits (1 John 4:1), test everything and hold fast what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:19–22), and weigh what is said in the gathered church (1 Corinthians 14:29). No one should demand unquestioning acceptance because a message is presented as a tongue or spiritual impression.

These are not merely differences in worship style. They concern the authority of Scripture, the consciences of believers, and the proper ordering of Christ's church. When a practice conflicts with apostolic instruction, love requires that it be corrected rather than protected because it is familiar or emotionally powerful.

12. Common objections answered

“God is the same yesterday, today, and forever. Why would tongues cease?”

God's character does not change, but his work in history includes different stages and purposes. The sacrificial system was given by God, yet it reached its fulfilment in Christ. The temple and priesthood had a real purpose, but they were not intended to remain the centre of God's covenant people after Christ's once-for-all sacrifice. A gift can be genuinely from God and still have a limited purpose in the history of redemption.

“The Bible says to desire spiritual gifts, especially prophecy.”

Paul's instruction to desire gifts is given in the context of the church's edification (1 Corinthians 12:31; 14:1). It does not mean every gift must continue in every age, nor does it authorise anyone to imitate a gift. The duration of the gifts must be considered in the light of the whole New Testament, including the unique apostolic foundation.

“Tongues are a private prayer language, so Paul's rules for public worship do not apply.”

First Corinthians 14 is written to correct the church's use of gifts, and it does not clearly command every believer to cultivate a private tongue. Paul's own conclusion is that prayer and singing should involve both spirit and mind (1 Corinthians 14:15). A disputed interpretation of one verse should not be used to place a modern practice beyond the scrutiny of the surrounding chapter.

“But speaking in tongues has been meaningful to me.”

We should not dismiss what you have experienced or pretend to know exactly what it has meant to you. An experience can feel powerful, bring emotional relief, or be closely connected with a season of renewed commitment. But the importance an experience has in our lives does not establish that our interpretation of it is correct. We all need to distinguish between what we felt and what Scripture teaches. The question is not whether the experience felt real, but whether the explanation given for it agrees with God's Word.

“People who speak in tongues have changed lives and show genuine Christian fruit.”

That may be true of many individuals. A person can sincerely love Christ, grow in holiness, and serve others while holding a mistaken belief about spiritual gifts. The presence of good fruit in someone's life does not prove every teaching associated with that person's church. We should give thanks for genuine Christian fruit while still testing the particular claim about tongues.

“You are quenching the Spirit by questioning this.”

In the same passage where Paul says not to quench the Spirit, he commands believers to test everything and hold fast what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:19–22). Testing is not opposition to the Spirit. The Spirit who inspired Scripture does not lead the church to disregard the very Word he gave.

“You are denying that God can perform miracles today.”

No. God remains able to heal, provide, protect, answer prayer, and act beyond what we can explain. Christians should pray for the sick and depend on God's wisdom. The question is not whether God can perform miracles, but whether Scripture teaches that the particular sign gift of tongues continues as an ordinary practice in the church. God's power does not need us to claim what Scripture has not promised.

“There are reports of tongues throughout church history.”

There are scattered reports, and they should not be hidden. But reports of unusual speech at different points in history do not establish an unbroken, ordinary continuation of the New Testament gift. The nature and context of those reports vary, and several influential Christian writers describe the apostolic sign as no longer commonly present. Historical evidence must be weighed carefully rather than reduced to either “nobody ever spoke” or “the gift has always continued in the same way.”

“If the modern practice is not the gift, does that mean everyone involved is deceived?”

It does not mean that every person is deliberately deceiving others. People may sincerely repeat what they were taught, interpret an intense experience through the theology of their church, or follow patterns they have seen modelled by trusted leaders. We cannot read every person's heart. But sincerity cannot make a teaching true. If Scripture shows that a belief is mistaken, the right response is to receive correction with humility, not to defend the belief simply because it has been part of our lives.

13. The Spirit's work in the church today

If the modern practice is not the New Testament gift of tongues, does that leave Christians with a weaker or less spiritual life? Not at all. The Holy Spirit is fully present and active in Christ's church. He gives new life to sinners, unites believers to Christ, assures them of adoption, produces holiness, empowers prayer, distributes gifts for service, and strengthens the church through the Word (John 3:5–8; Romans 8:1–16; 1 Corinthians 12:4–11; Galatians 5:16–25).

The Spirit's central ministry is to glorify Christ (John 16:13–14). He opens our eyes to the glory of the Son, turns us from sin, grows love and holiness in us, and equips us to serve one another. His work is not measured by how dramatic an experience appears. The fruit of the Spirit—love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control—is no small evidence of his gracious work (Galatians 5:22–23).

God also uses the ordinary means he has given to nourish his people: the reading and preaching of Scripture, prayer, the fellowship of the church, baptism, and the Lord's Supper. These are not lifeless substitutes for spiritual power. The Spirit works through the Word he inspired and forms Christ's people through the life of the church.

If you have been made to feel spiritually inferior because you do not speak in tongues, you can lay that burden down. Your hope is not an experience you must obtain, but Jesus Christ, who receives all who come to him in faith. If you do speak in tongues, you do not need to fear that examining the practice means abandoning Christ. Rather, ask the Lord to lead you into a fuller understanding of his Word and to give you the humility to follow it.

14. A direct appeal to those who speak in tongues and to church leaders

If you speak in tongues, I ask you not to dismiss this argument simply because it challenges something important to you. Please open the Scriptures and examine the passages discussed here. Ask whether the speech you practise matches the languages described in Acts 2, whether it follows Paul's instructions in 1 Corinthians 14, and whether the teaching you have received about it agrees with Paul's statement that not all Christians speak in tongues. These are fair questions, and every Christian should be willing to answer them from the Bible.

You may need to reconsider ideas you have held for a long time. That can be difficult, especially if the practice is part of your church, friendships, or personal testimony. But our deepest loyalty must be to Christ and his Word. We should not hold on to a belief merely because it is familiar, because respected people taught it to us, or because changing our minds would be uncomfortable. The Lord is kind to correct his people, and no one loses by submitting to the truth.

If you have been told that you cannot be sure you have the Holy Spirit unless you speak in tongues, do not let that fear govern your conscience. Scripture directs you to Christ. Everyone who belongs to him has the Spirit (Romans 8:9). The gospel calls you to trust in Christ's finished work, not to achieve a particular vocal experience before you may have assurance.

Pastors and church leaders have a responsibility to teach these passages faithfully. Do not pressure people to speak in tongues or imply that those who do not are second-class Christians. Do not coach people to imitate syllables as evidence of a divine gift. Do not permit public speech that ignores Paul's requirements for interpretation and order. If a claim is made that someone has miraculously spoken a foreign language, test the claim rather than accepting it on enthusiasm alone. And never allow a message supposedly from God to escape the authority and examination of Scripture.

Those who disagree with the argument in this article should answer it from the Bible and from reliable historical evidence. The aim is not to shame people or win a denominational argument. It is to help Christ's church worship in truth, with understanding, humility, love, and reverence. We should be gentle with people and uncompromising about the authority of God's Word.

15. Conclusion: Let Scripture, not experience, have the final word

The New Testament gift of tongues was a genuine work of the Spirit, not a technique for producing religious sounds or a badge of superior spirituality. At Pentecost, people heard God's mighty works in recognisable languages. In Corinth, Paul required interpretation, order, and concern for the understanding and edification of the church. He plainly taught that not all Christians speak in tongues.

The church was built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, and signs confirmed the gospel proclaimed by Christ's authorised witnesses. The case presented in this article is that the extraordinary sign gifts associated with that foundational period are not promised as ordinary features of the church today. Although Christians debate some details of the cessation passages, that debate cannot justify practices that contradict Paul's clear instructions.

History does not support the claim that nobody ever reported tongues after the apostles. There were scattered reports and occasional episodes. But they do not establish an unbroken, mainstream continuation of the New Testament gift. The modern Pentecostal movement took recognisable shape in the early twentieth century, especially through Parham's Topeka Bible school and Seymour's Azusa Street revival. Its early claims that tongues were miraculous foreign languages for missionary work encountered serious difficulties, and the movement's understanding of tongues developed over time.

Linguistic research into modern glossolalia has found that the utterances studied do not function as identifiable natural languages. This does not reveal the motives or spiritual condition of every speaker, but it challenges the claim that the common modern phenomenon is the same kind of miraculous human-language speech described at Pentecost. Above all, the practice must be tested by Scripture: the Spirit distributes gifts as he wills, not every believer has the same gift, and gathered worship must be intelligible and orderly.

So if you have believed that modern unintelligible speech is the New Testament gift, I urge you to reconsider that belief in the light of the passages examined here. Do not let familiarity, pressure, or fear keep you from following Scripture. If you have been told that tongues are necessary to prove you have the Spirit, reject that burden and rest in Christ. If you lead a church, teach your people what the apostles actually wrote and bring every practice under the authority of God's Word.

We should never mock those who have been taught differently, assume that every practitioner is dishonest, or deny God's present power. But neither should we call a practice biblical simply because it is widespread, emotionally meaningful, or sincerely held. The Spirit of God does not contradict the Word he inspired. Let us therefore seek to honour Christ through faith, love, holiness, sound doctrine, and worship that builds up his people.

“Let all things be done for building up.” (1 Corinthians 14:26)

May the Lord give us hearts that are humble enough to be corrected, minds that are eager to understand his Word, and love that seeks the spiritual good of others. Truth and charity belong together.

This article argues for a cessationist reading of the biblical evidence. It recognises that Christians differ over the interpretation and duration of certain gifts, and distinguishes biblical conclusions from historical and linguistic observations. Historical reports should be checked in their original context. The argument concerns the biblical status of a practice, not a judgement about the sincerity or salvation of every person associated with it.

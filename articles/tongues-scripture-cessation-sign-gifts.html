Biblical teaching · Spiritual gifts · Church history

What the Bible Says About Tongues

The New Testament gift, the modern practice, the history behind the movement, and the responsibility of the church to test every spiritual claim.
A biblical and historical examination · Scripture references and further reading included
A word about the purpose of this study. This article argues that the miraculous sign gift of tongues belonged to the foundational apostolic era and that the common modern practice of speaking in unintelligible syllables should not be presented as the New Testament gift or encouraged as a normal mark of Christian spirituality. That conclusion is reached by examining Scripture, the historical record, and linguistic research. It is not an accusation that every person who speaks this way is dishonest, insincere, or outside the Christian faith. People must be treated with patience and respect; teachings and practices, however, must be tested without fear.

Speaking in tongues has become one of the most recognisable features of modern Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity. In many churches, people are encouraged to speak in sounds that no one present can understand. Sometimes the practice is described as a heavenly language, a private prayer language, the evidence of baptism in the Holy Spirit, or a sign of unusual spiritual power. In some meetings, many people speak at once. In others, a leader urges the congregation to repeat syllables, make sounds, or release what is supposedly already within them.

These practices are often defended by appealing to the book of Acts and Paul's first letter to the Corinthians. But a biblical reference does not automatically validate a modern practice. We must ask what the apostles meant, what they commanded, what they forbade, and whether what is happening today actually resembles what Scripture describes.

The question is not whether God is powerful. He is. The question is not whether the Holy Spirit is active. He is. Nor is the question whether God could miraculously enable someone to speak a language they have never learned. God can do whatever pleases him. The question is whether the New Testament teaches that the sign gift of tongues continues as a normal practice in the church today, and whether the sounds commonly called tongues in modern meetings are the same gift.

The case presented here is that biblical tongues were a genuine, Spirit-given sign associated with the foundational expansion of the gospel; that they were subject to strict apostolic regulation; that they were not given to every Christian; and that the New Testament's teaching about the apostolic foundation and the purpose of signs supports their cessation with the completion of that foundational era. The historical record does not justify the careless claim that no one anywhere ever reported tongues after the apostles. It does show that the modern movement, in its recognisable form, arose in the early twentieth century, after many centuries in which the phenomenon was rare, disputed, or understood differently.

1. What do we mean by speaking in tongues?

The English word tongue can refer to the physical organ in the mouth or to a language. In the New Testament, the Greek word glōssa can carry either meaning. The phrase translated “speaking in tongues” therefore concerns speech in a language or languages. The disagreement is over the precise nature of the speech in every New Testament passage, especially in 1 Corinthians 12–14.

Two terms are useful. Xenolalia describes the miraculous ability to speak a real human language that the speaker has not learned. Glossolalia is commonly used for religious speech made up of syllables that are not identifiable as an ordinary human language. Acts 2 unmistakably describes recognisable human languages. The Corinthian situation is more debated because Paul describes speech that requires interpretation and is not understood by the congregation.

We should not pretend that every detail of the Corinthian phenomenon is beyond debate. But neither should we use that debate to make the text say whatever a modern practice requires. At the very least, Paul describes a genuine spiritual gift, not a performance that a person is taught to imitate, and he gives clear rules for its use in the gathered church.

2. What happened at Pentecost?

The clearest account of tongues is found in Acts 2. On the day of Pentecost, the Holy Spirit came upon the disciples, and they began to speak in other tongues as the Spirit gave them utterance. Jews from many nations were in Jerusalem. The crowd was astonished because each person heard the disciples speaking in his own native language. Luke names the regions and peoples represented, and the hearers say that they are hearing the mighty works of God in their own languages (Acts 2:1–11).

This was not a crowd hearing a stream of sounds that no one could identify. The miracle was that Galileans spoke languages they had not learned, and people from those language communities understood them. The text repeatedly emphasises actual languages and the hearers' ability to recognise them. Pentecost was a public, intelligible sign.

The event also belongs to the history of redemption. At Babel, human pride was judged through the confusion of languages and the scattering of peoples (Genesis 11:1–9). At Pentecost, people from many nations hear the mighty works of God proclaimed through the one gospel. The gospel does not erase the nations or require everyone to become culturally identical. It gathers people from every nation into one people through Jesus Christ.

Peter's sermon, not the mere spectacle of unusual speech, explains what God is doing. He proclaims the crucified and risen Jesus as Lord and Christ (Acts 2:14–36). The sign draws attention to the apostolic message; it does not replace that message. The crowd is called to repent and be baptised in the name of Jesus Christ (Acts 2:37–41).

When modern speakers claim the gift of Acts 2, the comparison is straightforward: are they speaking identifiable human languages they have never learned, in a way that speakers of those languages can understand? If the claim is that the sounds are a language, it is reasonable to ask for evidence. A listener's emotional impression that the speech sounds foreign is not enough. Nor is a later assertion by the speaker that the utterance was Chinese, Arabic, or another language. The claim concerns a miraculous linguistic ability and can be tested by competent speakers.

3. What did Paul teach the Corinthians?

First Corinthians 12–14 is the longest New Testament treatment of spiritual gifts. Paul writes to a church in which gifts had become a source of pride, rivalry, and disorder. His answer is not to encourage the most dramatic experiences. He teaches that the Spirit distributes gifts according to his will, that believers belong to one body, and that every gift must serve the good of the whole church (1 Corinthians 12:4–27).

In chapter 13, Paul places love above every gift. Even extraordinary speech, knowledge, faith, and sacrifice amount to nothing without love (1 Corinthians 13:1–3). The church's concern should never be to display spiritual status. Christian maturity is measured by love, humility, truth, and service.

Chapter 14 repeatedly contrasts tongues with prophecy. Prophecy is intelligible speech that strengthens, encourages, and comforts the congregation. Uninterpreted tongues do not do that because the hearers cannot understand what is said (1 Corinthians 14:1–5). Paul says that instruments must make distinct sounds if anyone is to recognise a tune, and a trumpet must give a clear call if soldiers are to know what to do. Speech that cannot be understood leaves the hearer without knowing what has been said (1 Corinthians 14:6–12).

Paul's concern is not that the congregation should be impressed by mystery. It is that the congregation should be built up. He says that in the church he would rather speak five intelligible words to instruct others than ten thousand words in a tongue (1 Corinthians 14:18–19). That is a remarkable statement from a man who says he speaks in tongues more than the Corinthians. He does not measure worship by how unusual it sounds, but by whether the church is instructed.

Paul also gives concrete rules. If anyone speaks in a tongue in the gathered church, only two or at most three may speak, each in turn, and someone must interpret. If there is no interpreter, the speaker must keep silent in the church and speak to himself and to God (1 Corinthians 14:27–28). The instruction is not optional. It is an apostolic command.

He concludes that God is not a God of confusion but of peace, and that everything must be done decently and in order (1 Corinthians 14:33, 40). A practice that routinely produces a room full of simultaneous, uninterpreted speech is not an expression of Paul's instructions. It is the opposite of them.

Some argue that 1 Corinthians 14:2 establishes a private prayer language because the speaker speaks to God and no one understands him. But Paul is correcting what happens in the church's gathering. He does not pause to establish a separate category of private devotional speech for every believer. His repeated concern is what happens when the church assembles, and his rule is that unintelligible speech must not be allowed to dominate that gathering. Even if someone understands the Corinthian gift as involving speech not ordinarily understood by people present, the chapter does not authorise modern disorder or make tongues a universal spiritual exercise.

Nor does the phrase “my spirit prays” in 1 Corinthians 14:14 establish that Christians should cultivate unintelligible syllables. Paul immediately says that his mind is unfruitful in that situation and asks what he should do. His answer is to pray and sing with both spirit and mind (1 Corinthians 14:15). The goal is not to set the mind aside as though unintelligibility were a higher form of worship.

4. What was the purpose of the sign?

Paul gives an important explanation in 1 Corinthians 14:20–25. He quotes the language of Isaiah 28:11–12, where foreign speech is associated with God's judgment on a people who would not listen to his word. Paul then says that tongues are a sign for unbelievers, while prophecy serves believers. The passage is not a simple endorsement of every use of tongues; it warns that unintelligible speech can leave outsiders thinking the church is mad, while intelligible proclamation can expose the heart and lead a person to worship God.

In the context of Isaiah, foreign lips and tongues were a sign of judgment upon unbelieving Israel. The sign warned that God's word, rejected in a familiar language, would come through foreign invaders. In the New Testament, tongues therefore carry a serious sign function. They are not a badge of spiritual superiority or a tool for creating an atmosphere in a meeting.

Hebrews 2:3–4 also connects signs, wonders, miracles, and gifts of the Holy Spirit with God's confirmation of the salvation first announced by the Lord and attested by those who heard him. The passage places miraculous signs in the historical confirmation of the apostolic gospel. It does not say that every generation must reproduce the same signs in order to prove that the gospel remains true.

Paul elsewhere refers to the signs of a true apostle as having been performed among the Corinthians with signs, wonders, and mighty works (2 Corinthians 12:12). These signs were not entertainment. They authenticated the extraordinary ministry through which the risen Christ established his church and delivered his authoritative testimony.

5. Tongues were never required of every Christian

One of the most damaging teachings associated with modern tongues-speaking is the claim that every genuine Christian should speak in tongues, or that tongues are the necessary initial evidence of a second experience called the baptism of the Holy Spirit. This teaching can leave sincere believers anxious that they are spiritually deficient because they have not produced the expected sounds.

Paul directly rules out the idea that all Christians possess the same gift. In 1 Corinthians 12:29–30 he asks a series of questions: Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Are all teachers? Do all work miracles? Do all possess gifts of healing? Do all speak with tongues? Do all interpret? The questions expect the answer no. The Spirit distributes different gifts to different members of the one body (1 Corinthians 12:11, 27–30).

If tongues were the necessary evidence that every believer had received the Spirit, Paul's question would make no sense. He would be asking whether all Christians possess a gift that he had just made essential to Christian life. Instead, he teaches that gifts differ and that no member should despise another because their gift is less visible.

The New Testament identifies every believer as belonging to Christ by the Spirit (Romans 8:9; 1 Corinthians 12:13). The Spirit's presence is not measured by whether a person can reproduce a particular vocal experience. The evidence of the Spirit's work includes faith in Christ, love for God and neighbour, the fruit of holiness, and perseverance in the truth (Galatians 5:16–25; Ephesians 1:13–14).

Acts does record tongues at significant moments as the gospel moves from Jerusalem to Samaria and then to the Gentiles. Those events show that different peoples are being brought into the one church. They should not be turned into a rigid formula that overrides the apostle's explicit teaching in 1 Corinthians. The narrative of the church's beginning and expansion is not a command that every Christian in every place must repeat each sign.

6. Why the sign gifts belonged to the apostolic foundation

The New Testament describes the church as a building with a foundation. Ephesians 2:19–22 says that God's household is built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the cornerstone. The apostles were not merely the first members of a continuing line of church officers. They were appointed witnesses of the risen Christ and foundational messengers through whom the church received the authoritative teaching of the new covenant.

A foundation is laid once. The building continues to rise, but it does not require a new foundation every generation. The church today is still being built as the gospel is preached and people are gathered into Christ. Yet it is built on the apostolic foundation already laid. The church does not need new apostles who add fresh binding revelation to the testimony Christ gave through his chosen witnesses.

This does not mean that every spiritual gift ceased at exactly the same moment or that every detail of the timing can be established by one verse. Christians who disagree about the duration of certain gifts should be careful not to claim more than Scripture says. The case for cessation rests on the combined teaching about the unique apostolic foundation, the confirmatory role of signs, and the completion of the foundational work of the apostles.

Hebrews 2:3–4 describes signs and wonders as God's testimony to the message first announced by the Lord and confirmed by those who heard him. Acts repeatedly associates tongues with decisive stages in the public establishment and expansion of the church. Second Corinthians 12:12 identifies signs as credentials of apostolic ministry. Taken together, these passages give the miraculous sign gifts a distinctive role in the foundational period.

The claim is not that God has become unable to heal, answer prayer, guide his people, or act in extraordinary ways. God is sovereign and can do whatever he pleases. The claim is that the church should not expect the particular revelatory and sign gifts that authenticated the apostolic foundation to continue as ordinary, repeatable features of church life. God has not lost his power; the unique historical role of the apostles has been completed.

There is also a vital distinction between the Spirit's continuing work and the continuation of every gift that appeared in the apostolic period. The Spirit still gives new birth, sanctifies believers, illumines Scripture, equips Christians for service, and empowers the proclamation of the gospel. The ordinary work of the Spirit is not a lesser work. It is the very work by which Christ saves and transforms his people.

7. What does “when the perfect comes” mean?

First Corinthians 13:8–13 says that prophecies will pass away, tongues will cease, and knowledge will pass away. Paul contrasts what is partial with what is complete. Some Christians understand “the perfect” to refer to the return of Christ and the final state, arguing that the gifts must continue until then. Others understand the passage as referring to the arrival of the mature or complete state of God's revelation and the end of the church's foundational period.

This disagreement deserves more than a slogan. The word translated “perfect” does not, by itself, settle the question. Nor should anyone claim that the passage explicitly names the completed New Testament canon when it does not use those words. The cessationist case should not rest on pretending that one disputed phrase answers every question.

The broader context matters. Paul is correcting a church that was fascinated with spectacular gifts and reminding it that these gifts are partial and temporary, while love remains. The church's present knowledge is incomplete; its future fullness will be far greater. Whatever precise timing one assigns to the phrase, the passage plainly teaches that tongues are not an eternal feature of the church. They belong to the realm of partial gifts that will pass away.

The argument in this article therefore considers 1 Corinthians 13 alongside the apostolic foundation, the purpose of signs, and the wider biblical account of the church's establishment. A disputed interpretation should be handled honestly, but it should not be allowed to erase the force of the other passages.

8. What happened to tongues after the apostles?

Historical claims need particular care here. It is not accurate to say that absolutely nobody reported speaking in tongues after the apostolic age until the twentieth century. That statement is too sweeping, and a reader who checks the early sources will find examples that need to be acknowledged. A strong biblical argument does not need an exaggerated historical claim.

Irenaeus, writing in the late second century, refers to people in the church who speak in various kinds of languages through the Spirit (Against Heresies 5.6.1). His brief statement is important, although it does not give enough detail to establish precisely what happened, how widespread it was, or whether the phenomenon was identical to every feature of the New Testament gift. Tertullian also refers to spiritual gifts in the context of the Montanist movement, a movement associated with prophetic claims and controversial teaching.

These references should not be hidden. But neither should they be turned into proof of an unbroken, ordinary, church-wide practice. They are scattered reports, and the nature of the speech is not always clear. A report that someone spoke in a tongue does not automatically establish that the event was the same as the apostolic gift, that it was accepted by the wider church, or that it continued continuously from one generation to the next.

By the fourth century, the evidence points in a different direction. John Chrysostom, in his homilies on 1 Corinthians, remarks that the relevant gift had become obscure and was no longer practised in the way described in Paul's day. Augustine also reflects on the sign of tongues as belonging to the church's beginnings and to the witness that the gospel was going to the nations. Later Christian writers commonly interpreted the gift as the miraculous ability to speak foreign languages, rather than as the modern form of ecstatic syllables.

There were later reports of unusual speech in various groups and periods, including medieval religious movements, revival movements, the Shakers, and the nineteenth-century Catholic Apostolic or Irvingite movement. Some of these reports are difficult to verify; others concern practices that differed from the modern Pentecostal doctrine. Their existence means we should not say there was an absolute historical vacuum. They do not demonstrate a continuous, mainstream transmission of the apostolic gift in the form now commonly practised.

A fair historical conclusion is this: after the apostolic period, references to tongues become sparse and their nature is disputed; some early Christian writers refer to extraordinary speech, while other influential writers describe the gift as having passed away or as belonging to the church's earlier period; and the recognisable modern Pentecostal movement emerged in the early twentieth century. History supports the observation that modern Pentecostal practice is not simply an obvious, uninterrupted continuation of a normal practice documented throughout the centuries. Scripture, not historical silence, remains the decisive authority.

9. The origins of modern Pentecostalism

The modern Pentecostal movement did not begin with a clearly documented, continuous chain of churches practising its distinctive doctrine from the apostles onward. Its recognisable modern beginnings are associated especially with Charles Fox Parham's Bible school in Topeka, Kansas, in 1901, and the Azusa Street revival led by William J. Seymour in Los Angeles from 1906. Historians debate how to describe the movement's beginnings and how much weight should be given to earlier revival movements, but these events are central to the movement's history.

Charles F. Parham and Topeka, 1901

Parham was a Holiness preacher who taught that a distinct baptism in the Holy Spirit should be evidenced by speaking in tongues. At his Bible school, students studied the book of Acts and concluded that tongues accompanied the recorded occasions of Spirit baptism. On or around 1 January 1901, Agnes Ozman, one of the students, asked Parham to lay hands on her. She then reportedly began speaking in what Parham identified as Chinese. Parham and his followers understood the experience as the supernatural ability to speak real foreign languages for missionary work.

This original claim matters. Parham did not initially present the experience merely as a private stream of devotional syllables. He believed that God was giving missionaries languages they had never learned so that they could preach the gospel without ordinary language study. He encouraged the expectation that recipients would be sent to foreign lands with this miraculous ability.

The claims were soon questioned. Contemporary reports were not uniform: some witnesses believed they heard recognisable languages, while others described the speech as unintelligible. Reports of written tongues were also disputed. A historical account from the Religion in Kansas Project records that journalists and language observers were unconvinced by many of the claims, and that a Chinese reader reportedly could not translate Ozman's writing. The early movement's own claim to miraculous foreign-language ability therefore faced serious problems from its beginning.

When the supposed missionary languages did not provide the expected practical means of evangelism, the movement's understanding shifted. Tongues came increasingly to be understood not as identifiable foreign languages for missionary preaching, but as spiritual or heavenly speech. That change is significant. The original claim could be tested by asking whether a speaker had actually spoken a language. When that claim failed to produce the expected evidence, the definition of the phenomenon changed in many Pentecostal circles.

William J. Seymour and Azusa Street, 1906

William J. Seymour, a Black Holiness preacher, studied under Parham in Houston and became a central figure in the Azusa Street revival in Los Angeles in 1906. Seymour had to listen to Parham's teaching from outside the classroom because of the racial segregation of the time. The meetings at Azusa Street became a major centre from which Pentecostal teaching and practice spread internationally.

The history should be told honestly. Seymour played a major role in the movement's expansion; Parham helped formulate the distinctive teaching that tongues were the evidence of Spirit baptism. Their relationship later became strained. Parham criticised the Azusa meetings, and the movement developed beyond the control of its early leaders. Modern Pentecostalism is not the work of one person alone, but these figures and events are central to its early history.

Parham's racial attitudes and the segregated setting in which Seymour encountered his teaching are part of this history. They do not, by themselves, prove or disprove a doctrine. It would be an error to reject a teaching merely because a person associated with it was morally compromised; it would be equally wrong to conceal relevant historical facts. The doctrine must be tested by Scripture, and the history must be told truthfully.

The point is not that a teaching must be false because it became prominent in 1901 or 1906. A doctrine is not refuted simply by being newly emphasised. The point is that the modern movement's distinctive teaching about tongues as the expected evidence of Spirit baptism has a traceable modern history. It should not be presented as though the same doctrine and practice had been plainly and continuously taught by the church in every century.

10. What does linguistic research tell us about modern tongues?

Historical claims about miraculous languages can be tested, at least in part, by linguistic evidence. If a person says that an utterance is Chinese, Arabic, Yoruba, or another human language, competent speakers and linguists can examine whether it has the vocabulary, grammar, sound patterns, and meaningful structure of that language.

William J. Samarin, a linguist and professor at the University of Toronto, studied religious glossolalia in different communities and published his findings in Tongues of Men and Angels: The Religious Language of Pentecostalism (1972). His work is widely discussed in the study of glossolalia. He found that the utterances he examined had some features that made them sound language-like, but did not function as identifiable natural languages with a stable vocabulary and grammar. His research does not establish what God can or cannot do; it describes the speech samples he studied.

More recent linguistic work has continued to examine glossolalia as a human speech phenomenon. Research has noted that the sounds often draw on the speaker's own language habits and phonological patterns. A 2023 study in Language and Speech describes glossolalia as phonologically structured utterance without the semantic system of a natural language. Such findings are relevant when a modern speaker claims to be producing a real foreign language, although they cannot determine the spiritual state or sincerity of the individual speaker.

There is an important distinction here. Linguistic research cannot prove that God never gives a miraculous language. If a genuine miracle occurred, it would be a miracle. But when a movement makes a public claim that its utterances are foreign languages, the absence of verifiable language structure is a serious problem for that claim. A practice should not be protected from examination by saying that questioning it is unspiritual.

What about the claim that modern tongues are not human languages but the language of angels? First Corinthians 13:1 mentions “the tongues of men and of angels” as part of Paul's sweeping description of extraordinary gifts without love. The passage does not command Christians to seek an angelic language, nor does it identify the modern practice of glossolalia as that language. Paul is making the point that even the most extraordinary speech is worthless without love. A phrase in a rhetorical argument cannot be turned into a permission slip for any unintelligible vocal practice.

What about interpretations of tongues? In some meetings, one person produces syllables and another gives a message in the congregation's language. Linguistic research has raised questions about whether the spoken utterance and its interpretation show a consistent, translatable relationship. That does not mean every person involved is knowingly inventing a message. It does mean that the claim should be examined rather than assumed. In the New Testament, interpretation is a gift that makes the tongue intelligible to the church; it is not a licence to attach any message whatsoever to an utterance that cannot be checked.

11. Where common modern practice departs from Scripture

Not every Pentecostal or charismatic church practises tongues in exactly the same way. Some place strong emphasis on order and interpretation; others encourage nearly everyone to speak at once. Some teach that tongues are required evidence of Spirit baptism; others do not. It would be unfair to attribute every practice to every congregation. Nevertheless, several widespread claims and practices deserve direct correction.

Making tongues the test of receiving the Holy Spirit

When a church teaches that a believer must speak in tongues to prove that he or she has received the Spirit, it contradicts Paul's teaching that not all Christians speak in tongues (1 Corinthians 12:29–30). It also burdens tender consciences with a requirement Scripture does not impose. A Christian's assurance rests on Christ and his promises, not on the ability to produce a particular vocal experience (Romans 8:9–16; Ephesians 1:13–14).

Teaching people how to produce the sounds

Some are instructed to begin with a repeated syllable, to speak faster, to loosen their tongues, to stop thinking, or to imitate the sounds of people around them. Such coaching should raise an immediate question: if this is a miraculous gift given by the sovereign Spirit, why must people be trained to manufacture the expected sound? The New Testament speaks of the Spirit distributing gifts as he wills, not of leaders teaching congregations a technique for producing an appearance of tongues.

Research into glossolalia has documented that people can acquire patterns of this kind of speech through participation, imitation, and practice. That does not prove that every participant is consciously pretending. It does show why the mere ability to produce the sounds cannot be treated as proof of a supernatural gift.

Everyone speaking at once

Paul's instruction is explicit: two or at most three, one at a time, and an interpreter must be present; without an interpreter the speaker must remain silent in the church (1 Corinthians 14:27–28). A meeting in which dozens of people speak simultaneously, with no interpretation, cannot appeal to Paul's instructions as its justification. The apostle's command is clear enough to be obeyed.

Calling unintelligibility a higher form of worship

Paul does not treat the mind as an obstacle to true worship. He says he will pray with his spirit and with his mind, sing with his spirit and with his mind, and speak intelligible words so that others can be instructed (1 Corinthians 14:14–19). Christian worship involves the heart and the understanding. Confusion is not evidence of a deeper encounter with God.

Using tongues to create spiritual status

When a church divides believers into those who have the experience and those who have not, it creates a hierarchy that Paul rejects. The body has many members, and no member can say to another, “I have no need of you” (1 Corinthians 12:14–26). Love, not a spectacular gift, is the more excellent way (1 Corinthians 12:31–13:13).

Giving messages that cannot be tested

A message supposedly from God must never be placed beyond examination. Scripture commands believers to test the spirits (1 John 4:1), to test everything and hold fast what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:19–22), and to weigh what is said in the gathered church (1 Corinthians 14:29). A person cannot demand unquestioning acceptance by claiming that the message came through a tongue or a spiritual impression.

These concerns are not minor matters of taste. They affect the authority of Scripture, the consciences of believers, the order of worship, and the way the church understands the Holy Spirit. A practice that contradicts apostolic instruction should be stopped, not protected because it has become familiar or emotionally meaningful.

12. Common objections answered

“God is the same yesterday, today, and forever. Why would tongues cease?”

God's character does not change, but his work in history includes different stages and appointed purposes. The sacrificial system was given by God, yet it reached its fulfilment in Christ. The temple and priesthood had a real purpose, but they were not intended to remain the centre of God's covenant people after Christ's once-for-all sacrifice. A gift can be genuinely from God and still have a limited purpose in the history of redemption.

“The Bible says to desire spiritual gifts, especially prophecy.”

Paul's instruction to desire gifts is addressed to the church in the context of its life and edification (1 Corinthians 12:31; 14:1). It does not mean every gift must continue in every age, nor does it authorise people to imitate a gift. The question of duration must be answered from the whole teaching of Scripture, including the unique apostolic foundation.

“Tongues are a private prayer language, so Paul's rules for public worship do not apply.”

First Corinthians 14 is written to correct the church's gathered worship. The chapter does not give a clear command that every believer should develop a private tongue, and it does not establish the modern practice as a separate category beyond testing. Paul's own conclusion is that prayer and singing should involve both spirit and mind (1 Corinthians 14:15). We should not build a universal doctrine on a disputed reading of one verse while ignoring the clear instructions surrounding it.

“There are people who speak in tongues and whose lives have changed.”

People may experience strong emotion, a sense of release, community belonging, renewed commitment, or even genuine growth in some areas of life. A person may also be a sincere Christian who loves Christ while holding a mistaken view about spiritual gifts. None of this proves that a particular vocal phenomenon is the New Testament gift. A good result in one person's life cannot make an unbiblical teaching true. We must test the teaching itself.

“You are quenching the Spirit by questioning this.”

Scripture says not to quench the Spirit, but in the same passage it commands believers to test everything and hold fast what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:19–22). Testing is not the enemy of spiritual life; it is one of the duties of spiritual life. The Spirit who inspired Scripture does not ask the church to disobey Scripture in order to prove its openness to him.

“You are denying that God can perform miracles today.”

No. God is able to heal, answer prayer, provide, protect, and act in ways beyond our understanding. We should pray for the sick and trust God's wisdom. The question is not whether God can do miracles, but whether the church should claim that a specific apostolic sign gift continues as a regular practice. God's power is not measured by how many people can reproduce an experience in a meeting.

“There are reports of tongues throughout church history.”

There are scattered reports, and this article has acknowledged them. The claim that no one ever reported tongues after the apostles is historically inaccurate. But isolated reports do not establish an unbroken, ordinary, widely recognised continuation of the New Testament gift. The evidence is uneven, the nature of the reported phenomena differs, and several major Christian writers describe the apostolic sign as having passed away. Historical evidence must be weighed, not reduced to a slogan.

“If the modern practice is not the gift, are all its practitioners deceivers?”

No. That conclusion would be unjust. Some people may be knowingly performing; others may be sincerely repeating what they were taught, responding to social expectations, or interpreting an intense experience through the theology of their church. We cannot read every person's heart. We can say that sincerity does not establish truth and that a practice should be corrected when it does not conform to Scripture. We should speak firmly about error without accusing every person involved of deliberate fraud.

13. What is the Holy Spirit doing in the church today?

A rejection of modern glossolalia as the New Testament gift is not a rejection of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit is not absent from the church, and the church is not left to human strength. The Spirit gives new life to those who are dead in sin, unites believers to Christ, assures them of adoption, produces holiness, empowers prayer, distributes gifts for service, and strengthens the church through the Word (John 3:5–8; Romans 8:1–16; 1 Corinthians 12:4–11; Galatians 5:16–25).

The Spirit's central ministry is to glorify Christ (John 16:13–14). He does not draw attention away from the Son or encourage people to measure spiritual maturity by dramatic manifestations. He opens blind eyes to see the glory of Christ, turns sinners from sin, produces love and holiness, and equips believers to serve one another.

The ordinary means through which Christians grow are not empty or powerless. God uses the reading and preaching of Scripture, prayer, the fellowship of the church, baptism, and the Lord's Supper to strengthen his people. The Spirit works through the Word he inspired. A quiet believer who trusts Christ, fights sin, loves the brethren, and perseveres in obedience is not spiritually inferior to someone who can produce an impressive religious sound.

We should seek the fullness of the Spirit in the biblical sense: to be filled with the Spirit, to walk by the Spirit, to put sin to death, to speak the truth in love, to worship with understanding, and to bear fruit that honours Christ (Ephesians 5:18–21; Galatians 5:16–25; Colossians 3:16–17). These are not lesser goals. They are central to Christian life.

14. A call to pastors, churches, and those who speak in tongues

Pastors and church leaders have a responsibility to protect the congregation from teachings that cannot be supported by Scripture. They should not pressure people to speak in tongues, suggest that those who do not speak are second-class Christians, or permit public speech that ignores the apostolic requirement of interpretation and order. They should teach 1 Corinthians 12–14 in context and explain why the Spirit gives different gifts to different members.

Leaders should also stop coaching people to produce syllables as though the ability to imitate a sound were evidence of divine power. They should not give a platform to claims of miraculous foreign languages without testing whether the language is genuine. They should not allow supposed messages from God to escape the scrutiny that Scripture requires.

If you have been taught that you cannot be sure you have the Holy Spirit because you do not speak in tongues, you should not carry that burden. Look to Christ. The promise of the gospel is not that you must achieve a particular experience before you may know that you belong to him. Everyone who belongs to Christ has his Spirit (Romans 8:9). Trust the promises of God rather than a test he has not given.

If you speak in tongues, do not take this article as an invitation to despair or to assume that your entire Christian life has been false. Examine the teaching you have received. Ask whether the practice agrees with the apostolic instructions. Be willing to stop a practice that cannot be defended from Scripture, even if it has been part of your life for years. The truth of God's Word is more important than protecting a familiar experience.

And if you are a Christian who disagrees with the argument presented here, respond from Scripture and history rather than caricature. The aim should not be to win an argument or humiliate another believer. The aim is that Christ's church should worship in truth, with understanding, humility, love, and reverence.

15. Conclusion: Let Scripture, not experience, have the final word

The New Testament gift of tongues was not a badge of superior spirituality, a technique that could be taught to a congregation, or an excuse for disorder. At Pentecost, people heard the mighty works of God in recognisable languages. In Corinth, Paul insisted that speech in the gathered church must be interpreted, limited, orderly, and directed toward the edification of others. He explicitly taught that not all Christians speak in tongues.

The apostolic foundation was unique. Signs and wonders confirmed the gospel proclaimed by Christ's authorised witnesses, and the church is now built on the foundation already laid. The case made here is that the extraordinary sign gifts associated with that foundational period are not promised as ordinary features of the church today. Christians may disagree about some details of the cessation passages, but no disagreement can justify practices that plainly contradict Paul's instructions.

History does not support the careless statement that no one ever reported tongues after the apostles. There were scattered references and occasional episodes. But they do not establish an unbroken, mainstream continuation of the New Testament gift. The modern Pentecostal movement took recognisable shape in the early twentieth century, beginning especially with Parham's Topeka Bible school and spreading widely through Seymour's Azusa Street revival. Its early claim that tongues were miraculous foreign languages for missionary work encountered serious difficulties, and the movement's understanding of tongues developed over time.

Linguistic research has also found that the modern glossolalia it examined does not function as an identifiable natural language. That finding does not allow us to judge every speaker's heart, but it does challenge claims that these utterances are the same kind of miraculous human languages described at Pentecost. More importantly, the modern practice must answer to the plain teaching of Scripture: gifts are distributed by the Spirit, not possessed by all; worship must be intelligible and orderly; and every claim must be tested.

Therefore, churches should stop treating modern unintelligible speech as the necessary evidence of the Holy Spirit, stop teaching people to imitate it, and stop permitting public practices that disregard the apostolic command for interpretation and order. Pastors should teach their people to seek Christ, love one another, grow in holiness, and serve with the gifts God has actually given them. Those who have been pressured by these teachings should be directed back to the sufficiency of Christ and the promises of the gospel.

We must not mock people, assume motives, or deny God's present power. But neither should we call something biblical simply because it is emotional, popular, or sincerely practised. The Holy Spirit does not contradict the Word he inspired. Christian worship must be governed by that Word.

“Let all things be done for building up.” (1 Corinthians 14:26)

May the church be marked not by confusion, spiritual competition, or the pursuit of extraordinary experiences, but by faith in Christ, love for the brethren, holiness, sound doctrine, and worship that is reverent and full of understanding.

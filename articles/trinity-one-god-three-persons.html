The Trinity: One God in Three Persons

What do Christians mean when they confess the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit as one God? A careful look at the biblical testimony and the language Christians have used to express it.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

Christians worship one God. Yet the New Testament speaks of the Father as God, identifies the Son as divine, and presents the Holy Spirit as divine and personal. It also distinguishes the Father, Son, and Spirit from one another. The doctrine of the Trinity is the historic Christian confession that holds these biblical claims together: there is one God, eternally existing as three distinct persons—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

This teaching is not the claim that there are three gods. Nor does it mean that one person merely appears in three different forms. The Father is not the Son; the Son is not the Spirit; and the Spirit is not the Father. Yet each is truly and fully God, sharing the one undivided divine being.

The word Trinity does not appear in the Bible. Christians use it as a concise theological term to describe what they understand the whole biblical witness to teach. The question, therefore, is not whether the word occurs in Scripture, but whether the teaching faithfully brings together what Scripture says about the one God and the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

A note on method: The doctrine is drawn from the Bible's combined testimony, not from one isolated proof-text. Read the cited passages in context. Where Christians disagree about the interpretation of a text, the disagreement should be stated honestly rather than hidden.

1. There is one God

The starting point is the Bible's clear affirmation that God is one. Israel was commanded: “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one” (Deuteronomy 6:4). The prophets reject the worship of rival gods because the Lord alone is God (Isaiah 45:5–6). The New Testament continues this conviction. Paul writes that “there is no God but one” (1 Corinthians 8:4), and James says that even demons believe that God is one (James 2:19).

Christian belief in the Trinity does not set aside biblical monotheism. It begins with it. The Christian confession is not that the Father is one god, the Son another, and the Spirit a third. There is one divine being, not three separate divine beings or three competing centres of deity.

This matters because some explanations of the Trinity accidentally sound like belief in three gods. That is not the doctrine. The three persons are not three parts that combine to make God, as though each were one-third divine. Each is fully God, and God is not divided into portions.

2. The Father is God

The Father is plainly identified as God throughout Scripture. Jesus teaches his disciples to pray, “Our Father in heaven” (Matthew 6:9). He speaks of the Father as “the only true God” (John 17:3), and Paul writes of “one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist” (1 Corinthians 8:6).

There is no serious dispute within historic Trinitarian Christianity that the Father is God. The question is how this confession relates to the Bible's teaching about the Son and the Spirit. We must not make the Father the only person who is truly God, because that would leave other biblical testimony unexplained.

3. The Son is God

The New Testament does not present Jesus as merely a prophet who points away from himself to God. John opens his Gospel by saying that the Word was with God and was God, and that all things were made through him (John 1:1–3). Later, John identifies the Word as the one who became flesh and lived among us (John 1:14). The Son is distinct from the Father, yet shares the divine identity.

Thomas addresses the risen Jesus as “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28). The author of Hebrews applies language of divine rule to the Son and says that the Son is the radiance of God's glory and the exact imprint of his nature (Hebrews 1:3, 8). Paul describes Christ as existing in the form of God and then taking the form of a servant (Philippians 2:5–8). These are not descriptions of a mere human being who began to exist at Bethlehem.

Jesus also does things Scripture associates with God. All things were created through the Son (John 1:3; Colossians 1:16–17). He forgives sins (Mark 2:5–12), receives worship (Matthew 14:33; John 9:38), and will be honoured just as the Father is honoured (John 5:22–23). These texts must be read alongside passages in which Jesus prays to the Father and speaks of being sent by him. The New Testament holds both truths together: the Son is truly divine, and he is not the Father.

Some passages describe the Son as obeying the Father, receiving a mission from him, or speaking of the Father as greater (John 5:19; 14:28). Trinitarian Christians understand these statements in relation to the Son's incarnate mission and his eternal relation to the Father, while maintaining that the Son shares the one divine nature. Christians who reject the Trinity interpret these passages differently. A fair discussion should address the whole range of relevant texts rather than quoting only one side.

4. The Holy Spirit is God

The Holy Spirit is not presented in Scripture as an impersonal force that God switches on and off. He speaks, teaches, guides, bears witness, and can be grieved (John 14:26; 15:26; 16:13; Acts 13:2; Ephesians 4:30). These are personal actions and relations. The Spirit is not simply another name for the Father or the Son.

Acts 5:3–4 is especially significant. Peter first tells Ananias that he has lied to the Holy Spirit, then says that he has not lied to men but to God. The passage closely connects lying to the Spirit with lying to God. Paul also describes believers as God's temple because the Spirit dwells in them (1 Corinthians 3:16; compare 6:19).

The Spirit possesses divine knowledge and acts with divine authority. He searches the depths of God (1 Corinthians 2:10–11), gives gifts as he wills (1 Corinthians 12:11), and is named alongside the Father and the Son in the baptismal command (Matthew 28:19). The Spirit is fully divine, not a lesser creature or an impersonal power.

5. The Father, Son, and Spirit are distinct persons

It is not enough to say that Father, Son, and Spirit are three names for God. Scripture shows them relating to one another. At Jesus' baptism, the Son is baptised, the Spirit descends like a dove, and the Father's voice speaks from heaven (Matthew 3:16–17). The scene is not naturally read as one person merely changing masks.

Jesus prays to the Father (John 17:1–5). The Father sends the Son (Galatians 4:4; 1 John 4:14), and the Father sends the Spirit in the Son's name (John 14:26). The Son asks the Father to give another Helper (John 14:16). These relationships would lose their ordinary meaning if the Father, Son, and Spirit were simply the same person under three temporary appearances.

Jesus' command to make disciples and baptise them “in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” places all three together under the one singular “name” (Matthew 28:19). Paul closes 2 Corinthians with a blessing that names the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, the love of God, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit (2 Corinthians 13:14). These passages do not provide a complete technical definition, but they show the three together in a way that the church has rightly taken seriously.

Calling them persons does not mean three human individuals, each with a separate divine being. In Trinitarian theology, “person” identifies the real distinction between Father, Son, and Spirit; “being” or “essence” identifies what they share: the one divine nature. The words are theological tools. They help us speak carefully, but they should never be used to claim more than Scripture reveals.

6. One God in three persons: what does that mean?

The classic Christian formulation can be stated simply: the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God; the Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Spirit, and the Spirit is not the Father; and there is only one God.

“One being” answers the question what is God? “Three persons” answers who is God? The Father, Son, and Spirit are not three beings who cooperate as a team. They are the three distinct, co-eternal persons who share the one undivided divine essence. None is created, none is less divine, and none exists independently of the others.

This language is not a mathematical puzzle that makes God three and one in the same sense. Christians confess one in essence and three in person. The number of persons is not the number of divine beings. The doctrine is profound and beyond our full comprehension, but it is not permission to speak nonsense or to contradict the Bible.

We should also be cautious with illustrations. Water as ice, liquid, and vapour suggests one person changing forms. An egg with shell, white, and yolk suggests three parts making up a whole. A person who is a parent, worker, and friend suggests one person playing different roles. These comparisons may seem helpful at first, but each distorts an important part of the doctrine. It is better to explain the biblical teaching directly than to make a flawed analogy carry the weight of the mystery.

7. The doctrine in church history

The word Trinity is not a biblical word, but the questions it addresses arose because Christians were trying to confess faithfully everything Scripture says about the Father, Son, and Spirit. Early Christians used different terms as they worked through these questions. The Latin word trinitas is associated with Tertullian in the early third century, though the vocabulary and precision of later creeds developed over time.

In the fourth century, controversy about the Son's relation to the Father became especially intense. The Council of Nicaea in AD 325 rejected the claim that the Son was a creature and confessed him as “of one substance” with the Father. The Council of Constantinople in AD 381 reaffirmed the Nicene faith and gave fuller expression to the confession concerning the Holy Spirit. The language of the creeds was intended to guard the church's reading of Scripture, not to replace Scripture with a new revelation.

Historical development should be described carefully. The exact technical formula was not written out in one biblical sentence, and Christians did not settle every question in a single meeting. The church gradually clarified its language in response to disputes over how the biblical texts should be understood. Trinitarian Christians argue that this was a clarification of the faith already present in Scripture, not an invention of a new God. The historical fact that terminology developed does not, by itself, prove or disprove the doctrine; its truth must be tested by the biblical testimony.

The Nicene Creed remains an important summary of historic Christian belief. It is valuable as a witness to how generations of Christians understood the faith, while Scripture remains the final authority by which every creed and teacher must be examined.

8. Common misunderstandings

“Christians believe in three gods.”

No. Historic Trinitarian teaching confesses one God, not three. The persons are distinct, but they do not possess three separate divine essences. If an explanation makes the Father, Son, and Spirit sound like three independent gods, it has failed to express the doctrine accurately.

“The Father, Son, and Spirit are just three forms of one person.”

This is commonly called modalism. It treats the three as different modes, roles, or appearances of one person rather than as eternally distinct persons. But the Father loves and sends the Son; the Son prays to the Father; and the Spirit is sent. These are genuine relations, not simply one person speaking to himself under different titles.

“The Son and Spirit are lesser gods.”

This is not the historic doctrine either. The Son is not a created being, and the Spirit is not a subordinate deity. The Son's willing obedience in his mission does not mean that he possesses a lesser divine nature. The Spirit is named and honoured with the Father and the Son.

“The word Trinity is absent from the Bible, so the doctrine is unbiblical.”

A word's absence does not settle whether the concept it describes is biblical. The word monotheism is also not a biblical word, yet the Bible plainly teaches that there is one God. The real question is whether the doctrine faithfully summarises the relevant passages. It must be demonstrated from Scripture, not defended merely by pointing to a later term.

9. Questions and objections

Does Deuteronomy 6:4 rule out the Trinity?

It rules out belief in multiple gods, and Trinitarian Christians wholeheartedly affirm that. The debate is whether the New Testament's teaching about the Son and Spirit requires a richer account of the identity of the one God. Deuteronomy 6:4 must be read alongside, not against, the New Testament's witness.

If Jesus is God, why does he pray to the Father?

Because the Son is not the Father, and because the eternal Son truly became human. His prayers express his real human life and his personal relation to the Father. The incarnation does not mean that Jesus stopped being divine; it means that the Son took a complete human nature. His prayers are therefore not evidence that he is a different kind of being from God.

Is the Trinity a contradiction?

A contradiction would be saying that God is one person and three persons, or one being and three beings, in the same sense and at the same time. The doctrine instead says one divine being and three distinct persons. That distinction does not explain everything about God's life, but it shows why the basic statement is not simply “one equals three.”

Can we fully understand the Trinity?

No finite creature can comprehend God exhaustively. But not comprehending everything about God does not mean we can know nothing truly about him. Christians believe God has made himself known through his works and, supremely, through his Son and the Spirit's work in Scripture. We can know what God has revealed without pretending to master his infinite being.

Do all Christians agree on the doctrine?

No. Trinitarian belief is the historic confession of the great majority of churches that identify with Nicene Christianity, but some groups and individual Christians reject it or interpret the biblical texts differently. A serious article should represent those disagreements accurately. This study explains the Trinitarian case; it does not claim that every reader will accept its interpretation.

10. Why the Trinity matters

The Trinity is not an abstract puzzle with no connection to ordinary Christian life. It shapes the way Christians understand salvation. The Father sends the Son in love (John 3:16–17; Galatians 4:4–5). The Son takes our humanity, dies for sinners, and rises again (Romans 5:6–11; 1 Corinthians 15:3–4). The Holy Spirit gives new life, unites believers to Christ, and applies the benefits of his saving work (John 3:5–8; Romans 8:9–16; Titus 3:4–6).

It also shapes Christian prayer and worship. Believers come to the Father through the Son in the Spirit (Ephesians 2:18). Christian baptism is in the one name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19). The church's worship is therefore not directed towards three gods, but to the one God who has made himself known as Father, Son, and Spirit.

The doctrine also protects the good news about Jesus. If the Son were merely a creature, Christians would not be worshipping him as the New Testament does. If he were not truly human, he could not truly represent us. The doctrine of the Trinity belongs alongside the incarnation: the eternal Son became man for our salvation, without ceasing to be God.

11. A fitting response

The right response to the doctrine of the Trinity is not pride in possessing a difficult formula, nor impatience with those who struggle to understand it. It is reverent attention to what God has revealed. We should read Scripture carefully, confess what it teaches, refuse explanations that distort it, and speak with humility where our understanding is limited.

Christians do not claim to have invented a definition that makes God manageable. They confess the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit because they believe this is the God whom Scripture reveals. The doctrine invites us to worship, not to pretend that the infinite God can be contained by our words.

In summary: There is one God. The Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God. The Father, Son, and Spirit are personally distinct and eternally related, yet they are not three gods. This is what Christians mean by the Trinity: one God in three persons.

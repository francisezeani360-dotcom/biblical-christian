What Is Repentance? Understanding Biblical Repentance

What does it mean to repent? A careful study of turning from sin to God, the relationship between repentance and faith, and why genuine repentance is a gift of grace rather than a payment for forgiveness.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

“Repent” is one of the first words Jesus used when he began preaching the good news of God's kingdom. It is also one of the words that can be misunderstood most easily. Some hear it as a demand to clean up their lives before they are allowed to come to God. Others reduce it to feeling bad about something they have done. Still others speak as though repentance were an optional addition to faith, useful for especially serious sinners but unnecessary for everyone else.

None of these ideas captures the Bible's teaching. Repentance is a change of mind and heart that turns a person away from sin and toward God. It includes honest recognition of our guilt, a changed attitude toward the sin we once defended, and a real turning to God for mercy. It is not a claim that we have made ourselves worthy, nor is it a promise that we will never stumble again.

The gospel does not tell sinners to make themselves acceptable and then approach Christ. It calls them to come to Christ as sinners. Yet the Christ who receives sinners does not leave them at peace with the rebellion from which he saves them. The grace that forgives also begins to change the heart. Repentance is therefore inseparable from genuine conversion, while remaining entirely distinct from the ground on which God justifies us.

We need to understand this carefully. If repentance is turned into a work that earns forgiveness, the freeness of grace is obscured. If repentance is removed from the gospel, the call of Jesus is weakened and a false confidence can take its place. Scripture holds together what we must not separate: salvation is by grace, Christ alone is the Saviour, and those who come to him are called to repent and believe.

1. What is repentance?

Repentance is a change of mind and heart concerning sin and God that leads to a genuine turning from sin to God. It is more than changing an opinion about a minor matter. In biblical repentance, a person comes to see sin as God sees it: not as a harmless weakness to excuse, but as evil against the holy and good Creator. The person no longer wishes to defend sin or make peace with it. He turns to God, seeking mercy and a new way of life.

The Bible's language of turning is important. God repeatedly calls his people to turn from their evil ways and return to him (Isaiah 55:6–7; Ezekiel 18:30–32; Joel 2:12–13). Repentance is not simply looking backwards with regret. It is a change of direction. The person who repents turns away from the path of rebellion and turns toward the Lord.

This does not mean that repentance is sinless perfection. A repentant person may still struggle with deeply rooted sins, experience temptation, and need repeated correction. The difference is not that sin has become impossible, but that sin is no longer welcomed as a rightful master. The repentant person confesses it, grieves over it, seeks help to put it to death, and returns to God rather than making excuses for remaining in it.

Repentance is therefore personal. We are not merely sorry that our actions have damaged our reputation, relationships, finances, or comfort. We recognise that we have sinned against God. David's confession after his sin with Bathsheba acknowledges the harm done to others, but reaches its deepest point in the words, “Against you, you only, have I sinned” (Psalm 51:4). His point is not that no one else was wronged. It is that sin is ultimately committed before God, whose judgment is perfectly right.

2. Why do sinners need to repent?

We need to repent because our problem is not merely that we have made isolated mistakes. We have turned away from the God who made us. Scripture describes sin as lawlessness (1 John 3:4), rebellion (Isaiah 1:2–4), and a failure to honour and thank our Creator (Romans 1:18–25). It affects our desires, thoughts, words, actions, and relationships. We cannot repair this condition simply by becoming more disciplined or religious.

God's call to repent is not unreasonable or cruel. He is the holy Creator, and his commands are good. Sin harms us and others, but its deepest evil is that it rejects the One to whom we owe our lives. Repentance is the fitting response when we recognise the truth about ourselves and the goodness of God.

Jesus warned people not to assume that tragedy proves its victims were worse sinners than everyone else. He then said, “unless you repent, you will all likewise perish” (Luke 13:1–5). His warning brings the question close to every hearer. The urgent issue is not whether someone else's sin appears more serious than ours. It is whether we ourselves have turned to God.

Repentance also matters because forgiveness is not permission to continue in rebellion. Christ saves his people from their sins (Matthew 1:21), not merely from the consequences they dislike. The gospel offers pardon and reconciliation, and it brings sinners into a new relationship with the God they once resisted.

3. The call of Jesus and the apostles

Repentance is not a minor theme added by later Christians. It stands near the beginning of Jesus' public ministry. Mark records that Jesus came proclaiming the gospel of God and saying, “The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel” (Mark 1:14–15). His message announced God's saving reign and summoned people to respond with repentance and faith.

Jesus also said that he came not to call the righteous, but sinners (Mark 2:17). He did not mean that some people are truly righteous and therefore have no need of him. He exposed the self-righteousness of those who would not acknowledge their need. The sick need a physician; sinners need the Saviour. Repentance begins when we stop pretending that we are well.

After his resurrection, Jesus explained that the Scriptures had foretold that the Christ would suffer and rise, and that “repentance for the forgiveness of sins should be proclaimed in his name to all nations” (Luke 24:46–47). The apostles carried out this commission. Peter called his hearers to repent (Acts 2:38; 3:19), Paul declared that God commands all people everywhere to repent (Acts 17:30), and he described his ministry as bearing witness to repentance toward God and faith in the Lord Jesus Christ (Acts 20:21).

The call is universal. Repentance is not only for people with notorious public failures, nor is it a special requirement for those who have been away from church for years. Every sinner needs to turn to God. Every hearer of the gospel is summoned to respond.

4. What genuine repentance involves

Repentance is one reality, but several features help us understand it. These should not be treated as a mechanical checklist or as separate steps that every person experiences in exactly the same way. They describe the shape of a heart that has been brought to acknowledge sin and turn to God.

A truthful recognition of sin

Repentance begins by agreeing with God about our sin. We stop minimising it, blaming everyone else, or comparing ourselves with people whose failures appear worse. We recognise that God's law is good and that our guilt is real (Romans 3:19–20). This honesty is not despair for its own sake. It brings us out of hiding and makes us look to the mercy God offers.

A changed attitude toward sin

The repentant person no longer regards sin as something to cherish, excuse, or protect. There is a change in the heart's judgment. What once seemed attractive and harmless is now recognised as offensive to God. This does not mean temptation instantly disappears. It means that the person no longer wants to call evil good or make a lasting peace with it.

A turning toward God

Repentance is not complete as a mere rejection of certain behaviours. It turns us toward the Lord. The prodigal son did not only recognise that his life had become miserable; he arose and returned to his father (Luke 15:17–24). Biblical repentance involves coming to God, confessing our need, and seeking the mercy that he has promised in Christ.

A changed direction that bears fruit

Where repentance is genuine, it begins to show itself in life. John the Baptist told his hearers to bear fruit in keeping with repentance (Matthew 3:8). Paul described his message as calling people to repent, turn to God, and perform deeds in keeping with their repentance (Acts 26:20). The fruit does not purchase forgiveness. It gives visible expression to a real change of heart.

5. Is repentance the same as feeling sorry?

No. Sorrow may accompany repentance, and godly sorrow can be an important part of it, but sorrow by itself is not repentance. A person can regret the consequences of sin while remaining unwilling to turn from the sin itself. Someone may be upset because a lie has been discovered, because an affair has damaged a marriage, or because dishonest behaviour has cost them money. That distress does not necessarily mean the person has turned to God.

Paul makes a distinction in 2 Corinthians 7:9–11 between worldly grief and godly grief. Godly grief produces repentance that leads to salvation without regret, while worldly grief produces death. The difference is not simply the intensity of emotion. It is where the sorrow leads. Godly grief moves a person toward honest confession, earnestness, and a desire to put things right. Worldly grief can remain centred on wounded pride, fear of punishment, or lost advantages.

At the same time, we should not demand a particular emotional display as proof of repentance. People differ in temperament, and sorrow is not always expressed with tears. The Bible does not teach that a person must feel a prescribed level of anguish before God will receive him. The question is whether the heart is turning to God in truth.

Nor should we confuse repentance with self-hatred or hopelessness. Judas felt remorse, but his despair did not lead him to seek the mercy of Christ (Matthew 27:3–5). Peter also wept bitterly after denying Jesus, but his failure was not the end of his story (Luke 22:54–62; John 21:15–19). Sorrow can be present in both cases; what matters is whether we turn toward the Lord who is merciful to those who come to him.

6. Repentance and faith belong together

Jesus' command in Mark 1:15 joins repentance and faith: “repent and believe in the gospel.” They are distinguishable, but they are not rival ways of being saved. Repentance turns from sin to God; faith receives and rests upon Jesus Christ. Repentance looks away from the rebellion we once embraced; faith looks to the Saviour who alone can forgive and reconcile us.

It is possible to explain the two separately, but we should not separate them in a genuine conversion. A person does not truly come to Christ while deliberately insisting that he has no intention of turning from the sin that Christ condemns. Equally, a person does not repent in the saving sense by merely abandoning certain behaviours while refusing to trust the Lord. We turn to God through Christ, and we trust Christ as the one who saves us from sin.

This is why the Bible can speak of conversion in terms of turning to God from idols to serve the living and true God (1 Thessalonians 1:9), and also in terms of believing in the Lord Jesus (Acts 16:31). These are not competing descriptions. They show different sides of one response to the gospel.

Faith and repentance do not contribute merit to salvation. Faith receives Christ; repentance does not pay for past wrongdoing. Both are responses brought about by God's grace. For a fuller explanation of the nature of saving faith, see What Is Saving Faith?

7. Is repentance a condition we fulfil to earn salvation?

No. Repentance is commanded by God, but it is never presented as a payment by which sinners purchase forgiveness. We are justified by God's grace through faith in Jesus Christ, not by the moral quality of our repentance or by the amount of sorrow we can produce. Christ's obedience, death, and resurrection—not our response—are the ground of our acceptance with God.

It is important to distinguish the ground of salvation from the response to the gospel. The ground is Christ and his finished work. The means by which we receive Christ is faith. Repentance accompanies this faith as the sinner turns to God. The fruit that follows includes a changed life of obedience. These truths belong together, but they must not be confused.

If we imagine that God forgives us because we have repented sufficiently, we have turned repentance into another form of self-righteousness. How sorry is sorry enough? How much change must we achieve before we can come? Scripture directs our confidence away from the quality of our performance and toward the mercy of God in Christ. We come because Christ is a sufficient Saviour, not because our repentance has made us worthy of him.

But the opposite error is just as serious. We must not use grace as an excuse to refuse repentance. Paul rejects the suggestion that we should continue in sin so that grace may abound (Romans 6:1–2). Grace does not make sin harmless. It unites us to Christ, whose people are no longer to live under sin's dominion.

God does not tell sinners to become their own saviours. He commands them to turn and come to the Saviour he has provided. That is a call to abandon both self-reliance and the defence of sin.

8. Repentance as a gift of God's grace

Scripture speaks of repentance both as something God commands and as something God graciously grants. Paul tells Timothy that God may perhaps grant repentance leading to a knowledge of the truth (2 Timothy 2:25). When Gentiles believed the gospel, the believers in Jerusalem glorified God, saying, “Then to the Gentiles also God has granted repentance that leads to life” (Acts 11:18).

This does not mean that God repents in our place or that we are not truly responsible to turn to him. The sinner really repents. He acknowledges his sin, turns from it, and comes to God. Yet the willingness and spiritual change involved are not achievements for which the sinner can boast. God is mercifully at work in the heart.

This fits the wider teaching of Scripture about salvation. We were dead in trespasses and sins, but God made us alive with Christ (Ephesians 2:1–10). He promises to give his people a new heart and put his Spirit within them (Ezekiel 36:25–27). The new birth is God's work, and the renewed person responds willingly in faith and repentance. Grace does not make our response unreal; grace makes it possible.

This truth should make Christians humble and prayerful. We should call people to repent clearly, but we should not imagine that persuasive words or pressure can change a heart. We plead with people to come to Christ, pray for them, and trust God to grant the repentance and faith that lead to life.

9. The fruit of genuine repentance

John the Baptist's instruction to bear fruit in keeping with repentance shows that a changed heart has consequences. Paul likewise speaks of deeds that are fitting for those who have turned to God (Matthew 3:8; Acts 26:20). These deeds do not earn salvation. They are the evidence and fruit of a new direction.

One fruit is confession rather than concealment. Instead of hiding sin behind excuses, a repentant person learns to bring it into the light before God (1 John 1:8–9). Where another person has been harmed, repentance may also involve honest acknowledgement and a willingness to make restitution where possible. Zacchaeus' response to Jesus included practical generosity and repayment for fraud (Luke 19:1–10). His actions did not buy the Lord's acceptance; they displayed the reality of the change taking place.

Another fruit is a growing hatred of sin and desire for holiness. This does not mean that Christians never experience strong temptation. It means that they increasingly recognise sin as an enemy rather than a friend. They seek to put sinful desires to death and to cultivate love, patience, truthfulness, purity, and mercy by the Spirit's help (Galatians 5:16–24; Colossians 3:1–17).

Repentance also changes how we treat other people. We become less eager to excuse our own faults and more ready to forgive those who wrong us. We learn to speak truthfully, pursue peace, and take responsibility for our actions. Not every consequence of sin can be undone, and not every relationship can be restored in the way we might wish. Still, the repentant person seeks to walk in the light and to do what is right.

The fruit will not be identical in every believer, and it will not appear fully formed at conversion. Growth takes place over time. But where there is no desire at all to turn from sin, no willingness to confess it, and no evidence of a changed direction, a confident claim to repentance should be examined seriously.

10. Do Christians continue to repent?

Yes. The Christian life is marked by continuing repentance. Conversion is a decisive turning to God, but believers are not made morally perfect in this life. We still sin, and Scripture continues to call Christians to confess, put away evil, renew their minds, and walk in obedience.

In 1 John 1:8–10, John warns believers against claiming to be without sin and assures them that God is faithful and just to forgive and cleanse those who confess their sins. This is not an invitation to treat sin casually. It is an invitation to walk honestly before God, trusting the cleansing provided through Jesus Christ. The same letter teaches that the person who knows Christ will not make a settled practice of lawlessness (1 John 2:3–6; 3:4–10).

Jesus' letters to the churches in Revelation include repeated calls to repent. These were not merely appeals to outsiders. Churches and professing believers needed correction. Some had abandoned their first love, tolerated false teaching, or grown spiritually complacent (Revelation 2–3). The Lord's warnings show that repentance remains necessary wherever sin is found.

Continuing repentance is not a second conversion every time a Christian sins. Nor does it mean that our standing with God rises and falls with each failure. Those who are in Christ are justified through him. Yet the same grace that gives us peace with God teaches us to reject ungodliness and pursue holiness (Titus 2:11–14). We return to the Father, confess our sins, and keep walking in the way of Christ.

11. False repentance and empty professions

Because repentance can be imitated outwardly, Scripture warns us not to confuse religious appearances with a changed heart. Pharaoh sometimes admitted that he had sinned when judgments came upon Egypt, but his words did not lead to lasting submission to God (Exodus 9:27–35; 10:16–20). His concern was repeatedly tied to the removal of suffering, not a humble return to the Lord.

There is also a kind of sorrow that is centred on the self. A person may regret being exposed, losing respect, or suffering consequences, while still cherishing the sin itself. Such regret may produce promises and emotional displays, but it does not necessarily produce a changed direction. The contrast in 2 Corinthians 7:10–11 helps us ask not merely whether someone feels bad, but what that sorrow is producing.

Another danger is outward reform without inward turning. A person may abandon a destructive habit for health, reputation, family pressure, or personal ambition, yet remain unwilling to submit to God. Such changes may be beneficial, but they are not necessarily repentance toward God. Biblical repentance is not simply becoming more respectable. It is returning to the Lord.

We must be careful, however, not to appoint ourselves as judges of every person's heart. We cannot see motives perfectly, and we should not pronounce a final verdict on someone merely because their repentance looks different from ours. The church should take persistent, unrepentant sin seriously, but it should also be patient with those who are weak, teachable, and genuinely seeking to walk in obedience.

12. Repentance, assurance, and struggling believers

Some Christians become troubled because they know their repentance is imperfect. They remember sins they have repeated, wonder whether their sorrow was deep enough, or fear that a particular struggle proves they were never sincere. It is right to take sin seriously, but it is dangerous to make the quality of our repentance the foundation of our assurance.

Our confidence rests first in Jesus Christ: his finished work, his promise to receive those who come to him, and his faithfulness to keep his people (John 6:37; Romans 8:1; Hebrews 7:25). We do not look inward until we find a perfect degree of sorrow. We look to Christ, who saves sinners.

At the same time, Scripture does call us to examine ourselves. A person who claims to know God while persistently refusing his commands has reason to question that profession (1 John 2:3–6; 2 Corinthians 13:5). The evidence is not sinless perfection, but a real pattern of faith, repentance, confession, and growth. A struggling believer who hates sin, grieves over it, seeks help, and returns to Christ should not conclude that every struggle proves the absence of grace.

When conscience is troubled, do not try to manufacture a stronger feeling. Confess what is true, turn from the sin you know, seek help from mature believers where needed, and rest again in the mercy of God through Christ. Assurance is strengthened as we hear and believe God's promises and see the fruit of his work in our lives.

13. Questions people often ask

Must I stop sinning before I can come to Christ?

No. You come to Christ because you are a sinner who needs mercy, not because you have already made yourself clean. But you must not come with a settled intention to keep defending your sin. Christ receives sinners and begins to change those who belong to him. Come honestly, asking him to save and transform you.

Can someone repent without feeling emotional?

Yes. Strong feelings may accompany repentance, but Scripture does not prescribe a particular emotional experience. What matters is a genuine change of heart and direction toward God. Tears are not the measure of repentance, and their absence does not prove that repentance is false.

Is repentance a one-time event or a lifelong practice?

There is a decisive repentance associated with conversion: the sinner turns to God in response to the gospel. Christians also continue to repent as they recognise and confess sin throughout their lives. Ongoing repentance is part of growth in grace, not repeated self-salvation.

Does repentance mean making restitution for every sin?

Where we have harmed someone or taken what belongs to another, we should seek to make things right where it is possible and wise to do so. Zacchaeus is an example of repentance bearing practical fruit (Luke 19:1–10). But not every consequence can be reversed, and restitution is not a payment that earns God's forgiveness. We make things right because repentance produces honesty and love.

Can a person be forgiven after serious sin?

Yes. The gospel is for sinners, including those whose sins are grievous. Scripture gives us examples of people who sinned seriously and were restored through God's mercy. The invitation is not to minimise evil, but to come to the merciful God who forgives those who repent and trust in Christ (Psalm 51; Luke 15:11–24; 1 Corinthians 6:9–11).

14. The call to repent and come to Christ

Repentance is not a ladder by which we climb high enough to reach God. It is the turning of a sinner who has stopped defending his rebellion and comes to the Lord for mercy. The gospel does not ask you to present a record of moral improvement. It announces Jesus Christ: the Son of God who lived in perfect obedience, died for sinners, and rose bodily from the dead.

God commands all people everywhere to repent (Acts 17:30). This is not a call to delay until you feel worthy. There is no worthiness to achieve before coming to Christ. Turn from sin and trust him. Do not put your confidence in a past prayer, a religious background, a church connection, or your ability to reform yourself. Look to Jesus, whose death is sufficient and whose invitation is genuine.

If you are aware of sin, do not hide it or make excuses. Confess it to God. Ask him for mercy. Turn from it and seek to walk in obedience by his grace. If you are unsure how to come to Christ, begin with the plain promise of the gospel: whoever believes in the Son has eternal life (John 3:16–18). The Saviour who calls sinners to repent is the same Saviour who receives all who come to him.

And if you already belong to Christ, keep walking in the light. Do not become casual about sin, but do not imagine that your failures are stronger than his mercy. Confess your sins, pursue holiness, forgive as you have been forgiven, and keep your eyes on Jesus. The Christian life is not a life without repentance; it is a life continually shaped by the grace of the God who has brought us to himself.

What Is the Doctrine of Adoption?

Understanding how God makes believers his children through Jesus Christ, gives them the Spirit, and welcomes them into the privileges of his family.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

There are truths in the Christian faith that are so familiar to us that we can begin to overlook how astonishing they are. We speak about God as our Father. We call one another brothers and sisters. We pray, “Our Father in heaven,” and sing about being children of God. But have we stopped to consider what it means for people who were once alienated from God to be welcomed into his own family?

The Bible does not describe salvation only as the cancellation of a debt or the removal of a guilty verdict. Those are wonderful realities, and neither should ever be diminished. But God's saving work reaches further. He does not merely pardon his enemies and leave them at a distance. Through Jesus Christ, he brings those who believe into his household. He gives them the standing of sons and daughters, the Spirit of adoption, access to himself, a new family, and the promise of an inheritance that cannot perish.

This is the doctrine of adoption. It is one of the great privileges of belonging to Christ, and it tells us something deeply personal about the purpose of salvation: God saves a people for himself, not simply from judgment, but also for fellowship with him.

To understand adoption properly, we must keep Christ at the centre. We do not become God's children by natural birth, religious effort, or moral improvement. We become his children by grace, through union with his Son. The Father adopts; the Son secures our place; and the Holy Spirit bears witness that we belong to God.

The central truth: Adoption is God's gracious act of bringing those who are united to Jesus Christ into his family, giving them the status and privileges of his children. It is not something we earn. It is a gift secured by the Son and confirmed in the hearts of believers by the Holy Spirit.
“See what kind of love the Father has given to us, that we should be called children of God; and so we are.”1 John 3:1

1. What is adoption?

In ordinary life, adoption is the act by which someone is formally welcomed into a family and given the standing of a son or daughter. The adopted child is not treated as a temporary guest or a distant acquaintance. He or she is received into the family, given a place, and brought under the care and love of the parents. The relationship is real, even though it did not arise through natural descent.

The Bible uses this language to describe a remarkable aspect of salvation. God does not become our Father in the same way that he is the eternal Father of the Lord Jesus Christ. Christ is the Son by nature; believers become God's children by grace. Yet the relationship he gives us is not pretend or merely symbolic. Those who are in Christ truly belong to God's household.

Paul writes that God “predestined us for adoption to himself as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will” (Ephesians 1:5). Notice the direction of the verse. Adoption begins with God's purpose, not with our initiative. It is something he planned and freely gives through Jesus Christ. The believer's place in the family is therefore not an achievement to boast about, but a gift that leads to praise.

Adoption should also be distinguished from justification and regeneration. In justification, God declares the sinner righteous on the basis of Christ's righteousness received through faith. In regeneration, the Holy Spirit gives spiritual life to one who was dead in sin. In adoption, God grants the believer the standing and privileges of a child in his family. These blessings belong together in salvation, but they are not identical. God does not merely change our legal standing; he also gives us a family relationship with himself.

2. Why do we need to be adopted?

It is tempting to speak of adoption as though it were simply an extra blessing added to an otherwise complete human life. Scripture gives us a more serious picture. By nature, we are sinners who have turned away from God. We are creatures made for him, yet we do not naturally live in the trust, love, and obedience for which we were created. Our problem is not that we are orphans waiting for a little encouragement. We are guilty and estranged from the God whose authority we have rejected.

Paul describes our former condition in Ephesians 2:1–3: dead in trespasses and sins, following the ways of the world, and under the just judgment of God. Later he says that Gentile believers were once “separated from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel and strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world” (Ephesians 2:12). That is not a flattering description of humanity, but it is an honest one.

Yet the passage does not end there. “But God, being rich in mercy, because of the great love with which he loved us,” made us alive with Christ (Ephesians 2:4–5). The same mercy that rescues us from death brings us near. Through Christ, those who were far off are brought near by his blood (Ephesians 2:13). Paul concludes that believers are no longer strangers and aliens, but members of God's household (Ephesians 2:19).

We need adoption because salvation is not simply escape from punishment. We were made for communion with God, and in Christ he restores us to himself. The Father does not merely open the prison door and send us away. He brings former rebels into his home.

3. Adoption comes through Jesus Christ

Every part of our salvation rests on the person and work of Jesus Christ. Adoption is no exception. Ephesians 1:5 says that God predestined us for adoption “through Jesus Christ.” Galatians 4:4–5 explains how this became possible: when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, to redeem those under the law, “so that we might receive adoption as sons.”

There is a beautiful order in Paul's words. The Son entered our condition in order to redeem us from it. He was born under the law and perfectly obeyed the Father. At the cross he bore the judgment due to sinners. He rose again, victorious over death. Those who trust in him are united to him and receive the benefits of his saving work. The Son who is eternally the Father's by nature brings redeemed sinners into the family by grace.

We must not confuse Christ's unique Sonship with ours. Jesus is not a son by adoption, nor is he one child of God among many. He is the eternal, only-begotten Son, sharing the divine nature with the Father and the Holy Spirit. Our sonship is dependent, received, and gracious. His is eternal and belongs to who he is. We become children only because we are joined to him.

John makes the same point when he writes that those who receive Christ and believe in his name are given the right to become children of God, “who were born, not of blood nor of the will of the flesh nor of the will of man, but of God” (John 1:12–13). Our family standing does not come from ancestry, human decision, or religious heritage. It comes through Christ and the new birth God gives.

“For in Christ Jesus you are all sons of God, through faith.”Galatians 3:26

That is why there is no other route into God's family. A person cannot bypass the Son and claim the Father on different terms. Jesus himself said, “No one comes to the Father except through me” (John 14:6). The welcome of the Father is wonderfully wide to all who come through Christ, but Christ himself is the only Mediator.

4. Adoption is an act of God's grace

One of the most humbling things about adoption is that it leaves no room for self-congratulation. God does not adopt us because he finds something in us that makes us worthy of his family. He does not look down the corridors of time, discover that we will make excellent children, and then owe us a place. Scripture traces adoption to “the purpose of his will” and to the riches of his grace (Ephesians 1:5–7).

This does not mean that God loves sin or overlooks the evil in us. His grace is not indifference to holiness. The cost of our welcome was not ignored; it was borne by Christ. The cross shows both the seriousness of our sin and the astonishing love of God. The Father does not welcome us by pretending that justice does not matter. He provides redemption through the blood of his beloved Son.

Nor does adoption mean that we earn God's favour by first making ourselves into obedient children. The gospel reverses that way of thinking. God receives sinners through Christ, gives them a new standing, and then teaches them to live as members of his family. Obedience is the fruit of belonging, not the purchase price of belonging.

When we understand this, pride has nowhere to stand. The Christian cannot look at another person and boast that he was naturally more deserving of God's love. If we are children of God, it is because God has been merciful to us. The proper response is gratitude, humility, worship, and a growing desire to please the Father who has shown us such kindness.

5. Are all people children of God?

There is a sense in which every human being belongs to God as Creator. Every person is made in his image (Genesis 1:26–27), receives life from him, and remains accountable to him. God is the Father of all in the sense that he is the Maker and sovereign Lord of all. The Bible can use fatherly language in this broad, creational sense.

But Scripture also speaks of a distinct relationship that belongs to those who are in Christ. John 1:12 does not say that everyone already possesses this saving privilege in exactly the same way. It says that those who receive Christ and believe in his name are given the right to become children of God. Galatians 3:26 likewise connects this sonship with faith in Christ. Adoption is not simply another way of saying that every person is created by God.

This distinction matters because it keeps the gospel clear. If everyone is already a child of God in the full saving sense, the Bible's call to repent and believe becomes difficult to understand. But if we recognise the difference between God's relationship to all people as Creator and his gracious adoption of believers in Christ, we can speak honestly about both God's universal rule and the particular saving privileges given to his people.

There is no reason for this teaching to make Christians proud or dismissive. We were not adopted because we were better. We were shown mercy. The right response is to invite others to come to Christ, knowing that everyone who comes to him will be received.

6. The Spirit of adoption

Adoption is not merely a change of status written in a heavenly record. God gives his people the Holy Spirit, who works in them and assures them that they belong to him. Paul writes, “For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, ‘Abba! Father!’” (Romans 8:15).

The word “Abba” expresses the closeness and confidence of a child approaching a father. It is not careless familiarity or an attempt to make God small. The God we approach remains holy, sovereign, and worthy of reverence. Yet through Christ, we are invited to come to him with the trust of children who know that they are welcomed.

Paul continues: “The Spirit himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God” (Romans 8:16). The Spirit's witness is not necessarily a dramatic feeling or an audible voice. He assures believers by working through the truth of the gospel, directing our hearts to Christ, producing the character of God's children, and moving us to call upon God as Father. He helps us understand and rest in what God has promised.

Galatians 4:6 says that because we are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba! Father!” Notice again that the Spirit's presence is connected to the work of the Son. The Christian's confidence is not built on an ability to generate religious emotion. It rests on God's promise in Christ, applied to the heart by the Holy Spirit.

7. The privileges of God's children

To be adopted by God is to receive privileges that no human achievement could secure. These privileges do not make us equal to God, erase the distinction between Creator and creature, or give us a right to demand whatever we want. They describe the generous care of the Father toward those who belong to Christ.

Access to the Father

Because of Christ, believers can approach God in prayer. We do not come as strangers hoping that he might listen. We come through the Son, in the Spirit, to the Father (Ephesians 2:18). Hebrews 4:16 calls us to draw near to the throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need. This confidence is not arrogance; it is trust in the welcome God has provided.

The Father's care and discipline

God's care does not mean that his children are spared every hardship. It means that nothing in their lives falls outside his wise and fatherly rule. Hebrews 12:5–11 explains that the Lord disciplines those he loves. His discipline is not the condemning punishment that Christ bore for his people. It is the correction of a Father who is committed to their holiness. It may be painful, but it is never pointless or cruel.

A new identity and belonging

Our deepest identity is no longer determined by our past, our failures, our social standing, or the approval of other people. We belong to God. This does not make our earthly relationships or responsibilities unimportant, but it gives them their proper place. The Christian is loved by the Father and called to reflect the family likeness in holiness, truth, mercy, and love.

Freedom from slavery to sin

Paul contrasts the Spirit of adoption with the spirit of slavery (Romans 8:15). Those who belong to Christ are no longer under sin's rightful dominion. They still struggle, and they must continue to put sin to death by the Spirit (Romans 8:12–13), but they are not abandoned to serve their former master. The Father is teaching his children to walk in freedom and obedience.

8. Adoption and the church

Adoption gives believers a Father, and it also gives them brothers and sisters. The church is not merely an audience gathered around religious interests. It is the household of God, made up of people who have been brought near through the same Saviour and given the same Spirit. Ephesians 2:19 says that believers are “members of the household of God.”

This truth should change the way Christians treat one another. We do not choose our brothers and sisters according to personality, background, income, ethnicity, or usefulness. Christ has joined us to one another. We are called to bear with one another, forgive one another, encourage one another, and pursue peace without compromising the truth. A church that speaks warmly about the Father but treats his children with contempt has forgotten something important about the gospel.

It also means that no Christian should regard himself as spiritually self-sufficient. God ordinarily strengthens his children through the ministry of the church: the preaching of the Word, prayer, the ordinances Christ gave, pastoral care, and the encouragement of fellow believers. We need one another. The family of God is a gift, not an optional accessory to private faith.

At the same time, the church must never take the place of God. Pastors and fellow Christians are not the source of our adoption. They are fellow servants and family members under the same Father. Our final confidence is in Christ, who brings us to God.

9. Adoption in suffering and weakness

One of the most painful misunderstandings of God's fatherhood is the assumption that if he truly loves us, life should become easy. Scripture never promises that. Romans 8, one of the clearest passages about adoption, speaks in the same breath about suffering, groaning, weakness, and hope. God's children live in a fallen world. They experience sickness, bereavement, persecution, disappointment, and the consequences of their own sins and the sins of others.

But suffering does not mean that the Father has disowned his children. Romans 8:17 says that if we are children, then we are heirs—“heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ, provided we suffer with him in order that we may also be glorified with him.” Paul does not pretend that the road to glory is painless. He insists that suffering with Christ is not the end of the story. The glory to be revealed is greater than the sufferings of this present time (Romans 8:18).

Sometimes a believer's circumstances seem to say, “You have been forgotten.” The gospel answers with something stronger than circumstances: God has given his Son for us and has joined us to him. If he has done that, our trials cannot separate us from his love. Romans 8:31–39 grounds Christian confidence not in an easy life, but in the unbreakable love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.

There will be moments when we do not know how to pray. Romans 8:26 says that the Spirit helps us in our weakness. Even when our words fail, we are not abandoned. Our Father knows our need, the Son intercedes for us, and the Spirit helps us. Adoption does not remove every tear now; it assures us that our tears are not shed outside the care of God.

10. The inheritance of God's children

In human adoption, a child is welcomed into a family and receives a share in its life and future. Scripture uses inheritance language to describe the future God has promised his people. Romans 8:17 says that children of God are heirs of God and fellow heirs with Christ. Peter describes this inheritance as imperishable, undefiled, and unfading, kept in heaven for believers (1 Peter 1:3–5).

This inheritance is not a promise of earthly wealth, guaranteed success, or freedom from ordinary hardship. It is the fullness of life with God: resurrection, the renewal of creation, freedom from sin and death, and the joy of seeing the Lord. The Christian hope is not merely that our present circumstances will improve. It is that Christ will return, his people will be raised, and they will share in the glory he has secured.

There is a sense in which believers already possess the inheritance because they already belong to Christ. Yet they await its full enjoyment. The Holy Spirit is described as the guarantee of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it (Ephesians 1:13–14). We live between promise and fulfilment, trusting the Father who has pledged himself to finish what he began.

That future changes how we live now. We do not need to build our entire identity around temporary possessions, recognition, or comfort. We can serve faithfully, give generously, endure hardship, and set our hope on the grace that will be brought to us at the revelation of Jesus Christ (1 Peter 1:13).

11. Adoption and assurance

Can a Christian be certain that God really receives him as a child? The Bible gives real grounds for assurance, but it does not teach us to base confidence on a passing feeling or on a claim that we have never sinned. Our assurance begins with the character and promise of God and the finished work of Christ. The one who comes to Jesus will not be cast out (John 6:37). There is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1).

The Spirit also bears witness with our spirit that we are God's children (Romans 8:16). And the family likeness begins to appear in the believer's life. We learn to hate what is evil, love what is good, confess our sins, seek holiness, and love the people whom God has made our brothers and sisters. These things do not purchase adoption. They are evidence of the Spirit's work in those whom God has received.

Believers can nevertheless experience seasons of doubt. Some are troubled by repeated failures; others struggle to feel the comfort of God's fatherly love. We should not tell such people to ignore Scripture or to manufacture certainty. We should direct them to Christ, remind them of God's promises, encourage honest repentance, and help them make use of the ordinary means by which God strengthens faith. A bruised reed he will not break (Isaiah 42:3; Matthew 12:20).

It is also important to distinguish the Father's discipline from condemnation. When God exposes sin in the life of his child, he is not declaring that Christ's work has failed. He is correcting and sanctifying someone who belongs to him. The right response is not to hide from God, but to confess sin and return to him through Christ (1 John 1:9).

12. Questions people often ask

Is adoption the same as being born again?

No. Regeneration is the work of the Holy Spirit by which he gives spiritual life. Adoption is God's gracious act of granting believers the standing and privileges of his children. They are distinct blessings, but they belong together in salvation. Those whom God makes alive in Christ are also welcomed into his family.

Does God adopt people because he knows they will believe?

Scripture presents adoption as flowing from God's gracious purpose and being accomplished through Jesus Christ (Ephesians 1:4–7). It does not present God's saving choice as a reward for foreseen human merit. Faith is necessary as the means by which we receive Christ, but faith itself is not a ground for boasting. Salvation is of grace from beginning to end.

Can someone be God's child and still struggle with sin?

Yes. God's children are not yet perfected. They still face temptation and need to confess their sins. But grace does not make peace with sin. The Spirit leads believers to put sin to death and grow in holiness (Romans 8:12–14). The presence of struggle is not proof that adoption is false; a settled refusal to turn from sin should never be excused by appealing to grace.

Does calling God “Father” mean we can approach him casually?

No. The privilege of adoption gives us confidence, not irreverence. We come to the holy God with the trust of children who have been welcomed through Christ. The Lord's Prayer itself joins intimacy with reverence: “Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name” (Matthew 6:9).

Will adoption ever be completed?

Our status as God's children is already real, but we still await the full public and bodily enjoyment of that privilege. Romans 8:23 speaks of believers waiting eagerly for adoption, “the redemption of our bodies.” The final resurrection will reveal openly what God has already made true in Christ.

What if my earthly experience of fatherhood has been painful?

Scripture's picture of God as Father is not a command to pretend that every human father has been loving or safe. Human fathers can fail grievously. God is not measured by their failures. He is the perfect Father, whose wisdom, justice, patience, and love are without defect. For someone wounded by an earthly father, this truth may take time to receive emotionally, but the character of God remains a secure foundation.

13. Living as children of God

If you belong to Christ, do not treat adoption as a small detail in your salvation. It tells you whose you are. You are not an unwanted visitor in God's house, hoping that he will tolerate your presence. Through the Son, you have been brought near. You may call upon the Father, confess your sins, seek his help, receive his correction, and trust his promises.

Live, then, in a way that reflects the family to which you belong. Put away bitterness, dishonesty, impurity, pride, and cruelty. Learn to forgive because you have been forgiven. Show patience because your Father has been patient with you. Love fellow believers as members of the same household. Pursue holiness, not to persuade God to adopt you, but because he has already welcomed you in Christ and is making you more like his Son.

If you have not come to Christ, do not assume that being created by God is the same as possessing the saving privilege of adoption. The invitation of the gospel is real: turn from sin and trust in Jesus Christ. He is the Son who came to redeem sinners and bring them to the Father. No one who comes to him in faith will be turned away.

And for every believer, let the wonder of this truth lead to worship. The holy God who made heaven and earth has not only forgiven his people; he has made them his own. The love that welcomes us is not earned, the Son who secures it is sufficient, and the Spirit who assures us is faithful. We have been given a Father, a family, and an inheritance that will never fade.

“Beloved, we are God’s children now, and what we will be has not yet appeared; but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is.”1 John 3:2

  • Romans 8:12–30 — The Spirit of adoption, suffering, inheritance, and future glory.
  • Galatians 3:23–4:7 — Faith in Christ and receiving adoption as sons.
  • John 1:9–13 — Receiving Christ and becoming children of God.
  • Ephesians 2:1–22 — From alienation to membership in God's household.

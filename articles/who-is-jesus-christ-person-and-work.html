Who Is Jesus Christ? His Person and Work

The Christian faith stands or falls on the identity of Jesus. What does Scripture say about who he is, why he came, what he accomplished, and what it means to follow him?
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

Jesus Christ is not simply a moral teacher, a prophet among many, or an inspiring example. The New Testament presents him as the eternal Son of God who truly became human, lived in perfect obedience, died for sinners, rose bodily from the dead, and reigns as Lord. Christianity is built around this person and these events.

This study follows the Bible's own testimony. It will distinguish what Scripture clearly teaches from questions on which Christians have reached different conclusions. The aim is not to force a label onto Jesus, but to listen carefully to what God has revealed about his Son.

1. The identity of Jesus: truly God

The first question is not merely what Jesus did, but who he is. The opening of John's Gospel deliberately echoes Genesis: “In the beginning was the Word.” John says the Word was with God, was God, and that all things came into being through him (John 1:1–3). The Word is then identified as Jesus Christ, who became flesh and lived among us (John 1:14, 17).

Jesus is distinct from the Father, yet he shares the divine identity. This is not the claim that Jesus is the Father. At his baptism, the Son is in the water, the Spirit descends, and the Father's voice speaks from heaven (Matthew 3:16–17). The persons are distinguishable; Scripture nevertheless speaks of the Son as truly God.

Thomas, having encountered the risen Jesus, addresses him, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28). Jesus does not correct him. Paul describes Christ as existing in the form of God and sharing divine glory (Philippians 2:6–11). Hebrews says the Son is the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of his being (Hebrews 1:1–4). The Son is also worshipped by angels (Hebrews 1:6).

These passages do not present Jesus as a created angel or merely an unusually gifted man. The Son existed before creation, shares the Father's divine glory, and is worthy of honour. Jesus himself says that all should honour the Son just as they honour the Father (John 5:23).

2. The Son became truly human

The eternal Son did not merely appear to be human. He took a real human nature. John writes that “the Word became flesh” (John 1:14). Paul says that God sent his Son, “born of a woman” (Galatians 4:4). Jesus experienced hunger (Matthew 4:2), thirst (John 19:28), tiredness (John 4:6), grief (John 11:35), and physical suffering.

Hebrews teaches that he shared in flesh and blood and was made like his brothers and sisters in every respect, yet without sin (Hebrews 2:14–18; 4:15). His humanity was not a costume. He had a human body, mind, emotions, and will. He could represent human beings because he truly became one of us.

At the same time, Jesus did not cease to be God. The one person of the Son possesses both a divine nature and a human nature. Scripture does not ask us to imagine two Jesuses, nor does it confuse his humanity with his deity. It presents one Lord Jesus Christ, truly God and truly man.

3. His life and teaching: the obedient Son

Jesus came announcing the kingdom of God and calling people to repent and believe the good news (Mark 1:14–15). He taught with authority, revealed the character of the Father, welcomed the needy, confronted hypocrisy, forgave sins, and showed compassion to the suffering. His teaching was not detached from his identity: he declared that seeing him was to see the Father (John 14:9), and that he had come to do the Father's will (John 6:38).

Jesus was tempted, yet he did not sin (Hebrews 4:15). Where Adam disobeyed, Jesus remained faithful. Paul contrasts the disobedience of one man with the obedience of the one man, Jesus Christ (Romans 5:18–19). His sinless life was not incidental; the one who gives himself for sinners is himself righteous.

His miracles were signs of the kingdom and witnesses to his identity. He healed the sick, cleansed lepers, gave sight to the blind, commanded the storm, and raised the dead. These works displayed compassion and authority, but they also pointed beyond themselves to the arrival of God's promised salvation (Matthew 11:2–6; John 20:30–31).

4. Why Jesus died: the cross and our salvation

Jesus' death was not an accident that defeated his mission. He repeatedly spoke of giving his life. He said the Son of Man came “to give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45). At the Last Supper, he spoke of his blood being poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins (Matthew 26:28).

Scripture explains the cross in several connected ways. Jesus bore our sins (1 Peter 2:24); he gave himself as a ransom (1 Timothy 2:5–6); he reconciled enemies to God (Romans 5:10); he redeemed people from the curse (Galatians 3:13); and he disarmed the powers through the cross (Colossians 2:13–15). These are not competing accounts. Together they show the depth and reach of what Christ accomplished.

God does not simply pretend that evil does not matter. At the cross, sin is judged and mercy is offered. Paul says God put Christ forward as a sacrifice of atonement, demonstrating God's righteousness so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus (Romans 3:23–26). The cross reveals both the seriousness of sin and the astonishing love of God (Romans 5:8).

Jesus' sacrifice is sufficient and decisive. The letter to the Hebrews contrasts the repeated sacrifices of the old covenant with Christ's once-for-all offering (Hebrews 9:24–28; 10:11–14). No human achievement can supplement his finished work as though his sacrifice were incomplete. We receive salvation as a gift, not as wages earned by our religious performance (Ephesians 2:8–9).

5. The resurrection: death did not have the final word

The New Testament does not describe the resurrection as merely the survival of Jesus' influence or the disciples' renewed hope. It announces that God raised Jesus bodily from the dead. The tomb was found empty, and the risen Jesus appeared to his followers (Luke 24:1–8, 36–43; John 20:24–29). The apostles proclaimed this as a real event and made it central to their message (Acts 2:22–36; 1 Corinthians 15:3–8).

Paul is direct: if Christ has not been raised, Christian preaching is empty and faith is futile (1 Corinthians 15:14, 17). But Christ has been raised, the firstfruits of those who have died (1 Corinthians 15:20). His resurrection declares that his sacrifice was not defeated, that death's rule has been broken, and that those who belong to him have a sure hope beyond the grave.

The resurrection also confirms Jesus' identity and vindicates his mission. The one crucified in weakness now lives in victory. Christian hope is therefore not wishful thinking or a vague belief that things will somehow work out. It rests on the risen Christ.

6. His ascension and present reign

After his resurrection, Jesus appeared to his disciples and was taken up into heaven (Acts 1:1–11). His ascension does not mean that he has abandoned his people. Scripture presents him as exalted at the Father's right hand, reigning with authority (Ephesians 1:20–23; Hebrews 1:3).

Jesus is the head of the church (Colossians 1:18). He intercedes for his people (Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25), gives gifts for the building up of his body (Ephesians 4:7–13), and possesses all authority in heaven and on earth (Matthew 28:18). Christian worship, mission, and obedience take place under his lordship.

This means Jesus is not merely a figure from the past. The church does not preserve the memory of a dead founder; it belongs to a living Lord. Christians are called to trust him, obey his teaching, love one another, and make disciples among all nations (Matthew 28:19–20).

7. His promised return and the renewal of all things

The same Jesus who ascended will return (Acts 1:11). Scripture teaches that he will judge the living and the dead (Acts 17:31; 2 Timothy 4:1), raise the dead (John 5:28–29), defeat every enemy, and bring God's purposes to completion (1 Corinthians 15:24–26). The final hope of believers is not escape into an immaterial existence, but resurrection life in the renewed creation (Romans 8:18–25; Revelation 21:1–5).

Christians differ on the details and sequence of events surrounding the return of Christ, including how to understand certain prophetic passages. Those disagreements should be handled carefully and without pretending that every detail is equally clear. Yet the central promise is plain: Jesus will return, evil will not endure, judgment will be righteous, and God will dwell with his people.

8. What should we do with Jesus?

Jesus does not leave people with the option of admiring him from a safe distance while ignoring his claims. He calls people to repent and believe the gospel (Mark 1:15). The apostles call everyone to turn to God and trust in the Lord Jesus (Acts 20:21; 16:30–31).

To believe in Christ is more than agreeing that he existed or that certain facts about him are true. It is to entrust yourself to him: to depend on his finished work, receive his mercy, and submit to him as Lord. Repentance is a real turning from sin to God. Neither repentance nor obedience purchases salvation; they belong to the response of a person who is turning to Christ.

Those who trust Christ are united to him, forgiven, adopted into God's family, and given the Holy Spirit (Romans 8:1–17; Galatians 4:4–7). They begin a new life of good works prepared by God (Ephesians 2:10). Christian obedience is not an attempt to make Jesus accept us; it is the grateful response of those who have received grace.

9. Common questions

Did Jesus ever claim to be God?

Jesus' claims should be read in their full setting. He spoke of sharing the Father's glory before the world existed (John 17:5), said “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30), and used language that his hearers understood as a claim reaching beyond ordinary human status (John 8:58–59). His works, authority to forgive sins, acceptance of worship, and the testimony of the apostles all contribute to the New Testament's presentation of his divine identity.

If Jesus is God, how could he pray to the Father?

Because the Son is not the Father. In becoming human, the Son lived in genuine dependence and obedience to the Father. His prayers reveal the real relationship between the Father and the Son, not a denial of his deity (John 17; Hebrews 5:7–9).

Could Jesus have sinned?

Scripture teaches both that Jesus was genuinely tempted and that he was without sin (Hebrews 4:15; 1 Peter 2:22). Christians have discussed how to explain the relation between his divine identity and human temptation. We should not claim more than Scripture reveals. What is clear is that Jesus truly faced temptation and remained perfectly faithful.

Why is Jesus the only way to God?

Jesus himself says that no one comes to the Father except through him (John 14:6). Peter declares that salvation is found in no one else (Acts 4:12), and Paul teaches that there is one mediator between God and humanity, Christ Jesus (1 Timothy 2:5). This claim is exclusive, but it is also an invitation: the gospel is proclaimed to all peoples, and everyone who calls on the Lord will be saved (Romans 10:9–13).

What is the difference between Jesus and other religious teachers?

Christianity does not present Jesus merely as one wise teacher among many. Its central claim is that the eternal Son became human, died for sins, rose bodily, and reigns as Lord. The question is therefore not simply whether Jesus offered valuable moral advice, but whether the apostolic testimony about him is true. The New Testament invites readers to examine that testimony and respond to Christ.

Conclusion: the centre of the Christian faith

Christianity is not first a collection of moral instructions or religious customs. It is the good news about Jesus Christ: the eternal Son who became human, lived without sin, gave himself for sinners, rose bodily, ascended to the Father's right hand, and will return in glory. His identity explains the worth of his work; his work is the ground of our hope.

The right response is not merely to know facts about Jesus, but to come to him in faith. Read the Gospel accounts for yourself. Listen to his words, consider the testimony concerning his death and resurrection, and take seriously his call to repent and believe. There is no better foundation for life, death, and eternity than the Lord Jesus Christ.

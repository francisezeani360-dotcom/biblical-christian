Christian apologetics · The identity of Jesus

Who Was Jesus? Did He Really Claim to Be God?

A close look at the words Jesus used, the authority he exercised, the claims his hearers understood him to be making, and the confession of the earliest Christians.
A Scripture-centred study · Biblical Christian

Few questions matter more than this one. Christians do not simply believe that Jesus was a wise teacher, a courageous prophet, or an unusually good man. They confess that he is the eternal Son of God who became man for our salvation. That is a much greater claim, and it deserves to be examined rather than repeated as a slogan.

“Before Abraham was, I am.”John 8:58 — reference for study

Some people say Jesus never claimed to be God. They point out that he never speaks the exact sentence, “I am God; worship me.” Others answer by collecting a handful of verses without explaining what those passages meant in their original setting. Neither approach gets us very far. We need to listen to Jesus in the context of his words and actions, and we need to pay attention to how his first hearers understood him.

The New Testament presents Jesus as truly human. He was born, grew, became tired, wept, prayed, suffered, and died. It also presents him as more than a human messenger. He speaks and acts with an authority that belongs to God, identifies himself with the divine identity, receives honour that belongs to God, and is confessed by his followers as Lord. The question is whether this picture comes from the texts themselves or has been read into them later.

We should be precise. Jesus does not use the exact modern wording, “I am God,” as a stand-alone formula. But that does not settle what he claimed. People communicate identity in many ways: by what they say about themselves, the authority they exercise, the titles they accept, and the things they claim the right to do. When the whole testimony is considered, the case is considerably stronger than a search for one particular sentence would suggest.

This article focuses on that question. It is not a substitute for our wider study, Who Is Jesus Christ? His Person and Work, which considers his incarnation, saving work, resurrection, and reign. Here we are asking a more focused question: did Jesus present himself as someone who shares the identity and glory of God?

A necessary distinction: Christians do not mean that Jesus is the Father, or that the Father and Son are two gods. The historic Christian confession is that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are distinct persons, yet there is one God. The Son is fully divine and became truly human; he did not cease to be God when he took on our nature.

1. What does it mean to say Jesus is God?

Before turning to individual passages, we need to be clear about the claim. Christians are not saying that Jesus is the same person as the Father. Jesus speaks to the Father, loves the Father, obeys the Father, and is sent by the Father. Those relationships are real. The Son is not the Father.

Nor are Christians saying that Jesus is a second god alongside the God of Israel. The Bible is uncompromising that there is one God (Deuteronomy 6:4; Isaiah 45:5–6; 1 Corinthians 8:4–6). The Christian claim is that the one eternal God exists as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Son shares the divine nature; he is not a creature who was promoted to a divine position.

This is why the incarnation matters. The eternal Son took a complete human nature without surrendering his deity. Jesus could truly hunger because he was man, and yet he could speak with divine authority because he is the Son. His humanity was not an appearance or disguise. He became like us in every respect necessary to be our representative, while remaining without sin (Hebrews 2:14–18; 4:15).

When we ask whether Jesus claimed to be God, then, we are asking whether he presented himself as sharing God's unique identity, authority, works, honour, and glory. We should not force later theological vocabulary into every sentence he spoke. But we should also not ignore what his words meant to people who knew the Scriptures and heard him in his own setting.

2. The Word who was God

John begins his Gospel with a statement that leaves no room for treating Jesus as merely a human teacher. He writes that in the beginning was the Word, that the Word was with God, and that the Word was God (John 1:1). The Word is both distinguished from God in personal relationship—he was “with God”—and identified as God by nature—he “was God.” John then says that all things were made through him, and that this Word became flesh and dwelt among us (John 1:3, 14).

John is not describing a created being who came into existence before the rest of creation. If all things that came into being were made through the Word, the Word himself cannot belong to the category of things made. He is on the Creator's side of the distinction, not among the things created. The same Gospel later identifies this Word with Jesus Christ (John 1:14, 17).

Some object that the wording of John 1:1 does not mean the Word is truly God. But the sentence must be read as a whole. John carefully holds together distinction and identity: the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He is not saying that the Word is the same person as the Father; he is saying that the Word shares the divine nature.

This is not an isolated thought added at the end of the Gospel's development. John builds his account around it. Jesus speaks of the glory he had with the Father before the world existed (John 17:5). He says that he came down from heaven (John 6:38). He speaks of the Father's love for him before the foundation of the world (John 17:24). The Jesus John presents is a real man in history, but his existence did not begin at Bethlehem.

3. “Before Abraham was, I am”

In John 8, Jesus is speaking with people who challenge his testimony and question his identity. The conversation turns to Abraham. Jesus says that Abraham rejoiced to see his day, and his hearers object that he is not yet fifty years old. How, they ask, could he have seen Abraham?

Jesus answers: “Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am” (John 8:58). Notice the contrast. He does not simply say, “Before Abraham was, I was.” He uses the expression “I am.” The statement points to an existence that precedes Abraham and carries a weight that his hearers clearly recognise.

The reaction is important. They pick up stones to throw at him (John 8:59). We should not assume that every angry reaction proves a speaker has made a divine claim. But in this context, the reaction fits the seriousness of what Jesus has said. The surrounding conversation concerns his identity and his relationship to the Father. His words are not received as a harmless claim to have existed a long time ago.

The expression also recalls the language of God's self-revelation in Exodus 3:14, though interpreters debate the precise relationship between the two expressions. The argument should not rest on a simplistic claim that the English words are identical in every respect. The larger point is clearer: Jesus claims an existence prior to Abraham and speaks in a way that places him far beyond the category of an ordinary descendant of Abraham.

That claim is consistent with the opening of John's Gospel. The Word existed in the beginning; Abraham came much later. Jesus is not merely saying that he is older than one of Israel's ancestors. He is revealing something about who he is.

4. “I and the Father are one”

John 10 records Jesus speaking about his sheep. He says that he gives them eternal life, that they will never perish, and that no one can snatch them out of his hand. He then speaks of the Father's hand and says, “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30).

The word translated “one” is neuter in Greek. Jesus is not saying that he and the Father are one person. The statement expresses unity, and the surrounding context shows that this unity is far more than a shared opinion. Jesus places his own hand alongside the Father's hand as the power that keeps the sheep secure. He claims a divine work and an inseparable unity in the preservation of God's people.

Once again, the response of his hearers matters. They take up stones and say that they are going to stone him for blasphemy, because, being a man, he makes himself God (John 10:31–33). Jesus does not respond by saying, “You have misunderstood; I am only claiming to be a good representative.” Instead, he continues to defend the legitimacy of his claim and speaks of the Father being in him and him in the Father (John 10:34–38).

It is sometimes argued that Jesus' appeal to Psalm 82 means he is only calling himself one of many “gods.” But his argument in that passage is a defence against the charge brought against him, not a withdrawal of his unique claims. He immediately returns to the works he does in the Father's name and to the mutual indwelling of Father and Son. The passage should be read in its full argument, not reduced to one word.

Jesus is not teaching that the Father and Son are interchangeable. He is claiming a unity so profound that his works, his protection of the sheep, and his relationship with the Father cannot be separated.

5. Authority to forgive sins

In Mark 2, a paralysed man is brought to Jesus. Before healing him, Jesus says, “Son, your sins are forgiven” (Mark 2:5). Some scribes object inwardly: “Who can forgive sins but God alone?” Their theological instinct is right. Sin is ultimately committed against God, and God has the authority to forgive it.

Jesus does not tell them that forgiveness is unimportant or that they have misunderstood the seriousness of sin. Instead, he asks which is easier to say: that the man's sins are forgiven, or that he should rise, take up his bed, and walk. He then heals the man publicly so that they may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins (Mark 2:8–12).

Prophets could announce God's forgiveness. Priests could pronounce a person ceremonially clean according to God's law. But Jesus does something more direct: he speaks forgiveness on his own authority and confirms that authority with a miraculous healing. The miracle is not a party trick. It is a visible sign that his claim about the invisible matter of forgiveness is real.

Jesus also speaks of himself as the final judge and says that the Father has given judgment to the Son (John 5:22–23). In the Old Testament, final judgment belongs to God. Jesus' authority to forgive and judge is part of a wider pattern in which he exercises prerogatives that belong to God himself.

6. The honour given to the Son

One of Jesus' most striking statements appears in John 5. After speaking about his relationship with the Father, he says that the Father has entrusted judgment to the Son “that all may honour the Son, just as they honour the Father” (John 5:23). This is not a request for the respect given to a prophet or a king. The comparison is direct: the Son is to receive the same honour as the Father.

Jesus goes on to speak of giving life to whom he will (John 5:21). In Scripture, the giving of life and the final judgment of humanity are divine works. The Son does not act as an independent rival to the Father; he does what the Father does and acts in perfect unity with him (John 5:19–23). Yet the Son is personally distinct and is worthy of the same honour.

Jesus also receives worship in the Gospel narratives. After he walks on the sea and enters the boat, those with him worship him and confess that he is the Son of God (Matthew 14:33). After the resurrection, the disciples worship him (Matthew 28:9, 17). The New Testament does not present Jesus as a servant who must refuse divine honour. Instead, it repeatedly shows him receiving the response that belongs to the Lord.

We should be careful not to build the entire case on the English word “worship,” since the relevant biblical terms can also describe acts of homage in other settings. The force comes from the wider context: Jesus receives this honour in the setting of divine authority, resurrection, and the confession that he is God's Son. The apostles' worship of Jesus is not an accidental courtesy.

7. The Son of Man and Daniel's vision

Jesus often calls himself “the Son of Man.” Some readers assume this is simply a humble way of saying “a human being.” The expression can certainly emphasise his humanity, but its biblical background is richer than that.

In Daniel 7:13–14, Daniel sees “one like a son of man” coming with the clouds of heaven. He approaches the Ancient of Days and receives dominion, glory, and a kingdom. All peoples, nations, and languages serve him, and his dominion is everlasting. The vision distinguishes this figure from the Ancient of Days, yet gives him a universal and enduring reign.

Jesus draws on this passage when he speaks of the Son of Man coming with the clouds and being seated at the right hand of Power (Mark 14:62; Matthew 26:64). He is not merely claiming to be an ordinary man. He identifies himself with the heavenly figure who receives everlasting dominion and whose authority reaches every nation.

This helps explain why the title matters in the trial before the high priest. Jesus' opponents understand that he is making a claim about his heavenly authority and coming vindication. The title joins his genuine humanity to his unique place in God's rule. He is the Son of Man who suffers, but he is also the one who will come in glory.

8. The trial before the high priest

At Jesus' trial, the high priest asks whether he is the Christ, the Son of the Blessed. Jesus answers, “I am,” and then speaks of the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven (Mark 14:61–64). This brings together two important Old Testament passages: Psalm 110:1, where the Lord invites David's Lord to sit at his right hand, and Daniel 7:13–14, where the Son of Man receives everlasting dominion.

Jesus is claiming more than the role of a political liberator. He presents himself as the promised King who shares God's heavenly rule and will be vindicated by God. The high priest tears his garments and charges him with blasphemy. The precise legal and theological reasoning behind the charge has been discussed by scholars, but the scene clearly shows that Jesus' identity and authority are at the heart of the accusation.

It would be too simple to say that the word “Messiah” always meant “God” in the minds of first-century Jews. It did not. But Jesus' answer goes beyond a bare claim to be Messiah. By joining the Son of Man's heavenly enthronement with the language of Psalm 110, he claims a place of extraordinary authority at God's right hand.

The irony is central to the Gospel story. Jesus stands condemned before earthly authorities, yet he says that the day is coming when his true position will be revealed. The one being judged will be the universal judge.

9. Thomas's confession

After the resurrection, Thomas initially refuses to believe that the other disciples have seen the risen Jesus. He says that he must see the wounds for himself. When Jesus appears and invites him to examine the evidence, Thomas responds, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28).

Some suggest that Thomas is merely expressing surprise, as someone might exclaim, “Oh my God!” But the text gives no indication that John intends it as a careless outburst. Thomas addresses Jesus directly. Jesus answers him, and John presents the confession as the climax toward which the Gospel has been moving. The Gospel opened by declaring that the Word was God; it now ends this scene with a disciple confessing the risen Jesus as his Lord and God.

Jesus does not correct Thomas for blasphemy or redirect his worship toward the Father. He speaks about belief and the blessing of those who have not seen and yet have believed (John 20:29). The confession is not an isolated misunderstanding. It is the fitting response to the identity John has revealed throughout the Gospel.

The resurrection matters here. Thomas is not confessing a vague spiritual presence or an idea that lives on after death. He is addressing the crucified Jesus who now stands before him alive. The one who truly suffered and died is the one whom Thomas calls Lord and God.

10. What about the difficult passages?

If Jesus is God, why does he pray to the Father? Why does he say the Father is greater than he is? Why does he say that he does not know the day or hour? These are fair questions, and a biblical answer must take them seriously. The answer is not to deny Jesus' humanity or to pretend that the passages are easy. It is to remember that the eternal Son truly became man.

“The Father is greater than I” (John 14:28)

Jesus says this as he prepares his disciples for his departure. In the incarnation, the Son has taken the place of a servant. He has come to obey the Father and accomplish the work given to him (Philippians 2:6–8; John 6:38). The Father is greater in the order of the Son's incarnate mission, not greater in divine nature. John has already said that the Word was God (John 1:1), and Jesus has spoken of the glory he shared with the Father before the world existed (John 17:5). John 14:28 should not be made to cancel the rest of John's Gospel.

Jesus prays to the Father

Prayer does not prove that Jesus is a creature. It shows that he is the Son, personally distinct from the Father, and that he has truly taken our human nature. As man, Jesus lives in perfect dependence and obedience. His prayers are real, not theatre. The Christian doctrine of the Trinity does not confuse the persons; the Son can speak to the Father because he is not the Father.

“Of that day or hour no one knows” (Mark 13:32)

Jesus says that the Son does not know the day or hour, but the Father does. Christians have understood this passage in different ways, especially when explaining the relationship between Christ's divine knowledge and his human mind. The important point is not to solve the mystery by denying either nature. The Son assumed a genuine human life, including a human mind, and lived the humble mission appointed by the Father. The passage should be read alongside the New Testament's clear statements about his divine identity, not used to erase them.

“The only true God” (John 17:3)

Jesus addresses the Father as the only true God. This is fully consistent with belief in one God. The verse distinguishes the Father from the Son, but it does not say that the Son is a false god or a creature. In the same prayer Jesus speaks of the glory he shared with the Father before the world existed (John 17:5). John has already identified the Word as God (John 1:1), and Thomas will address Jesus as “My Lord and my God” (John 20:28). We should not isolate one sentence from the Gospel's wider witness.

“Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone” (Mark 10:18)

Jesus is responding to a man who calls him “Good Teacher.” He does not say that he is sinful or that he is not good. He presses the man to consider the meaning of his own words. If goodness in its fullest sense belongs to God, what does it mean to call Jesus good? Jesus' question exposes the shallowness of a title offered without a true understanding of the person receiving it.

These passages remind us that Jesus is not the Father and that his humanity is real. They do not overturn the evidence that he shares the divine identity. A sound reading allows all the passages to speak rather than making one text silence the others.

11. Did Christians invent his deity later?

Another common claim is that Jesus was originally regarded as only a human teacher and that later Christians gradually turned him into God. But the earliest Christian writings do not support such a simple story. Paul's letters, written within the first generation of the church, already speak of Jesus in remarkably exalted terms.

In 1 Corinthians 8:6, Paul adapts the language of Israel's confession of one God and speaks of one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist. This is not the language of a movement that has forgotten monotheism and casually added another god. Paul places Jesus within the confession of the one God while maintaining the distinction between Father and Son.

Philippians 2:6–11 describes Christ as existing in the form of God, humbling himself by taking the form of a servant, and becoming obedient to death on a cross. God then highly exalts him, and every knee bows and every tongue confesses that Jesus Christ is Lord. The passage echoes Isaiah 45:23, where every knee bows to the Lord. The earliest Christians were applying language of universal divine homage to Jesus.

Hebrews 1 says that the Son is the radiance of God's glory and the exact imprint of his nature, that he upholds the universe by his word, and that the Father addresses the Son in language drawn from the Psalms: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever” (Hebrews 1:3, 8). These writings belong to the New Testament period, not to a distant era after the church had supposedly invented Christ's deity.

The Council of Nicaea in AD 325 did not invent the belief that Jesus is divine. It addressed a controversy about how to confess the Son's relationship to the Father and used careful language to reject the idea that the Son was a created being. The council's wording was a response to dispute, not the beginning of Christian worship of Jesus. Our article on the early church and the Council of Nicaea explores that history further.

There were disagreements in the early centuries, and it would be inaccurate to claim that every Christian explained Christ's identity with perfect clarity from the beginning. But the worship of Jesus and the conviction that he shares the divine glory are present in the earliest Christian sources. The later councils clarified and defended what the church had received from the apostolic witness.

12. What does this mean for us?

When all the evidence is read together, the picture is difficult to reduce to the claim that Jesus was merely a good teacher who never made any extraordinary claims about himself. He speaks of an existence before Abraham. He claims unity with the Father. He forgives sins, gives life, exercises judgment, receives the same honour as the Father, and identifies himself with the heavenly Son of Man who receives everlasting dominion. His followers confess him as Lord and God, and the earliest Christian writings place him within the worship and identity of the one God.

We should not pretend that every verse is equally straightforward or that every objection can be answered in a sentence. The New Testament itself requires us to hold together truths that are easy to pull apart: Jesus is truly man, he is distinct from the Father, and he is truly God. The incarnation is not a contradiction to be explained away. It is the astonishing claim at the centre of the Christian faith.

There is a reason this question cannot remain merely academic. If Jesus is only a teacher, we may admire him, learn from him, and still leave his authority behind when it becomes inconvenient. But if he is the eternal Son who came to save sinners, then his words demand more than admiration. He has the right to command our lives, and he alone is able to reconcile us to God.

The apostles do not invite us to place Jesus among a list of impressive religious figures. They call us to trust him. The Son of God took flesh, obeyed where we have disobeyed, bore the judgment our sins deserve, and rose from the dead. The proper response is not simply to win an argument about his identity, but to repent, believe, worship, and follow him.

Jesus asked his disciples, “But who do you say that I am?” (Matthew 16:15). That question still reaches every reader. The evidence of Scripture gives us every reason to confess with Thomas: “My Lord and my God” (John 20:28).
